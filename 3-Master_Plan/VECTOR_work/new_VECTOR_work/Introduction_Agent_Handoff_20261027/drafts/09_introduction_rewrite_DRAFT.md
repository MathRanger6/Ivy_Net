**New paragraph A (v2, 11-03 13:20) — Alex's step-by-step argument. IN WORD (13:05 replaced the three-channel paragraph; 13:20 tracked edits: development on both sides per Charles, assignment named in the model sentence).**

A person's success depends on that person's own performance and on the environment in which the performance is produced, judged, and rewarded. A stronger environment can work in both directions. Strong peers and a strong organization can improve the performance itself, through example, coaching, and better training. They can also lend their members prestige, visibility, and credit, so that the same performance is noticed and valued more when it comes from a strong group. But performance is usually judged against that of others, and the positions it can win are scarce. Beside stronger peers, the same performance can look weaker, and more of those peers are credible candidates for the same positions. The same peers can also crowd a person out of the chances to play, lead, or practice through which performance improves. Because forces of both kinds can operate at once, the relationship between the strength of a person's environment and that person's chance of success need not take a single form. It may rise throughout, rise and then level off, or rise and then fall, and it may take different forms in different systems. The question is therefore not simply whether stronger peers help or hurt, but what determines the shape of that relationship, and in particular at what point a stronger environment begins to impose a disadvantage. This dissertation develops a model that relates the shape of that relationship to how people are assigned to groups, how performances are evaluated, and how scarce positions are allocated, and examines its predictions in three selection systems: the professional basketball draft, promotion in the Army, and academic tenure.

The model follows a person through three steps, and the environment can enter at each. The first is assignment, the step at which people are placed into groups, partly by their own choices and partly by the organization's. Assignment determines who a person's peers are. Because stronger individuals may choose, or be chosen into, stronger groups, it is also the main rival explanation for any association between the strength of a person's environment and that person's success. The second is evaluation, the step at which a performance is measured and compared. The environment can change it through the comparison group, the rater, or the label attached to the same work. The third is selection, the step at which scarce positions are allocated among those evaluated. The environment can change it through the number of positions and the number and strength of the others competing for them. Evaluation and selection are where a stronger environment helps or hurts a person whose performance is unchanged: it changes how that performance is seen and what it can win. Assignment is where the environment comes from. A separate chapter therefore models assignment as a network linking individuals to groups, in which each group has a fixed number of places and people tend to end up in groups whose members perform like them. Without that tendency, the model places people at random, which shows how much groups would differ by chance alone; as the tendency strengthens, groups differ more, and a person's own performance becomes tied to the strength of that person's peers. Development, the first of the forces above, is treated as background: it changes the performance that the three steps then act on, while the question here is a person's chance of success for a given performance.

**New paragraph B v1 (superseded by v2) (v1, 11-03 13:00) — evaluation and selection central; development background**

These forces act mainly through evaluation and selection, the two steps on which this dissertation concentrates. Evaluation is the step at which a performance is measured and compared, and the environment can change it through the comparison group, the rater, or the label attached to the same work. Selection is the step at which scarce positions are allocated among those evaluated, and the environment can change it through the number of positions and the number and strength of the others competing for them. Peers and the organization enter both steps. The environment can also change the performance itself, by developing a person's skills, habits, and output. That channel is real and widely studied, but here it is treated as background: it shapes the performance that evaluation and selection then act on, and the literatures below are read with evaluation and selection in the foreground.

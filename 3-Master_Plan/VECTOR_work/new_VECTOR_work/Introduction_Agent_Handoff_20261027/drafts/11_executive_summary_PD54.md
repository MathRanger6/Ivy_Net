A large literature studies talent, performance, and success. It divides into four bodies of work, and the division is the reason for the review that follows.

The largest body studies individuals and sets the group aside. Performance is what a person does; success is a community's recognition of that performance (Yucesoy and Barabási 2016; Wang, Gates, and Barabási 2025). The two come apart. Success is far more unequally distributed than the performances from which it is inferred, especially near the top, because recognition amplifies small differences (Merton 1968; Sinatra et al. 2016; Bol, de Vaan, and van de Rijt 2018; Fraiberger et al. 2018; Wang, Jones, and Wang 2019; Mariani et al. 2024). The intuition this work leaves standing is that more talent produces better performance and better performance produces more success, so that more talent is always better.

A second body studies teams, and the picture is complicated. Teams produce a growing share of the highest-impact work, prior success among teammates predicts later victory, and the structure of collaboration can raise a group's performance up to a point and then reverse (Wuchty, Jones, and Uzzi 2007; Mukherjee et al. 2019; Uzzi and Spiro 2005).

A smaller body asks how the mix of individuals affects the people around them. Placing an especially strong person among ordinary ones changes everyone else's performance, and the direction depends on the rewards: a dominant competitor can lower effort when rewards are relative, while a strong coworker can raise output when rewards are shared (Brown 2011; Bandiera, Barankay, and Rasul 2005; Mas and Moretti 2009; Hamilton, Nickerson, and Owan 2003). What these studies measure is performance. A growing body of work asks the sharpest question about the group itself: whether it can have too much talent, and whether concentrating high performers helps the group only up to a point (Swaab et al. 2014). Tests that can tell a downturn from a plateau find that the returns to talent diminish in some settings and hold steady in others. Every one of these studies measures the success of the group (Simonsohn 2018; Gula et al. 2021; Long, Graf, and Bilalić 2024; Kim et al. 2026).

Almost none of this work asks the remaining question, which is how the group affects the individual. The familiar form of that question is whether it is better to be a big fish in a small pond or the same fish in a larger one (Davis 1966). Education has asked it longest. Elsewhere it is barely asked. That is where this review engages.

Turned from the group to the person, the too-much-talent question is whether more talent among an individual's peers is always better for that individual. Three questions follow. To what extent does the quality of a person's peer group influence that person's success? If people of the same performance are placed among peers of different quality, does their success change even though the performance does not? And does the answer depend on how high-performing the person is? What varies is how a performance is recognized and whether it wins a scarce position.

The sections below are the fields that have touched some part of this path. The science of success separates performance from success and then treats strong peers mostly as a resource (Barabási 2018; Fortunato et al. 2018; Wang and Barabási 2021; Yucesoy and Barabási 2016; Wang et al. 2025; Mariani et al. 2024; Merton 1968; Menger 2024; Salganik et al. 2006; Van De Rijt et al. 2014; Bol et al. 2018; Sinatra et al. 2016; Janosov et al. 2020; Pluchino et al. 2018; Li et al. 2019; Ma et al. 2020; Sinatra et al. 2018; Wuchty et al. 2007; Mukherjee et al. 2019; Clauset et al. 2015; Way et al. 2019; Fraiberger et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2019; Rajkumar et al. 2022; Uzzi and Spiro 2005). Economics shows that when rewards depend on rank, a person's prospects depend on rivals, and that the answer changes from one setting to another (Lazear and Rosen 1981; Rosen 1981; Menger 2024; Kahn and Huberman 1988; Asch and Warner 2001; Benson et al. 2019; Brown 2011; Bandiera et al. 2005; Borjas and Doran 2015; Mas and Moretti 2009; Guryan et al. 2009; Arcidiacono et al. 2017; Hamilton et al. 2003; Azoulay et al. 2010; Waldinger 2012; Kuehn 2023; Kuehn and Rebessi 2023; Manski 1993; Bramoullé et al. 2009; Angrist 2014). Network science makes one person's outcome depend on others, and it rarely observes a selection with the other candidates measured (Breiger 1974; Lazer et al. 2020; McPherson et al. 2001; Newman 2003; Kossinets and Watts 2009; Feld 1991; Granovetter 1973; Burt 1992; Burt 2004; Rajkumar et al. 2022; Clauset et al. 2015; Gould 2002; Uzzi and Spiro 2005; Guimerà et al. 2005; Lazer and Friedman 2007; Mason and Watts 2012; Centola and Macy 2007; Christakis and Fowler 2007; Cohen-Cole and Fletcher 2008; Shalizi and Thomas 2011; Aral et al. 2009; Eckles et al. 2016; Centola 2010; Centola 2011; Gee et al. 2017). Education can separate the effect of a school from the sorting of students into it, and it uses admission to study what a school does to a student (Davis 1966; Marsh 1987; Marsh and Hau 2003; Fang et al. 2018; Murphy and Weinhardt 2020; Elsner and Isphording 2017; Elsner et al. 2021; Denning et al. 2023; Abdulkadiroğlu et al. 2014; Dobbie and Fryer 2014; Pop-Eleches and Urquiola 2013; Jackson 2010; Barabási 2018; Dale and Krueger 2002; Sacerdote 2001; Carrell et al. 2009; Lyle 2007; Feld and Zölitz 2017; Booij et al. 2016; Duflo et al. 2008; Carrell et al. 2013; Angrist 2014; Sacerdote 2014; Jackson and Bruegmann 2009; Hoxby 2000; Sönmez and Switzer 2013; Schulker 2010). Psychology supplies the comparisons through which people judge and are judged, and its too-much-talent studies stop at the group's result (Festinger 1954; Zell and Alicke 2010; Stouffer et al. 1949; Davis 1966; Tesser 1988; Eden and Shani 1982; Musch and Grondin 2001; Menger 2024; Grant and Schwartz 2011; Pierce and Aguinis 2013; Swaab et al. 2014; Simonsohn 2018; Gula et al. 2021; Long et al. 2024; Kim et al. 2026). Organizational studies treats promotion as the allocation of a fixed number of positions, and it counts competitors rather than measuring them (White 1970; Doeringer and Piore 1971; Stewman and Konda 1983; Menger 2024; Rosenbaum 1979; Staw and Hoang 1995; Groysberg et al. 2008; Huckman and Pisano 2006; Scullen et al. 2005; Podolny 1993; Kanter 1977; Bothner et al. 2007). The Army writes assignment, evaluation, and selection into regulation, and what is known about it comes from separate studies (Doeringer and Piore 1971; Wardynski et al. 2010; Asch and Warner 2001; Rostker et al. 1993; Lyle and Smith 2014; U.S. Army 2019; Department of the Army 2019; Evans and Robinson 2020; Evans and Bae 2019; Department of the Army 2020; Department of the Army 2019 (2); Spain et al. 2020; Spain et al. 2021; Johnson and Andersen 2010; Spain et al. 2023; Dabkowski et al. 2010). The fields belong in one review because each describes a piece of the path from a performance to a success, and none follows that path for a person whose own performance is held fixed while the peer group changes.

Answering the questions requires settings in which an individual's performance can be measured and held fixed, and in which the quality of the peer group can be varied.

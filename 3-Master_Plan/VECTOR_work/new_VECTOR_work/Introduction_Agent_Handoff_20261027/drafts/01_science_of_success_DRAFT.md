## The science of success

**The question and its unit.** The science of success asks why some individuals, works, and organizations receive recognition and rewards far beyond others, and it answers with large data sets on careers, citations, exhibitions, and markets (Fortunato et al. 2018; Barabási 2018; Wang and Barabási 2021). Its organizing distinction separates performance from success. Yucesoy and Barabási (2016) define performance as "an individual measure that captures the actions of a performer" and success as "a collective measure that captures a community's reactions to these actions." Wang, Gates, and Barabási (2025, 592) state the same principle: success "is a collective measure, representing a community's recognition of an individual's performance," and whereas performance is bounded, with small differences among top performers, success is heavy-tailed. Mariani et al. (2024, 2) define success more broadly, as "the attainment of highly-valued outcomes for an individual, team, or organization," and conclude that "differences in success do not necessarily reflect differences in performance or quality" (4). The field therefore already treats success as collective. That premise carries a consequence this dissertation takes literally: if others confer success, then the others who confer it, and the others who compete for it, belong in the explanation. Plotting advancement against the peer mean, and not only against one's own measured performance, is one way to examine that consequence.

**Recognition amplifies small differences.** The field's best-established mechanism is cumulative advantage. Merton (1968) described how eminent scientists receive disproportionate credit for contributions comparable to those of lesser-known colleagues, and his "Matthew effect" became the template for later models in which citations and visibility accrue in proportion to those already held (Wang, Gates, and Barabási 2025). Experiments have since shown that this feedback is causal and not merely a sign of hidden quality. In an artificial music market of 14,341 participants, stronger social influence increased both the inequality and the unpredictability of success, and quality only partly determined outcomes (Salganik, Dodds, and Watts 2006). Field experiments that gave early success to arbitrarily chosen recipients raised their later success rates in funding, status, endorsement, and reputation systems (van de Rijt et al. 2014). At an explicit selection cutoff, winners just above a funding threshold accumulated more than twice the subsequent funding of nonwinners with nearly identical review scores, partly because nonwinners stopped competing (Bol, de Vaan, and van de Rijt 2018). Chance enters the same accounts. A scientist's highest-impact paper is equally likely to appear anywhere in the sequence of that scientist's work (Sinatra et al. 2016), luck shapes career impact across film, music, books, and science (Janosov, Battiston, and Sinatra 2020), and in an agent-based model the most successful agents were rarely the most talented (Pluchino, Biondo, and Rapisarda 2018). These findings establish that recognition is not a transparent readout of performance. In the terms used throughout this chapter, they document the *evaluation* channel: the same performance can be measured, perceived, and rewarded differently.

**Peers and organizations enter as sources of advantage.** Where the field examines the environment, it finds mostly benefits. Among peers, junior researchers who coauthor with highly cited scientists keep a lasting advantage over matched researchers with similar early records, and the benefit is largest for those at less prestigious institutions (Li et al. 2019). In genealogical data on nearly 40,000 scientists, mentorship strongly predicts protégé success, including the protégé's chance of winning prizes (Ma, Mukherjee, and Uzzi 2020). Scientists seldom appear as senior authors in a top journal before publishing there under a senior colleague, a pattern called the chaperone effect (Sekara et al. 2018). Teams increasingly produce the highest-impact work (Wuchty, Jones, and Uzzi 2007), and teammates' prior shared success predicts victory in team competitions (Mukherjee et al. 2019). Among organizations, doctoral prestige predicts faculty placement better than published rankings do, and the faculty hiring network is steeply hierarchical (Clauset, Arbesman, and Larremore 2015). In a matched-pair study of 2,453 early-career faculty in all 205 PhD-granting computer science departments in the United States and Canada, "faculty's work environments, not selection effects, drive their productivity and prominence" (Way et al. 2019), which places the organization in the *development* channel. In art, early exhibition at central institutions gave artists lifelong access to prestigious venues and lowered dropout, whereas starting at the periphery predicted exit (Fraiberger et al. 2018), a "prestige lock-in effect" (Wang, Gates, and Barabási 2025, 598) that joins evaluation to *selection*. In each case, a stronger environment raises the individual's prospects.

**How the field separates effects from sorting.** The strongest studies in this literature rely on three designs: matched pairs (Li et al. 2019; Way et al. 2019), comparisons just above and below funding thresholds (Bol, de Vaan, and van de Rijt 2018; Wang, Jones, and Wang 2019), and randomized experiments (Salganik, Dodds, and Watts 2006; van de Rijt et al. 2014; Rajkumar et al. 2022). Much of the remaining evidence is predictive. The Markov model of artists' careers and the models that predict book sales from the publisher's imprint show that prestige forecasts success without showing that it causes success (Wang, Gates, and Barabási 2025). Mariani et al. (2024, 11) accordingly call for experiments that "could not only improve the causative interpretations" of the field's findings. Sorting, in which strong individuals choose or are chosen into strong environments, is the main rival explanation whenever environment and success move together, and Way et al. (2019) is the clearest attempt to rule it out.

**Opposing forces are documented, though not for the strength of peers.** The field already recognizes environmental effects that change sign. Junior scientists whose National Institutes of Health proposals fell just below the funding threshold were more likely to leave the system, yet those who stayed outperformed narrow winners over the long run (Wang, Jones, and Wang 2019): one selection event carried opposing consequences. Network structure can also help up to a point and then hurt. The small-world structure of the network of Broadway's creative artists raised the financial and artistic performance of their musicals only up to a threshold, after which the effect reversed (Uzzi and Spiro 2005). In randomized experiments on LinkedIn, moderately weak ties produced the most job transmission (Rajkumar et al. 2022). Mariani et al. (2024) review all three findings. A non-monotonic environmental effect is therefore not foreign to the field, and a claim that the relationship between environment and success can change direction would not be new in itself.

**What the field leaves open.** Neither of the two recent reviews addresses how the strength of the people with whom an individual competes changes that individual's chance of being selected (Mariani et al. 2024; Wang, Gates, and Barabási 2025). In the studies reviewed here, strong peers and prestigious organizations appear as sources of resources, reputation, and access, and selection thresholds appear as fixed cutoffs on a ranking that serve to identify the effect of winning. The composition of the pool in which a person is ranked, and the number of slots that pool must share, are not the object of study. Yet if success is a community's recognition of performance, recognition is allocated within particular pools: a team's roster, a department's tenure lines, a senior rater's profile, a promotion board's quota. In such a pool, a strong environment can raise what a person becomes while lowering where that person stands among those competing for the same slots. The science of success supplies the distinction between performance and success, the evidence that recognition amplifies small differences, and the evidence that environments confer advantage. It leaves open how development and local competition combine when the environment is also the pool in which selection takes place.

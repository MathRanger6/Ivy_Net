## Education: the big fish, the small pond, and the same fish in a bigger pond

**The question and its unit.** Is a student better off as a big fish in a small pond or as the same fish in a big pond? Education research has posed this question longer and more directly than any other field, and it is the question of this dissertation in its simplest form: hold the individual fixed, change the environment, and ask what changes. Davis (1966, 20–21) stated it from a national survey of college seniors: "Among high-school graduates with a given level of scholastic aptitude, the more selective the college they attend, the lower their grades will be. The diversity of American colleges and universities means that students in the middle ranges of academic aptitude can choose to be big frogs in little ponds or little frogs in big ponds." The unit of analysis in education is the student within a classroom, a school, or a cohort, and the outcomes are achievement, beliefs about one's own ability, choices of subject or career, and educational attainment. Schools are also organizations that perform all three functions this dissertation separates. They *develop* students by teaching them, they *evaluate* students by grading them, often against their classmates, and they *select* students by admitting some and not others. Education therefore supplies the clearest evidence that the same individual can fare differently in different environments, as well as the most developed methods for separating the effect of an environment from the sorting of individuals into it.

**The pond changes how the fish is graded and how it judges itself.** Davis framed his result through the theory of relative deprivation, borrowing the wartime finding that soldiers judged their chances of promotion against the soldiers around them. He found that college grades, and not the academic caliber of the college, predicted whether men chose demanding careers: in his partial associations, "the coefficient for GPA (+.311) is twice as large as that for school quality (+.151)" (23). Students, he argued, judge their ability against the classmates they can see, and grades become "the accepted yardstick" (25). His evidence was limited in ways he acknowledged: grades were self-reported, freshman career plans were recalled at graduation, and no measure of aptitude covered the full sample. Marsh (1987) gave the pattern its modern name, the big-fish-little-pond effect, and showed in a representative sample of 2,213 boys from 87 American high schools that equally able students reported lower academic self-concept in schools of higher average ability. He identified two routes: students compare themselves with abler classmates, and teachers grade on a local curve, so that the same ability earns lower grades in a stronger school. Later work extended the result across settings. In nationally representative samples of 15-year-olds from 26 countries (N = 103,558), the effect of school-average achievement on self-concept was negative in every country, with a mean standardized coefficient of −.20 (Marsh and Hau 2003). A meta-analysis of 33 studies with more than 1.2 million students estimated the effect at β = −0.28, although the between-study heterogeneity was extreme, with I² = 99.78 percent (Fang et al. 2018, 5). The evidence that a stronger pond lowers how a student is graded and how the student rates herself is therefore broad, but it rests largely on self-reports and on covariate adjustment rather than on assigned environments.

**Rank, not ability alone, carries forward.** A second body of work replaced self-concept with a sharper quantity, the student's ordinal rank among classmates, and asked whether rank has consequences after the student has left the pond. Murphy and Weinhardt (2020) used administrative data on more than 2.25 million English students whose primary-school tests were marked externally and not curved. Students with identical scores but higher rank in their primary school later scored higher on national tests, reported more confidence, and were more likely to choose the subject at age 18, even though they then attended secondary schools where most peers were new and teachers did not know their earlier rank. Elsner and Isphording (2017) exploited the near-random composition of adjacent cohorts within American high schools and found that higher rank raised the probability of finishing high school and attending college, operating partly through students' expectations and their perceived intelligence. Elsner, Isphording, and Zölitz (2021) used repeated random assignment of students to teaching sections in a Dutch business school, where exams were centrally graded, and found that a one-standard-deviation increase in rank raised the probability of graduating in a related major by 2.2 percentage points; improvements in rank raised performance, whereas declines had no effect. They conclude that students "appear to rely on their rank as a heuristic, thereby placing considerable weight on noisy information" (3204). Denning, Murphy, and Weinhardt (2023) followed Texas students from third grade into the labor market and found that moving from the 25th to the 75th percentile of rank in one's class raised later wages by about $1,500 a year, roughly 7 percent (1440). This literature makes the frog-pond question precise. The same student, with the same measured achievement, faces different later outcomes depending on where she stood among the particular people around her.

**Winning a seat in the bigger pond.** If rank matters, gaining admission to a stronger school should carry a cost as well as a benefit, and the evidence on selective schools is consistent with that possibility without settling it. Regression discontinuity designs compare applicants just above and just below an admission cutoff, who are nearly identical except for the school they attend. At the exam schools of Boston and New York, admission sharply raised the achievement of a student's peers, yet "the marked changes in peer characteristics at exam school admissions cutoffs have little causal effect on test scores or college quality" (Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist, and Pathak 2014, 137); a separate study of New York's three most selective schools likewise found little effect on college enrollment, graduation, or quality (Dobbie and Fryer 2014). In Romania, where a national mechanism assigns students to high schools at about 2,000 cutoffs, admission to the better school raised performance on the high-stakes graduation exam by only 0.02 to 0.10 standard deviations (Pop-Eleches and Urquiola 2013, 1291). The same study documented responses by the people around the student: students who just made the cutoff "on average perceive themselves as weaker relative to their peers" and reported more negative interactions with them, parents reduced their help, and "more qualified teachers are matched with higher achieving students" within schools (1291). In Trinidad and Tobago, by contrast, attending a school with stronger peers had large positive effects, and a student who missed her first-choice school was 9 to 12 percentage points less likely to obtain the qualifications required for tertiary education (Jackson 2010, 1419). The rank literature offers lower rank as one reason that admission to elite schools so often yields little. Denning, Murphy, and Weinhardt (2023, 1428) estimate that if parents chose schools solely on mean peer achievement, rank effects would erase 39 percent of the potential gains for a median student. None of the cutoff studies, however, tests this explanation directly.

**Peers in the classroom help some students and hurt others.** A separate tradition estimates the effects of classmates and roommates by exploiting random or quasi-random assignment. At Dartmouth, where freshman roommates were randomly assigned, a one-standard-deviation increase in a roommate's grade point average was associated with a .05 increase in one's own, and roommates influenced the decision to join a fraternity (Sacerdote 2001, 694). At the U.S. Air Force Academy, where cadets are randomly assigned to squadrons of about 33 freshmen, a one-standard-deviation increase in peers' verbal aptitude scores raised freshman grades by .05 points, an effect largest in mathematics and science (Carrell, Fullerton, and West 2009, 455). At West Point, by contrast, academic peer effects nearly vanished once shocks common to a company were taken into account, although peers and upperclassmen influenced the choice of academic major and the decision to remain in the Army (Lyle 2007). Averages conceal opposite responses. In randomly assigned sections at Maastricht University, a one-standard-deviation increase in peer grade point average raised grades by 1.26 percent of a standard deviation, but this result "masks important heterogeneity: low-achieving students are actually harmed by high-achieving peers" (Feld and Zölitz 2017, 389). In a randomized experiment at the University of Amsterdam that varied the composition of tutorial groups, low-ability students benefited from abler peers only in groups of mixed ability, and grouping students by ability reduced dropout among low- and medium-ability students by 12 percentage points (Booij, Leuven, and Oosterbeek 2017, 547). In Kenya, random assignment of schools to ability tracking raised test scores for students at every level, a result the authors explain through teachers: because teachers' rewards depended on a later exit exam that weaker students were unlikely to sit, teachers directed instruction toward the top of the class, and separating students changed whom they taught (Duflo, Dupas, and Kremer 2008). In each of these designs the organization assembles the group, and the effect of the group differs across the students within it.

**When the organization redesigned the pond, the effects reversed.** The most instructive study in this literature is a policy experiment. Using peer effects estimated from randomly assigned squadrons, the Air Force Academy, whose leadership sought to reduce academic probation, assigned half of two entering classes to squadrons designed to raise the grades of its weakest students. The estimates "predicted a Pareto-improving allocation in which grades of students in the bottom third of the academic distribution would rise, on average, 0.053 grade points while students with higher predicted achievement would be unaffected"; instead, the lowest-ability students lost 0.061 grade points and middle-ability students gained 0.082 (Carrell, Sacerdote, and West 2013, 856). Surveys suggested that students within the designed squadrons sorted into more homogeneous study groups and avoided the peers with whom the design meant them to interact. The authors concluded that "social processes are so rich and complex that one needs a deep understanding of their formation before one can formulate 'optimal policy'" (881), and they could not identify a method that would turn such estimates into reliable predictions (880). Booij, Leuven, and Oosterbeek (2017, 568) reproduced the Academy's result by simulating its design with their own estimates. Angrist (2014) read the same result differently, as evidence that the original estimates were spurious, and argued more generally that regressions of individual outcomes on group averages are close to mechanical. In a review of the experimental literature, Sacerdote (2014) drew three conclusions: estimated peer effects are "highly context specific"; the linear-in-means model "is often not a good description of the world, although we do not yet have an agreed-upon model to replace it"; and the field cannot yet use such estimates reliably to design policy.

**Teachers are workers in a pond as well.** The same questions arise for the employees of schools, which links education to the study of organizations. Using data on North Carolina elementary schools, Jackson and Bruegmann (2009) found that a teacher's students gained more in mathematics and reading when the teacher's colleagues in the same grade were more effective, with the largest spillovers for less experienced teachers. They drew two conclusions that bear on evaluation. First, measures of an individual teacher's effectiveness absorb part of the colleagues' contribution, so the standard assumption of no spillovers across teachers "is not valid." Second, "compensation schemes that reward a teacher's performance relative to her peers may be detrimental to fostering peer learning" (23). The environment thus enters both what a worker becomes and how that worker's performance is measured.

**How education separates sorting from effects.** Education has built the widest set of designs for this purpose. Researchers have used random assignment of roommates, squadrons, sections, and tutorial groups; regression discontinuity at admission cutoffs; idiosyncratic differences between adjacent cohorts within a school (Hoxby 2000; Elsner and Isphording 2017); and variation in test-score distributions across classes, which separates rank from achievement (Murphy and Weinhardt 2020). The field has also learned the limits of these designs. Angrist (2014) shows that measurement error and weak instruments can produce apparent peer effects without any social interaction, and Denning, Murphy, and Weinhardt (2023, 1428) note that "random classroom allocation is not sufficient for the identification of rank effects," because rank and peer composition move together. These methods are the standard against which any descriptive pattern relating outcomes to peers, including the one this dissertation examines, must be read.

**What education leaves open.** The studies reviewed here leave the question of this dissertation open for specific and documented reasons. First, the outcomes are almost always development or the student's own choices, such as self-concept, grades, test scores, choice of major, or enrollment, and not a selection decision made by an organization among competitors. Elsner and Isphording (2017, 789–790) raise the possibility that colleges admit students on their rank within a high-school cohort, as automatic admission of the top 10 percent does, but because they observe no admission decisions they cannot test it. Carrell, Fullerton, and West (2009, 446) note that "there are no explicit grading curves at USAFA. Therefore, a benefit to one student or group is not, mechanically, at the expense of another student or group"; the setting excludes by design the competition for a fixed number of places that defines selection. Second, each study observes one system: a country, a state, a city, an academy, or a single business school. Estimates conflict across systems, from no effect of elite admission in Boston and New York to small effects in Romania and large ones in Trinidad and Tobago, and no framework predicts which result a given system will produce. Third, there is no fitted model of the mechanism. Davis's mechanism was verbal; Denning, Murphy, and Weinhardt (2023, 1427) are "agnostic about what is driving the effects"; Murphy and Weinhardt (2020) derive one implication from a model but do not estimate it; and Duflo, Dupas, and Kremer (2008) test their model of teacher targeting by rejecting special cases rather than by fitting it. The Air Force Academy experiment showed the cost of this gap, and Sacerdote (2014) states that no model has yet replaced the linear-in-means framework. Fourth, the early and widely cited evidence uses imperfect measures, notably self-reported grades in both Davis (1966) and Marsh (1987). Fifth, the two forces that education documents, the benefit of stronger peers and the penalty of lower rank among them, are mostly estimated in separate studies; Denning, Murphy, and Weinhardt (2023) are among the few to weigh them against each other, and they do so for wages rather than for selection. The opening these limitations leave is a model in which the same group serves both as an environment that develops a person and as a pool in which that person is ranked and selected, fitted to data in which the selection decision is observed, and tested in more than one system. Education poses the question of the big fish and the small pond most clearly; the chapters that follow ask whether the answer generalizes to organizations that select their members for scarce positions.

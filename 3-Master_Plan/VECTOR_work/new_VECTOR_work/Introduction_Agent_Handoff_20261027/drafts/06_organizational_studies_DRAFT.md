## Organizational studies: opportunity structures, embedded performance, and the rules of selection

**The question and its unit.** Organizational sociology and management research ask two questions that bear directly on this dissertation: how the structure of an organization determines who advances, and how much of what looks like individual performance belongs to the organization in which it is produced. The unit is the career, or the worker, inside a bounded system of jobs: a corporation, a church, a police force, a hospital, an investment bank, a professional league. The evidence comes mostly from personnel records and from settings in which performance is publicly ranked. Where economics treats promotion mainly as an incentive device, organizational research treats it as an allocation of a fixed number of positions, governed by rules and by the flow of vacancies, among people whose measured performance is partly a product of where they work. That framing touches all three channels this dissertation distinguishes. Structure governs selection; the organization and its members shape development; and status, prior commitments, and ranking rules shape evaluation.

**Opportunity is created by vacancies, not by people.** White (1970, 1) opened *Chains of Opportunity* with the observation that "conflicts among individuals over opportunities are central to many theories in social science," while "less often noted is the regenerative nature of much opportunity." In a system of fixed jobs, a death, retirement, or new position opens a vacancy that moves down the hierarchy as successive incumbents move up to fill it. White modeled this movement as a Markov chain on vacancies among strata of jobs and fit it to the official registers of three American Protestant clergies over about fifty years. His conclusion inverted the usual account of careers: "Men's careers are by-products, not generators, of mobility in the vacancy model" (249). The model fit aggregate flows without information on individuals, and when White added seniority he found little gain: "Random selection of men seems a reasonable first approximation" (281). That result, however, rests on an assumption rather than a test of how candidates are chosen. White stated that "the simplest assumption, random selection, is made about allocation of calls to men within a stratum, partly because no mechanism for distinguishing between men has been built into the vacancy model thus far" (249), and that "vacancy chain models are blind to particular ties among men" (298). Doeringer and Piore (1971) supplied the institutional description that such models leave out. In an internal labor market, outsiders enter only at designated ports of entry; higher jobs are filled by promotion from within and are thereby shielded from outside competition; rules for posting vacancies define the pool of applicants for each opening; and rules of priority rank those applicants by some mix of seniority and ability. Taken together, the two accounts separate three elements that later research often merges: the number of slots that open, the pool eligible to compete for them, and the rule that ranks the pool.

**Organizational demography sets the odds of promotion at a fixed level of merit.** Stewman and Konda (1983) turned White's vacancy logic into probabilities of promotion for individuals. In their model, the chance that a person is promoted depends on the number of vacancies, on a managerial preference over attributes of candidates, and on "the ratio of that person and his competitors to the number of job vacancies" (642). Competitors thus enter the model directly, as a denominator. From grade ratios and vacancy chains in four organizations (a private firm, a state police force, a military hierarchy, and the senior federal civil service), they showed that "a pyramidal grade structure does not imply declining career chances as one proceeds up the hierarchy" (650), and that a shift from 3 percent growth to none implies "a drop of 20% or more in promotion chances" (655). Within the police force, cohort size mattered. In principle, for two cohorts facing the same sequence of vacancies, "the smaller cohort will have more chances of promotion at each seniority level and will therefore move earlier" (671). In the data the relation was not monotone: small cohorts reached the first promotion most often and medium-sized cohorts least often (673). Members of slower cohorts also reached the top in nearly the same proportion ("the relative turtles eventually also get there," 675). The authors then drew the inference most relevant to this dissertation. Part of what is read as individual excellence is position: the "brightness" attributed to a rising star "is therefore not a product of the individual's genius alone but is, in part, and perhaps in large part, a reflection of the underlying social process or social structure" (672). Menger (2024) gives that inference its theory of talent. Comparative rankings, he argues, "do not merely reveal unequally distributed qualities ... and then select individuals on that basis. These competitions cause divergences in contenders' career paths, even though their abilities may have been rather similar" (20). A slight early gap among competitors then "comes to appear as a significant, intrinsic difference in quality" (22). The inference joins the selection channel to the evaluation channel, but Stewman and Konda do not test it. The only attribute in the managerial preference is seniority, and the individual-level results come from a single police force.

**Selection is sequential, and early outcomes condition later ones.** Rosenbaum (1979) followed a cohort of 671 employees who entered one large corporation in 1960–1962 and stayed through 1975. He proposed a tournament model in which careers are "a sequence of competitions, each of which has implications for an individual's mobility chances in all subsequent selections" (222–223). Employees promoted in the first three years reached lower management far more often than those promoted later ("81 percent versus 33 percent," 233), and none of the 128 employees who became foremen after the first interval reached middle management within thirteen years. Those not promoted early, he concluded, "are no longer competing in the same system with employees who were promoted in the first period" (234), and "an employee's career is not a series of equal contests" (238). Rosenbaum was explicit that the evidence is associational ("the aim is descriptive analysis, not causal inference," 225), and he noted that the cause of early selection may lie in prior education, initial placement, or the quality of the entering cohort (238, n. 16). Staw and Hoang (1995) found a related pattern in a different setting, in which a past selection decision biases later ones. Among players taken in the first two rounds of the 1980–1986 professional basketball drafts, "teams granted more playing time to their most highly drafted players and retained them longer, even after controlling for players' on-court performance, injuries, trade status, and position played" (474). A first-round pick stayed in the league about 3.3 years longer than a second-round pick (484), although draft position predicted performance only in the second and third seasons and "is not a significant predictor over longer periods of time" (488). The score by which an organization selects therefore includes more than current performance. It also carries the organization's earlier choices.

**Individual performance is partly a property of the organization.** If selection relies on measured performance, it matters whether that performance belongs to the person. Groysberg, Lee, and Nanda (2008) followed star security analysts ranked by *Institutional Investor* between 1988 and 1996. Analysts who changed firms declined in rank immediately and remained below comparable analysts who stayed for at least five years. For a ranked analyst who did not move, the predicted probability of a first-place ranking the next year was 0.106; for one who moved, it was 0.056 (1222). Analysts who moved with teammates, or to firms with stronger research capabilities, showed no significant decline (1224). The authors concluded that "the performance of an outstanding worker is not owned by the worker alone; it is a property of the worker/firm combination" (1226). Huckman and Pisano (2006) reached the same conclusion with a sharper design. Because many Pennsylvania cardiac surgeons operated at more than one hospital in the same quarter, the authors could "examine a given worker across several firms at a single time" (474). Measured by patient mortality, a surgeon's performance at a hospital improves with the surgeon's recent volume at that hospital but "does not significantly improve with increases in his or her volume at other hospitals," so that "surgeon performance is not fully portable across hospitals" (473). These studies place the organization and its members in the development channel. They also bear on evaluation, because a rating that treats performance as a trait of the person records the contribution of the person's surroundings as well.

**Evaluation is relative, and position shapes how performance is read.** Organizations often rank people against those around them, and the composition of that reference group then affects each person's outcome even if it has no effect on ability. The ranking Groysberg, Lee, and Nanda studied is a poll of clients that also "constitutes a core component of the internal performance evaluation for security analysts at every investment bank" (1219). Scullen, Bergey, and Aiman-Smith (2005) simulated a forced distribution system in which the lowest-rated workers in each group of ten are dismissed each year. They noted that ranking within groups means "workers who are fired from the better groups would have been retained if they had worked in some of the poorer groups" (4), the same objection critics raise when they say such systems "penalize good employees who happen to work on good teams and reward mediocre employees who are members of poor teams" (2). In their simulation, workforce potential rose by an average of 0.44 standard deviations over thirty years (11), with most of the gain in the first few years and none "after Year 20" (20). Status adds a second source of distortion. Podolny (1993, 831) treated a producer's status as "a signal of the underlying quality of a firm's products" when quality cannot be observed directly. In investment-grade debt underwriting, higher-status banks priced offerings below lower-status ones after controlling for recent volume, and a lower-status bank "cannot simply buy its way into a high-status niche" (863). Podolny concluded that "status is, therefore, not simply an epiphenomenal reflection of quality" (867). The Matthew effect he invokes, in which higher-status actors "obtain greater recognition and rewards for performing a given task and lower-status actors receive correspondingly less" (841–842), is an effect on evaluation that arises from position rather than performance.

**The composition of the peer group changes how individuals are seen.** Kanter (1977, 965) argued that "proportions, that is, relative numbers of socially and culturally different people in a group, are seen as critical in shaping interaction dynamics." She studied the sales force of a large industrial corporation, where about twenty women had entered a force of more than 300 men (970). In groups this skewed, the women drew attention as members of a category, so that "every act tended to be evaluated beyond its meaning for the organization" (973). "No token in the study had to work hard to have her presence noticed, but she did have to work hard to have her achievements noticed" (973). Kanter concluded that where informal interaction is central to success, "tokens are not very likely to do well compared with members of the majority category, at least while in the token position" (987). Her dimension of composition is social category rather than ability. The argument nonetheless has the form this dissertation examines: the same performance is read differently depending on who else is in the group. Her evidence consists of illustrations from one field study, and she closed by noting that "quantitative analyses are called for in order to provide precise documentation of the points at which interaction shifts" (986).

**Under relative ranking, nearby competitors change behavior.** Rank-based allocation makes a person's prospects depend on the specific rivals near that person's position. Bothner, Kang, and Stuart (2007) studied this directly in a season-long racing tournament, 1990–2003, in which drivers are ranked by accumulated points. They counted, before each race, the lower-ranked rivals who could pass a driver in that race, and found that with each standard deviation increase in that count "the odds of crashing go up by 19 percent" (227), while rivals within reach above the driver had no significant effect. The effect held with fixed effects for drivers and for races. Their summary states a point that applies well beyond racing: "the conduct of actors in a tournament is shaped not only by their relative positions but also by the level of crowding around those positions" (233). They proposed the race for partnership in law firms as a further test: when more rivals are poised to pass an associate, "his or her job security and likelihood of achieving partnership have declined" (235).

**How the field separates sorting from effects.** Most of this literature is descriptive or relies on controls rather than designs that isolate a cause. Rosenbaum disclaimed causal inference. Stewman and Konda argued that research which ignores structure misreads its own results: "individual variation is treated at length, but the underlying structures are not identified" (680). Podolny measured status before the period in which outcomes were observed and controlled for recent volume, but he had no direct measure of quality. The strongest designs come from management research on performance. Huckman and Pisano compared the same surgeon across hospitals within a quarter, which removes fixed traits of the surgeon. Bothner, Kang, and Stuart used fixed effects for drivers and for races and controlled for the ability and accident rates of neighboring rivals. Groysberg, Lee, and Nanda modeled the decision to move, though the correction appears in a supplement rather than in the article. None of these designs tests whether the advantage an organization confers changes who is selected for a scarce position.

**What organizational studies leaves open.** Organizational research supplies the clearest account of how structure allocates opportunity and the strongest evidence that performance is embedded in organizations. The studies reviewed here nonetheless leave this dissertation's question open, for five specific reasons. First, the formal models of opportunity either assume the selection step or reduce it to a single attribute. White assumed random selection within strata because "no mechanism for distinguishing between men has been built into the vacancy model" (1970, 249). Stewman and Konda's managerial preference uses seniority alone. Rosenbaum and Doeringer and Piore describe rules of selection without estimating them. Second, competitors enter these studies as counts rather than as people of measured quality. Stewman and Konda's denominator is the number of competitors, and Bothner, Kang, and Stuart count the rivals within reach. Groysberg, Lee, and Nanda record only whether any colleague moved with an analyst. Kanter treats the composition of the group as the variable, but by social category rather than ability, and she called for the quantitative analysis of thresholds without supplying it (1977, 986). None measures how strong a person's rivals are, or how that strength changes the person's chance of selection. Third, each study examines one system: one corporation, one sales force, three clergies, one police force for the individual-level results, one securities market, one population of analysts, the surgeons of one state, one league, and one racing series. Authors repeatedly flag this, from Rosenbaum's "single organization" (1979, 238) to Bothner, Kang, and Stuart's "idiosyncratic context" (2007, 234). Fourth, the outcome is usually not selection. Groysberg, Lee, and Nanda measure rank in a poll, Huckman and Pisano patient mortality, Podolny price, Bothner, Kang, and Stuart crashes, and Scullen, Bergey, and Aiman-Smith the average potential of a simulated workforce. Only Rosenbaum, Stewman and Konda, and Staw and Hoang observe who is allocated a scarce position, and none of the three measures the quality of the competitors. Fifth, the only formal model that both scores individuals and selects among them is not fit to data. Scullen, Bergey, and Aiman-Smith drew their parameters from published averages and called their simulation a baseline that "holds the many complicating factors constant" (2005, 26), and the formal model in Bothner, Kang, and Stuart uses illustrative parameter values. The opening these limitations leave is a model in which the organization and peers shape each person's score, and a fixed number of slots is then allocated among competitors whose quality is measured, fit to data from more than one organization.

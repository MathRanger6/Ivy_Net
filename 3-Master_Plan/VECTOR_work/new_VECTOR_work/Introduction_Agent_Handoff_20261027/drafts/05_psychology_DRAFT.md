## Psychology: social comparison, expectations, and the shape of the curve

**The question and its unit.** Psychology asks how people evaluate their own abilities and the abilities of others, and how those evaluations change what people do and how well they perform. Its unit is the individual within a comparison set: a pair of friends, a small laboratory group, a training class, or a team. The field matters here because the education findings reviewed above rest on a psychological claim, that students judge themselves against the classmates they can see. Festinger (1954) stated the foundation of that claim. "To the extent that objective, non-social means are not available, people evaluate their opinions and abilities by comparison respectively with the opinions and abilities of others" (118), and "the tendency to compare oneself with some other specific person decreases as the difference between his opinion or ability and one's own increases" (120). Psychology therefore supplies the mechanisms by which peers enter *evaluation*, both self-evaluation and the evaluations made by others, and through evaluation enter *development*. As the paragraphs below show, it has said much less about *selection*.

**The comparison set is local, and it is the set of competitors.** Festinger drew a consequence from his hypotheses that bears directly on competition for scarce positions. Because people are pushed to do slightly better than those with whom they compare themselves, and "not everyone in a group can be slightly better than everyone else," he concluded that "with respect to the evaluation of abilities, a state of social quiescence is never reached" (125). He cited an experiment in which, when one of three participants scored well above the other two, "these two can and do act so as to prevent the high scorer from getting additional points" (127). Later experiments established how local the relevant comparison is. Zell and Alicke (2010, 368) define the local dominance effect as "the tendency for comparisons with a few, discrete individuals to have a greater influence on self-assessments than comparisons with larger aggregates." In one study, participants evaluated themselves more favorably when they ranked best in a small group but at the 32nd percentile of their school than when they ranked worst in the group but at the 84th percentile (374). In another, about 80 percent of participants said they preferred the general, more diagnostic feedback, yet they relied on the local comparison when it was available (374). The authors extend the argument to careers: "When it comes to such ordinary goals as making a Little League team, advancing in a company, or succeeding in courtship, local comparisons are the coin of the realm" (377). The people who shape an individual's self-evaluation are therefore the few nearby others with whom that individual competes.

**The same chance of promotion feels different depending on what one's peers received.** The first large-scale evidence of this pattern came from the Army. In World War II surveys of enlisted men, Stouffer and colleagues (1949) found that soldiers in the Military Police, a branch with among the poorest promotion chances, rated the promotion prospects of "a soldier with ability" more favorably than did soldiers in the Air Corps, where promotion was common. Among men with one to two years of service, "only 24 per cent of MP's were noncoms as compared with 47 per cent of the Air Corps men" (251). The authors explained the paradox through the theory of relative deprivation: such opinions "represent a relationship between their expectations and their achievements relative to others in the same boat with them" (251). A man who failed to earn a rating in the Military Police shared that outcome with most of his peers, whereas a man who failed in the Air Corps had watched the majority succeed. The organization set the number of available grades through its Table of Organization (253), and peers set the standard against which each man judged the outcome. The authors were careful about what their data could show: whether a change in promotion policy would raise satisfaction "could be determined only from controlled experiments, which were never made" (257). Davis (1966) later carried the same reasoning from soldiers to college students, as the education section describes.

**Peers act on one another's performance.** Comparison does not only change how people feel; it changes how they treat the people they compare themselves with. Tesser's (1988) self-evaluation maintenance model holds that a close other's success can raise one's self-evaluation through reflection, as when one takes pride in a friend's achievement, or lower it through comparison, and that comparison dominates when the performance is on a dimension central to one's own identity. In an experiment in which participants could give a partner easy or difficult clues in a word game, the prediction held: "When relevance was low the friend was helped more than the stranger in 10 of the 13 sessions. When relevance was high, the stranger was helped more than the friend in 10 of the 13 sessions" (185). Tesser illustrated the model with a selection event, two friends trying out for a school band in which only one is chosen, and listed the ways the friend who was not chosen might protect herself, including undermining the other's next performance (183). The illustration was hypothetical, however, and the central quantity of the model was never measured; Tesser describes self-evaluation as "a hypothetical construct" (208). Peers, in this account, are not a passive background. They can raise or lower the measured performance of a rival, which is the input on which selection operates.

**What the organization expects shapes what people become.** Expectations held by superiors have effects of their own. Eden and Shani (1982) told the instructors of a 15-week combat command course in the Israel Defense Forces that some trainees had high "command potential." The labels were assigned at random, within blocks of equal aptitude, before instructors met the trainees. Achievement was scored by a commander who did not know of the experiment, and the label "explained 73% of the variance in performance" (194). The authors argued that the effect may be "self-perpetuating, as well as a self-fulfilling prophecy," because the favored trainees would begin the next course with more enthusiasm (197–198), and that "viewed as a scarce resource, leadership may be suboptimally allocated due to interpersonal expectancy effects" (198). The design also shows what such studies leave aside. For ethical reasons, the base commander agreed that "grades and instructor evaluations in this course would not determine these trainees' admission to the next course for which they were slated" (197). The one experiment that tested expectancy effects in a military setting therefore had to switch off the selection consequence of the evaluation it manipulated. With four instructors, it also rested on very few independent sources of expectation.

**When the organization fixes the comparison pool, selection follows.** The clearest psychological evidence that the comparison pool affects selection comes from sport. In youth programs that group children by birth year, "children born shortly before the cutoff date for age grouping in youth sport programs suffer from being promoted to higher age groups earlier than their later-born peers," and this disadvantage appears in skewed birthdates among players up to the professional level (Musch and Grondin 2001, 147). Natural experiments identify the cause: after Australia moved its soccer cutoff date in 1988, the birthdate distribution of professional players shifted in step, "strong evidence that the cutoff date is indeed the causal factor" (154). The proposed mechanism is selection among competitors for a fixed number of places. The authors reason that with 15 places and 15 interested children no effect should appear, whereas with 150 applicants "there will be strong competition to obtain a place," so that "the larger the pool of potential players for a given sport in a given category, the stronger the resulting RAE should be" (154). Selection then compounds. Relatively older children "are more likely to be chosen for select teams and high ability groups," which brings "better coaching and facing better opponents" (159), and those who benefit early "are likely to be erroneously perceived as the most talented in their age group" (158). That conversion of a ranking into a quality of the person is what Menger (2024, 14) describes as talent being "retro-dicted": it is attributed after the fact from relative standing. In this literature an organizational rule defines the pool, the evaluation made within the pool decides who is selected, and selection feeds later development. The authors acknowledge, however, that no study provides "direct evidence that the level of competition directly determines the magnitude" of the effect (154).

**The shape of the curve is an empirical question.** Psychology and management also hold a strong prior about the form such relationships take. Grant and Schwartz (2011, 62) argue that "there is no such thing as an unmitigated good," and Pierce and Aguinis (2013, 313) propose that "all seemingly monotonic positive relations reach context-specific inflection points after which the relations turn asymptotic and often negative." The best-known application to peers is the too-much-talent effect. Across national soccer teams and professional basketball teams, Swaab et al. (2014, 1584) reported that "top talent benefited performance only up to a point, after which the marginal benefit of talent decreased and turned negative." The mechanism they proposed is a contest among individuals within the team: "In the absence of a clearly defined pecking order, energy that would normally be steered toward intrateam coordination and performance is diverted toward jockeying for dominance" (1582). The evidence for the downturn depended on the test. A significant negative quadratic term does not establish a downturn, because "the quadratic formula is unable to generate a long plateau and so, when its functional form is forced on the data, it generates a spurious sign change" (Simonsohn 2018, 539). Estimating separate slopes for low and high values, Simonsohn found that the slope for the most talented soccer teams was positive and concluded that "these data do not support the conclusion that there is such a thing as 'too much talent'" (553). Gula et al. (2021, 301) reached the same conclusion for 64 seasons of professional basketball: "We did not observe any case in which increased talent was detrimental to team success." The same tests have since been applied elsewhere. In 42 seasons of seven European football leagues, talent "did not display diminishing returns, as its impact on success remained stable even at the highest concentrations of talent" (Long, Graf, and Bilalić 2024). Among 56,400 articles produced by research teams at the U.S. National Institutes of Health, a logarithmic function generally fit better than a quadratic one, and "at most one IC showed evidence of a TMT effect, and this effect was not statistically robust" (Kim et al. 2026, 8311). The defensible finding is that more talent does not harm the team: returns diminish in some settings and hold constant in others, but they do not reverse. Every study in this debate, however, measures the outcome of the team. The mechanism Swaab et al. proposed is a contest among individuals for standing within the team, and its consequences for those individuals, including which of them advance, remain unmeasured. Grant and Schwartz (2011, 69) offer the more durable insight: following McGuire, an inverted U can be read as "the resultant of two opposed mediating processes, both monotonic," and whether a peak exists depends on how the two processes compare in a given setting. That account is a reason to estimate the shape of an environmental effect rather than to assume it.

**How psychology separates sorting from effects.** Psychology relies mainly on assignment by the researcher. Laboratory studies randomize the feedback participants receive about their standing (Zell and Alicke 2010) or the relevance of a task (Tesser 1988), and Eden and Shani randomized the labels given to instructors. The relative age literature uses changes in cutoff dates as natural experiments. Field correlations remain open to sorting, and the field has long recognized the problem. Festinger (1954, 136) observed that people's tendency to join groups whose abilities are near their own, combined with influence and competition within those groups, "will guarantee that we will find relative similarity in opinions and abilities among persons who associate with one another." Stouffer and colleagues noted that better-educated men were more often assigned to the Air Corps (250), so the branch comparison mixes the effect of the reference group with the sorting of men into branches.

**What psychology leaves open.** The studies reviewed here leave the question of this dissertation open for specific reasons. First, the outcome is rarely selection. Stouffer and colleagues measured opinions about promotion, not promotion. Zell and Alicke (2010, 380) state that local dominance "has primarily been assessed using subjective outcome indices" and that "research is needed to specifically show that the local dominance effect extends to objective outcomes." Tesser's selection event was a hypothetical illustration, Eden and Shani removed the selection consequence by design, and the too-much-talent studies, from basketball and football to biomedical research teams (Gula et al. 2021; Long, Graf, and Bilalić 2024; Kim et al. 2026), measure the performance of the team rather than the fate of the individuals competing within it. The relative age effect is the exception, and it shows what the others miss. Second, no study writes down a formal model of how the comparison pool enters selection, and none fits one. Festinger's hypotheses are verbal, Tesser's central construct is unmeasured, the pool-size argument for the relative age effect is a thought experiment, and Grant and Schwartz describe their two-process account as "admittedly speculative" (69). Third, the data are small or come from single systems: laboratory groups of three to ten people, four instructors in one course, one army in one war, and male youth sport. In the extended basketball data, only 13 of 1,417 team-seasons lay beyond the estimated peak (Gula et al. 2021, 302). Fourth, the most widely cited claims about curve shape rest on a test that cannot distinguish a downturn from a plateau. The opening these limitations leave is a model in which the organization defines the comparison pool and the number of positions, peers shape both measured performance and how it is judged, and the chance of selection is the outcome; the shape of the relationship would then be derived, fitted to data, and tested with a method that can tell a reversal from diminishing returns. Psychology supplies the mechanisms such a model must contain: local comparison, interference among rivals, expectations that become performance, and selection from a pool that an organizational rule defines.

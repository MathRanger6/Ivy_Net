## Economics: relative rewards, peer spillovers, and the identification of social effects

**The question and its unit.** Labor and personnel economics asks how a worker's output and rewards depend on coworkers and on the rules by which an organization rewards and promotes. Its unit is the worker within a firm, team, or tournament, and its evidence comes from personnel records and from settings in which output is measured precisely, such as sales, piece-rate work, sports, and science. Three strands bear on this dissertation: theories of rewards based on relative standing, estimates of productivity spillovers among coworkers, and a methodological literature on whether social effects can be identified at all. The first strand concerns the selection channel, the second the development channel, and the third limits what any study of either can claim.

**Rewards based on rank make an individual's prospects depend on others.** Lazear and Rosen (1981) analyzed compensation schemes that pay "according to an individual's ordinal rank in an organization rather than his output level," and showed that such tournaments can induce the same efficient effort as pay for individual output. Rosen (1981, 845) described the related "phenomenon of Superstars, wherein relatively small numbers of people earn enormous amounts of money and dominate the activities in which they engage." Promotion rules extend the logic to careers. Kahn and Huberman (1988) derived "up-or-out" contracts from a two-sided incentive problem: a worker who does not make the grade is dismissed, because otherwise the firm would understate the worker's value. Asch and Warner (2001) built a model of a large hierarchical organization to explain three features of the U.S. military, namely flat pay spreads within rank, a generous pension, and heavy reliance on up-or-out promotion, and found that "the lack of lateral entry" plays a key role. Benson, Li, and Shue (2019) found that firms promote their best current salespeople even when other observable traits better predict managerial performance, consistent with the Peter Principle. In all of these accounts, what an individual receives depends on where that individual ranks among others, not on output alone.

**The strength of competitors can lower effort and performance.** Relative rewards create an externality among competitors, and several studies find that the composition of the field changes behavior. Brown (2011) modeled how "large differences in skill may reduce competitors' efforts" and found that higher-skill professional golfers scored about 0.8 strokes worse when Tiger Woods entered a tournament, with no effect on lower-skill players and an effect that grew during Woods's winning streaks. In a field study of fruit pickers, productivity was at least 50 percent higher under piece rates than under relative incentives, because workers partly internalized the cost their effort imposed on others, especially when working beside friends (Bandiera, Barankay, and Rasul 2005). Among Soviet mathematicians, the large post-1992 emigration revealed "strong competitive effects in idea space" for those who stayed, alongside knowledge spillovers among direct collaborators (Borjas and Doran 2015). That last finding locates both an adverse and a beneficial effect of peers within one population, separated by the kind of contact.

**Peer spillovers in output are real in some settings and absent in others.** Using high-frequency data from a supermarket chain, Mas and Moretti (2009) found that adding a highly productive cashier to a shift raised coworkers' productivity, but only among coworkers whom the new cashier could see. Random assignment of playing partners in professional golf, by contrast, revealed no effect of partners' ability on performance (Guryan, Kroft, and Notowidigdo 2009). In professional basketball, where output is measured on every possession and teammates change often, players differ both in their own productivity and in their ability to raise others' productivity (Arcidiacono, Kinsler, and Price 2017). In a garment plant that moved from individual to group piece rates, teams raised productivity by 14 percent on average, and more heterogeneous teams were more productive (Hamilton, Nickerson, and Owan 2003). In science, the unexpected deaths of 112 academic superstars lowered their collaborators' quality-adjusted publication rates by 5 to 8 percent (Azoulay, Graff Zivin, and Wang 2010), whereas the dismissal of scientists from German universities in 1933 produced "no evidence for peer effects at the local level" among those who stayed (Waldinger 2012). Guryan, Kroft, and Notowidigdo (2009) conclude that social effects vary across labor markets, across individuals, and with the form of incentives.

**Evaluations can misattribute what peers contribute.** If peers raise measured output, then the measure on which selection relies partly reflects the peers. Arcidiacono, Kinsler, and Price (2017) found that despite large spillovers, "worker compensation is largely determined by own productivity with little weight given to productivity spillovers." Kuehn (2023) found that college basketball players who score more because of their teammates are drafted higher, so that "college choice can significantly impact a prospect's draft position," and estimated that teams could raise the offensive value of their draft picks by about 20 percent by accounting for teammate effects. After the draft, rookies who played with teammates who raised their scoring by one point per 100 possessions earned second contracts worth an estimated 9.9 to 23.6 percent more (Kuehn and Rebessi 2023). These studies place peers in the evaluation channel: the same underlying ability is read differently depending on who surrounded the player.

**Identification is the central difficulty.** Manski (1993, 532) distinguished "endogenous effects, wherein the propensity of an individual to behave in some way varies with the behaviour of the group," from "exogenous (contextual) effects," which vary "with the exogenous characteristics of the group," and from correlated effects, in which members of a group behave alike because they share characteristics or face similar institutional environments. He showed that the first cannot be separated from the others without prior information on how reference groups are formed. Bramoullé, Djebbari, and Fortin (2009) showed that when each individual has a distinct reference group defined by a social network, endogenous and exogenous effects are generally identified, although identification fails for some network structures. Angrist (2014) sharpened the warning: regressions of individual outcomes on group averages are "vacuous," and when the average is defined as a leave-out mean the estimate is "determined by a generic intraclass correlation coefficient." This critique bears directly on displays of advancement against the peer mean, which this dissertation uses. Such displays describe a pattern; they do not by themselves estimate a peer effect, and the organization's own contribution, a correlated effect in Manski's terms, is part of what the peer mean records. The credible designs in this literature exploit random or rule-based assignment (Guryan, Kroft, and Notowidigdo 2009), sudden exogenous changes in the peer group (Azoulay, Graff Zivin, and Wang 2010; Waldinger 2012; Borjas and Doran 2015), or changes in incentive schemes within one workplace (Bandiera, Barankay, and Rasul 2005).

**What economics leaves open.** Economics supplies the strongest theory of rank-based selection and the most careful vocabulary for identification, but the studies reviewed here leave the question of this dissertation open for specific reasons. First, each estimate comes from one system: one supermarket chain, one golf tour, one garment plant, one field of science, one professional league. The signs disagree across systems, and Guryan, Kroft, and Notowidigdo (2009) attribute the disagreement to differences in markets and incentives, without a framework that predicts which systems should show which sign. Second, the adverse and beneficial effects of peers are usually estimated separately. Tournament studies measure how competitors change effort; spillover studies measure how coworkers change output. Borjas and Doran (2015) find both forces in one population, but neither they nor the other studies reviewed here model how the two combine to determine who is selected when a fixed number of slots must be filled. Third, the outcome is usually output or pay, not selection. Kuehn (2023) comes closest by relating draft position to college teammates, but in a single system and only through the inflation of measured output. Fourth, in the empirical studies reviewed here the number of slots relative to the number of competitors is fixed by the setting and is not varied, so none asks whether the relationship between peers and selection should change with scarcity, although the rules of a draft, a tenure system, or a promotion board set that ratio explicitly. The opening this leaves is a model in which development and competition for a fixed number of slots operate together, with predictions about how the strength of an individual's pool should change that individual's chance of selection, tested in more than one system.

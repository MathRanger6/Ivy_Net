## ASSISTANT SYNTHESIS

All of this section is **(Assistant judgment)**; supporting quotes and pages are in the source sections below.

1. **The comparison set is small, local, and similar, so it is the same set as the competitors.** Festinger's Hypothesis III ("The tendency to compare oneself with some other specific person decreases as the difference [...] increases," p. 120) and Zell & Alicke's local dominance effect (local comparisons override "more general, and typically more diagnostic" ones, p. 369, even when the local group is strangers) agree that people evaluate themselves against a few nearby, similar others. Zell & Alicke say outright that for "advancing in a company [...] local comparisons are the coin of the realm" (p. 377). For the dissertation, this means the peers who shape a person's self-evaluation are the people competing for the same slot.

2. **The same selection rate feels different depending on what peers got.** Stouffer et al.'s Military Police vs Air Corps contrast (pp. 250–253) is the founding evidence. MPs had the worst promotion chances (24% noncoms vs 47% in the Air Corps, p. 251), yet they rated promotion chances for "a soldier with ability" more favorably. The authors explain this through "expectations and their achievements relative to others in the same boat" (p. 251). The organization sets the number of slots (the Table of Organization, p. 253); peers set the reference point. So the outcome of selection feeds back into evaluation through peers.

3. **Competition for relative standing never settles, and peers act on each other's performance.** Festinger: "not everyone in a group can be slightly better than everyone else [...] a state of social quiescence is never reached" (p. 125). In Hoffman, Festinger & Lawrence, the two low scorers "can and do act so as to prevent the high scorer from getting additional points" (p. 127). Tesser & Smith found that on a self-relevant task "the stranger was helped more than the friend in 10 of the 13 sessions" (Tesser 1988, p. 185). Peers are therefore not a passive backdrop. They can raise or lower a rival's measured performance, which is the input that selection works from.

4. **What the organization expects becomes what people develop.** In Eden & Shani, instructors were told at random which trainees had high "command potential." That label explained "73% of the variance in performance" on tests scored by a blind evaluator (p. 194). The authors argue that "leadership may be suboptimally allocated" when it is "viewed as a scarce resource" (p. 198), and that the effect is "self-perpetuating" (pp. 197–198). An organization's early evaluation can therefore create the differences that later selection rewards, a cumulative-advantage path.

5. **Sorting and influence are named but never separated in field data.** Festinger says that joining similar groups plus influence within them "will guarantee that we will find relative similarity" (p. 136). Tesser's friend-GPA correlation (pp. 188–189) and Stouffer's branch comparison (education sorted men into branches, p. 250) have the same problem. Only the lab experiments and Eden & Shani's randomized labels identify effects, and they do so in tiny or artificial settings.

6. **What the set leaves open for selection among competitors for scarce slots.** No study measures who actually wins a fixed number of slots as a function of peers and organization:
   - Stouffer measures *opinions about* promotion **[outcome is not selection]**.
   - Zell & Alicke's outcomes are Likert self-ratings, and they say extension "to objective outcomes" is untested (p. 380) **[outcome is not selection] [imperfect measure]**.
   - Tesser's one selection event (Alice and Barbara's band tryout, p. 183) is a hypothetical.
   - Eden & Shani deliberately switched selection off: "grades and instructor evaluations in this course would not determine these trainees' admission to the next course" (p. 197) **[outcome is not selection]**.

   No source writes a formal model, and none fits one to data **[no model of mechanism] [model not fit to data]**. The evidence comes from lab triads, one army in one war, and one Israel Defense Forces course **[small data] [single system]**.

7. **How this positions the dissertation.** In the dissertation's terms, psychology explains how peers and the organization shape *evaluation* (how standing is perceived) and *development* (what people become). It does not explain *selection* (who gets the K slots). In the score-then-select framing, these studies inform the score side: local comparison, expectancy, and rival interference all shape measured performance and perceived standing. None of them models the selection step, a top-K cut among the same local competitors. A possible one-line version for Alex: "Psychology shows that the peers who matter for self-evaluation are the few nearby competitors, and that organizations' expectations shape performance; it never models how those same peers and expectations decide who wins a fixed number of promotions."

## 1. Festinger (1954) — A Theory of Social Comparison Processes

### Citation
Festinger, L. (1954). A theory of social comparison processes. *Human Relations, 7*(2), 117–140. https://doi.org/10.1177/001872675400700202

(PDF pp. 1–24 = printed pp. 117–140. The PDF has no usable text layer; quotations were OCR'd at 300 dpi and spot-checked against the page image.)

### Data / setting / sample
- Theory paper. Evidence is drawn from prior and concurrent small-group laboratory experiments (level-of-aspiration studies; Festinger, Gerard et al.; Schachter; Back; Hochbaum; Dreyer; Hoffman, Festinger & Lawrence; Festinger, Torrey & Willerman). "Three experiments, specifically designed to test predictions from this extension of the theory, have now been completed (5, 12, 19). They all provide good corroboration." (p. 117) No sample sizes are reported in this paper.

### Design
- Nine hypotheses (I–IX) with corollaries and derivations, each followed by supporting experimental evidence. Scope: "we shall develop below how the theory applies to the appraisal and evaluation of abilities as well as opinions." (p. 117)

### Main findings (verbatim; hypotheses in order)
- **Hypothesis I:** "There exists, in the human organism, a drive to evaluate his opinions and his abilities." (p. 117)
- **Hypothesis II:** "To the extent that objective, non-social means are not available, people evaluate their opinions and abilities by comparison respectively with the opinions and abilities of others." (p. 118)
  - "Corollary II A: In the absence of both a physical and a social comparison, subjective evaluations of opinions and abilities are unstable." (p. 119)
  - "Corollary II B: When an objective, non-social basis for the evaluation of one's ability or opinion is readily available persons will not evaluate their opinions or abilities by comparison with others." (p. 120)
  - On ability vs opinion: "Abilities are of course manifested only through performance which is assumed to depend upon the particular ability." (p. 118); "a person's evaluation of his ability to write poetry will depend to a large extent on the opinions which others have of his ability to write poetry." (p. 118)
- **Hypothesis III:** "The tendency to compare oneself with some other specific person decreases as the difference between his opinion or ability and one's own increases." (p. 120)
  - "If some other person's ability is too far from his own, either above or below, it is not possible to evaluate his own ability accurately by comparison with this other person. There is then a tendency not to make the comparison. [...] Nor does a person who is just beginning to learn the game of chess compare himself to the recognized masters of the game." (p. 120)
  - "Corollary III A: Given a range of possible persons for comparison, someone close to one's own ability or opinion will be chosen for comparison." (p. 121) Evidence (Whittemore): "the subjects stated that they had almost always spontaneously selected someone whose performance was close to their own to compete against." (p. 121)
  - "Corollary III B: If the only comparison available is a very divergent one, the person will not be able to make a subjectively precise evaluation of his opinion or ability." (p. 121)
  - "We may then conclude that there is selectivity in comparison on abilities and opinions and that one major factor governing the selectivity is simply the discrepancy between the person's own opinion or ability and that of another person." (p. 121)
  - "In the case of abilities, the phenomenal process is that of designation of status inferior or superior to those persons who are noncomparable to oneself." (p. 122)
- **Derivations A–C** (pp. 122–123): "Derivation A (from I, II, III): Subjective evaluations of opinions or of abilities are stable when comparison is available with others who are judged to be close to one's opinions or abilities." "Derivation B (from I, II, III): The availability of comparison with others whose opinions or abilities are somewhat different from one's own will produce tendencies to change one's evaluation of the opinion or ability in question." (p. 122) "Derivation C (from I, III B): A person will be less attracted to situations where others are very divergent from him than to situations where others are close to him for both abilities and opinions." (p. 123)
  - Evidence (Hochbaum): "Of those who felt they were very much above the others in the group, only 38 per cent wanted to return to the same group. Of those who felt that they were considerably inferior to the others, 68 per cent wanted to return to the same group." (p. 124)
- **Derivation D (pressures toward uniformity):** "The existence of a discrepancy in a group with respect to opinions or abilities will lead to action on the part of members of that group to reduce the discrepancy." (p. 124)
- **Hypothesis IV (unidirectional drive upward):** "There is a unidirectional drive upward in the case of abilities which is largely absent in opinions." (p. 124) "In Western culture, at any rate, there is a value set on doing better and better which means that the higher the score on performance, the more desirable it is." (pp. 124–125)
- **Hypothesis V:** "There are non-social restraints which make it difficult or even impossible to change one's ability. These non-social restraints are largely absent for opinions." (p. 125)
- **Competition never reaches rest (key for scarce slots):** "The resolution of these two pressures, which act simultaneously, is a state of affairs where all the members are relatively close together with respect to some specific ability, but not completely uniform. The pressures cease acting on a person if he is just slightly better than the others. It is obvious that not everyone in a group can be slightly better than everyone else. The implication is that, with respect to the evaluation of abilities, a state of social quiescence is never reached." (p. 125)
  - "Competitive behavior, action to protect one's superiority, and even some kinds of behavior that might be called cooperative, are manifestations in the social process of these pressures which do not reach quiescence." (p. 126)
  - "Derivation D₁: When a discrepancy exists with respect to opinions or abilities there will be tendencies to change one's own position so as to move closer to others in the group." "Derivation D₂: When a discrepancy exists with respect to opinions or abilities there will be tendencies to change others in the group to bring them closer to oneself." (p. 126)
  - "When pressures toward uniformity exist with respect to abilities, these pressures are manifested less in a social process and more in action against the environment which restrains movement. Thus, a person who runs more slowly than others with whom he compares himself, and for whom this ability is important, may [OCR: "many"] spend considerable time practising running." (p. 126)
  - "Because of this unidirectional push and the pressure toward uniformity, the individual is oriented toward some point on the ability continuum slightly better than his own performance or the performance of those with whom he is comparing himself." (p. 126)
- **Holding back the leader (sabotage-like evidence):** "An experiment by Hoffman, Festinger, and Lawrence (19) specifically designed to test parts of the present theory, shows this competitive process clearly. In a performance situation where one of three persons is scoring considerably above the other two, these two can and do act so as to prevent the high scorer from getting additional points. Thus, when the situation is arranged such that the performance of each person is controllable by the others in the group, action is taken to change the position of the members to reduce the discrepancies which exist." (p. 127)
  - Top performer may help others: "It is also possible that he will devote considerable time and effort to trying to improve the performance of the others in the group to a point where at least some of them are close to, but not equal to, him. This could take the form of helping them practice, coaching them, motivating them to improve and the like. Once comparability has been achieved, however, the process should change to the familiar competitive one." (p. 127)
- **Derivation D₃ (ceasing comparison with divergent others):** "When a discrepancy exists with respect to opinions or abilities there will be tendencies to cease comparing oneself with those in the group who are very different from oneself." (p. 128) "In other words, one merely ceases to compare oneself with those persons." (p. 128)
- **Hypothesis VI:** "The cessation of comparison with others is accompanied by hostility or derogation to the extent that continued comparison with those persons implies unpleasant consequences." (p. 129) "The process of making others incomparable results in a "status stratification" where some are clearly inferior and others are clearly superior." (p. 129) "Corollary VI A: Cessation of comparison with others will be accompanied by hostility or derogation in the case of opinions. In the case of abilities this will not generally be true." (p. 129)
  - Evidence: "When the nature of the situation allowed, the two low scoring subjects ceased to compete against the high scorer and began to compete against each other. When they did this they also rated the intelligence of the high scorer as considerably higher than their own, thus acknowledging his superiority." (p. 129)
- **Hypothesis VII and corollaries (importance, attraction, relevance):** "Any factors which increase the importance of some particular group as a comparison group for some particular opinion or ability will increase the pressure toward uniformity concerning that ability or opinion within that group." (p. 130) "Corollary to Derivation E: An increase in the importance of an ability or an opinion, or an increase in its relevance to immediate behavior, will increase the pressure toward reducing discrepancies concerning that opinion or ability." (p. 130) Example: "a person's drive to evaluate his intellectual ability will be stronger when he must decide between going to graduate school or taking a job." (p. 130) Hoffman et al. result: "the competition with the high scorer was significantly greater for the high importance than for the low importance condition." (p. 130)
  - "once inferior or superior status has been conferred, there will be a cessation of competition with respect to those who have been thus rendered incomparable." (p. 131) "In other words, he would be more competitive within a narrower range." (p. 131)
  - "Corollary VII A: The stronger the attraction to the group the stronger will be the pressure toward uniformity concerning abilities and opinions within that group." (p. 131) "Corollary VII B: The greater the relevance of the opinion or ability to the group, the stronger will be the pressure toward uniformity concerning that opinion or ability." (p. 132)
- **Hypothesis VIII:** "If persons who are very divergent from one's own opinion or ability are perceived as different from oneself on attributes consistent with the divergence, the tendency to narrow the range of comparability becomes stronger." (p. 133)
- **Hypothesis IX:** "When there is a range of opinion or ability in a group, the relative strength of the three manifestations of pressures toward uniformity will be different for those who are close to the mode of the group than for those who are distant from the mode." (pp. 134–135)
- **Groups and status ("Implications for Group Formation and Societal Structure," pp. 135–137):**
  - "People, then, tend to move into groups which, in their judgment, hold opinions which agree with their own and whose abilities are near their own. And they tend to move out of groups in which they are unable to satisfy their drive for self evaluation." (p. 136)
  - "These selective tendencies to join some and leave other associations, together with the influence process and competitive activity which arise when there is discrepancy in a group, will guarantee that we will find relative similarity in opinions and abilities among persons who associate with one another [...]." (p. 136)
  - "The segmentation into groups which are relatively alike with respect to abilities also gives rise to status in a society. [...] It is also important to the members of the lower status to maintain them for it is in this way that they can relatively ignore the differences and compare themselves with their own group. Comparisons with members of a different status group, either higher or lower, may sometimes be made on a phantasy level, but very rarely in reality." (p. 136)
- **Forced comparability ("Consequences of Preventing Incomparability," pp. 137–138):** "We have then created a situation where a person's values and strivings are quite out of line with his performance and we would expect, if he is below others, deep experiences of failure and feelings of inadequacy with respect to this ability." (p. 137) Second case: a person "prevented from leaving the group" (p. 138).
- **Summary:** "social influence processes and some kinds of competitive behavior are both manifestations of the same socio-psychological process [...]. Both stem directly from the drive for self evaluation and the necessity for such evaluation being based on comparison with other persons." (p. 138) "The theory is tentatively supported by a variety of data and is readily amenable to further empirical testing." (p. 138)

### Mechanism or model (formal? fit to data?)
- Mechanism: a drive to evaluate (I) + comparison with similar others (II–III) + upward push for abilities (IV) + hard-to-change abilities (V) ⇒ persistent competition, local comparison groups, and status strata.
- **(Assistant judgment:)** Axiomatic in form (numbered hypotheses, corollaries, derivations) but verbal, not mathematical. Nothing is estimated; evidence is directional confirmation from small lab experiments. The author concedes that "much of the theory which is to be presented here is not "plausible"" in the common-sense meaning, but that it "leads to testable derivations." (p. 117)

### Separating sorting from effects
- Festinger names both processes but does not separate them empirically: sorting ("People [...] tend to move into groups [...] whose abilities are near their own," p. 136) and influence/competition within groups (D₁, D₂, pp. 126–127) jointly "guarantee that we will find relative similarity" (p. 136). **(Assistant judgment:)** This is an early statement that homophily (selection into groups) and influence produce the same cross-sectional signature — the identification problem later formalized in network science.

### Limitations (tagged)
- Evidence for abilities is thin, stated by author: "In the case of abilities the evidence is less direct for a number of reasons. First, there have been fewer studies conducted relevant to this point. Second, since the process resulting from pressure to reduce discrepancies concerning abilities is not clearly shown in a social process, and since it is complicated by the drive to do better and better, it is harder to identify." (p. 127) **[small data] [imperfect measure]**
- "Unfortunately there are no relevant data from experiments concerning opinions." (p. 130); "No other evidence bearing on Corollary VII B has been located." (p. 133); "The conceptual definition of relevance of an opinion or an ability to a group is not completely clear." (p. 132) **[small data]**
- Culture-bound upward drive: "Whether or not this is culturally determined, and hence culturally variable, is an important question but one with which we will not occupy ourselves here." (p. 125) **[single system]**
- **(Assistant judgment:)** All evidence comes from short lab sessions with small groups (triads, groups of four), outcomes are self-reported evaluations, levels of aspiration, or points-blocking. **[small data] [model not fit to data] [outcome is not selection]** The Hoffman et al. points-blocking design is the closest analogue to competing for a slot, but no fixed number of winners exists.

### Channel and source (Assistant judgment)
- **Channel:** EVALUATION (self-evaluation by comparison) primarily; DEVELOPMENT secondarily (D₁: practice/study to close the gap, p. 126); proto-SELECTION in the Hoffman et al. result (low scorers suppress the leader's points, p. 127) and in the claim that ability competition has no equilibrium because "not everyone in a group can be slightly better than everyone else" (p. 125).
- **Source:** PEERS (similar others; the local comparison group). Organization enters only through group attraction, relevance, and restraints on leaving (pp. 131–138).

### Design

### Main findings (verbatim)

**(a) Relative deprivation introduced (Chapter 4, Section III, "Personal Commitment, Personal Esprit, and the Concept of Relative Deprivation," begins p. 122, PDF 134).**
- "To help explain such variations in attitude, by education, age, and marital condition, a general concept would be useful. Such a concept may be that of relative deprivation, which, as we shall see, is to prove quite helpful in ordering a rather disparate collection of data both in this chapter and in other chapters of these volumes. The idea is simple, almost obvious, but its utility comes in reconciling data, especially in later chapters, where its applicability is not at first too apparent. The idea would seem to have a kinship to and, in part, include such well-known sociological concepts as "social frame of reference," "patterns of expectation," or "definitions of the situation."" (p. 125, PDF 137)
- "Becoming a soldier meant to many men a very real deprivation. But the felt sacrifice was greater for some than for others, depending on their standards of comparison." (p. 125, PDF 137)
- Married-man example: "The very fact that draft boards were more liberal with married than with single men provided numerous examples to the drafted married man of others in his shoes who got relatively better breaks than he did. Comparing himself with his unmarried associates in the Army, he could feel that induction demanded greater sacrifice from him than from them; and comparing himself with his married civilian friends he could feel that he had been called on for sacrifices which they were escaping altogether." (p. 125, PDF 137)
- Older men: deferments "thus providing the older soldiers, like the married soldiers, with ready-made examples of men with comparable backgrounds who were experiencing less deprivation." (p. 126, PDF 138)
- Link to promotion and education: "The concept of relative deprivation is particularly helpful in evaluating the role of education in satisfaction with status or job [...]. With higher levels of aspiration than the less educated, the better educated man had more to lose in his own eyes and the eyes of his friends by failure to achieve some sort of status in the Army. Hence, frustration was greater for him than for others if a goal he sought was not attained—and this happened often, indeed, as a consequence of the kind of criteria which the Army traditionally employed in selecting enlisted men for promotion." (pp. 153–154, PDF 165–166)

**(b) Military Police vs Air Corps (Chapter 6, subsection "Who Were the Most Critical of the Army's Promotion Opportunities?", pp. 250–254, PDF 262–266).**
- "Data from research surveys to be presented will show, as would be expected, that those soldiers who had advanced slowly relative to other soldiers of equal longevity in the Army were the most critical of the Army's promotion opportunities. But relative rate of advancement can be based on different standards by different classes of the Army population. [...] Hence we would expect, at a given rank and a given longevity, that the better educated would be more likely than others to complain of the slowness of promotion. The facts, as we shall see, tend to bear this out. The better educated, in spite of their superior chances of promotion, were the most critical." (p. 250, PDF 262)
- Survey item: "Do you think a soldier with ability has a good chance for promotion in the Army?" (p. 250; Chart IX, p. 252)
- "Longevity is held roughly constant by taking only men who had been in the Army 1 to 2 years. It will be noted that more of the less educated, among both privates and noncoms in both branches, had favorable opinions than did the better educated. For example, among privates and Pfc's in the Military Police, 33 per cent of the less educated said that a soldier with ability had a very good chance for promotion, as compared with 21 per cent of the better educated privates and Pfc's. Finally, it will be seen, among both privates and noncoms in each educational group, that the Air Corps men tended to take a dimmer view of promotion opportunities for men of ability in the Army than did the Military Police." (pp. 250–251, PDF 262–263)
- The paradox and its interpretation: "Without reference to the theory that such opinions by soldiers represent a relationship between their expectations and their achievements relative to others in the same boat with them, such a finding would be paradoxical, indeed. For chances of promotion in the Military Police were about the worst in any branch of the Army—among this sample of men in the Army 1 to 2 years, only 24 per cent of MP's were noncoms as compared with 47 per cent of the Air Corps men. The MP's felt, too, that as a branch the Military Police had been discriminated against in getting ratings, two thirds of them saying in answer to another question that MP's do not have as good a chance for promotion as men in other branches." (p. 251, PDF 263)
- "But consider a high school graduate or college man in the Military Police with Army longevity of 1 to 2 years. The chances of his being a noncom were 34 out of 100, based on the proportions of noncoms in this sample at this time. If he earned the rating, he was one of the top third among his fellows of equal educational status. If he failed to earn the rating, he was in the same boat with two thirds of his fellows with equal schooling. Contrast him with the Air Corps man of the same education and longevity. The chances of the latter's being a noncom were 56 in 100 [...]. If he had earned a rating, so had the majority of his fellows in the branch, and his achievement was relatively less conspicuous than in the MP's. If he had failed to earn a rating, while the majority had succeeded, he had more reason to feel a sense of personal frustration, which could be expressed as criticism of the promotion system, than if he were one of two thirds in the same boat, as among the MP's." (p. 251, PDF 263)
- Less educated: "In the MP sample, only 17 per cent of the less educated were noncoms; in the Air Corps sample, the corresponding figure was 47 per cent. An MP who did not complete high school would feel unusually rewarded compared with others in his outfit in becoming a noncom; one who remained a private had so much company that he hardly could view discrimination against him as a reflection on his personal competence. In the Air Corps, those with ratings had almost as much company as those who remained privates—with less room for personal satisfaction over comparative achievement and more room for dissatisfaction over comparative failure to climb the status ladder." (p. 253, PDF 265)

- Within- vs between-group reversal: "True, cross tabulation, within a particular subgroup, of opinions about promotion and expressions of job satisfaction will almost invariably show that men who were most critical about promotions were also least satisfied with their jobs. But that is within a given subgroup. As between subgroups, the relationship may vanish or reverse itself. In the case of the comparison of the Military Police and the Air Corps it reverses itself. Although the Air Corps men were more critical of promotion, they also were more likely than the MP's to be satisfied with their Army job. For example, 36 per cent of the Air Corps men in this sample said they would not change to some other Army job if given a chance, whereas only 21 per cent of the MP's gave this response." (p. 253, PDF 265)

**(c) Broader replication across Forces (Chart X, pp. 254–257, PDF 266–269).**
- "These data were especially selected to exhibit the structure of opinion on two sharply contrasting groups with respect to promotion opportunities in the Army. It is desirable to see whether the same general pattern holds up on a broader basis, where there is less contrast between groups." (p. 254, PDF 266)
- "In general, differences in opinion about promotion opportunities are rather small, tending in any individual case to be somewhat less striking than in the extreme illustration presented earlier, but a definite pattern is present [...]." (p. 254)
- "To be conservative, we should limit our conclusion by saying that a force with relatively less promotion chances tended to have a larger proportion of men speaking very favorably of promotion opportunities than another force with greater promotion chances." (p. 257, PDF 269)

### Mechanism or model (formal? fit to data?)
- Mechanism stated verbally: opinions "represent a relationship between their expectations and their achievements relative to others in the same boat with them" (p. 251). The reference group is people of the same education and longevity in the same branch ("among his fellows of equal educational status," p. 251).
- **(Assistant judgment:)** Not formal. No model of how the promotion rate of the reference group enters satisfaction is written down or fit; the argument is an ex post reading of cross-tabulations. The binomial sign test in the Chart X footnote (p. 257, n.: "the likelihood of getting 12 or more successes by chance, in 16 comparisons, would be less than .04") is the only inferential statistic in this passage. The authors' own wording ("The idea is simple, almost obvious," p. 125) signals a heuristic concept, later formalized by others (Merton & Rossi; Runciman; Davis 1959 — not in this source set).

### Separating sorting from effects
- Rank and longevity held "roughly constant" (p. 251); education stratified. But branch assignment is not random, and the authors note education sorted men into branches: "educated men tended more than others to be assigned to the component of the Army which had the highest proportion of noncoms" (p. 250, PDF 262). **(Assistant judgment:)** Air Corps vs MP differences could reflect who was sent where, not only local promotion rates.
- Authors explicitly disclaim causal policy inference: "a caution must be sounded against assuming from these findings that a liberalization of promotion policy—which might reduce rather than raise the relative self-gratification of the successful men and increase rather than reduce the sense of defeat of the unsuccessful—would increase job satisfaction. What actually would happen we do not know, because this could be determined only from controlled experiments, which were never made." (p. 257, PDF 269)

### Limitations (tagged)
- "But when we get down to finer details, available data are simply not adequate. Particularly, the relative roles of job, health, and dependency cannot be separately evaluated, as they operated differentially by education, age, and marital condition." (p. 128, PDF 140) **[imperfect measure] [no model of mechanism]**
- No experiments: "this could be determined only from controlled experiments, which were never made." (p. 257) **[no model of mechanism] [model not fit to data]**
- Hand-picked extreme contrast; small cells in broader replication: "The number of cases on which an individual bar is based is in many instances very small. No comparison is shown if the numbers in the sample for a particular rank, longevity, and education group fell below 30 for any one of the three Army Forces." (p. 254) **[small data]** (Air Corps noncom cell for less educated N = 70.)
- **(Assistant judgment:)** The outcome is an *opinion about* promotion chances, not promotion itself; actual selection rates enter only as the background reference-group rate (24% vs 47% noncoms). **[outcome is not selection]** One army, one war, white enlisted men only (Chart IX header). **[single system]**

### Channel and source (Assistant judgment)
- **Channel:** EVALUATION (how one perceives one's standing and the fairness of the promotion system) conditioned on the realized SELECTION rate of one's reference group. This is the earliest empirical statement that the same selection outcome is experienced differently depending on how many comparable others got the slot.
- **Source:** PEERS ("fellows of equal educational status" in the same branch) inside an ORGANIZATIONAL structure (branch T/O, which "specified the number of grades authorized," p. 253, fixes the number of scarce slots). The organization sets K; peers set the comparison.

## 3. Zell & Alicke (2010) — The Local Dominance Effect in Self-Evaluation

### Citation
Zell, E., & Alicke, M. D. (2010). The local dominance effect in self-evaluation: Evidence and explanations. *Personality and Social Psychology Review, 14*(4), 368–384. https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868310366144

(Printed page = PDF page + 367; PDF 1 = p. 368. Clean text layer.)

### Data / setting / sample
- Review article synthesizing (a) correlational school research on the frog-pond / big-fish-little-pond effect and (b) a program of lab experiments by the authors with undergraduates (Ohio University is named as the participants' school in the Zell & Alicke 2008b description, p. 375).
- **Sample sizes are not reported in this review for any experiment.** The only size given is session size: "Ten participants were brought to the laboratory and subdivided into two minimal groups of five." (pp. 372–373). Comparison pools are described by size ("more than 500 previous participants," p. 371; "nearly 1,500 previous test takers," p. 372; "more than 1,000 students," p. 374). Ns must be taken from the primary papers (Buckingham & Alicke 2002; Zell & Alicke 2009; Alicke, Zell & Bloom 2010) — not in this source set.

### Design
- Typical paradigm: participants complete a bogus task (lie detection or verbal reasoning), then receive manipulated feedback crossing a local standard (rank vs one coactor or a small group) with a general standard (rank vs hundreds of past participants or school vs other schools), in full factorial with local-only and general-only control cells. Outcomes: Likert self-evaluations of ability and mood.

### Main findings (verbatim)
- **Definition:** "The local dominance effect is the tendency for comparisons with a few, discrete individuals to have a greater influence on self-assessments than comparisons with larger aggregates." (Abstract, p. 368)
- "Our central thesis is that when multiple comparison standards are available for self-evaluation, people rely on the most local comparison information while deemphasizing more general, and typically more diagnostic, forms of comparison feedback. This tendency is referred to as the local dominance effect [...]. Local information specifies a person's status in relation to one or a few discrete individuals (e.g., friends, family members, classmates), whereas general information includes comparisons with aggregates and larger samples." (p. 369)
- "Therefore, local dominance often promotes biased self-evaluations. Students who, by chance, associate with friends or peers who perform terribly on standardized tests may have inflated ability perceptions. Students, on the other hand, who have friends or peers who perform exceptionally well are likely to have deflated ability perceptions. These self-evaluative consequences are important, given the powerful role that self-views have in shaping achievement and career strivings (Marsh & O'Mara, 2008)." (p. 369)
- On Festinger: "Festinger's (1954) seminal article on social comparison theory, which launched this field of investigation, was a hodgepodge of propositions rather than a systematic theory." (p. 368) "We argue that Festinger ignored an essential component of social comparison but turned out to be essentially correct in his inadvertence." (p. 369)
- **Key experiments:**
  - Buckingham & Alicke (2002): feedback vs one same-gender coactor (confederate) and vs "the average student at their school, based on results from more than 500 previous participants." "Consistent with the local dominance hypothesis, individual comparison with the coactor, but not aggregate comparison with the average student, influenced self-evaluations of lie detection ability." (p. 371)
  - Zell & Alicke (2009, Study 1): standing at university (local) vs university vs other schools (general). "intergroup comparison information detailing the standing of the participant's school in relation to other schools influenced self-evaluations and mood only when intragroup comparison information was unavailable." (p. 372)
  - Zell & Alicke (2009, Study 2): groups of five; best or worst in group vs group "better than 90% or 30% of several hundred similar groups." "students who ranked best in a low-quality group evaluated themselves more favorably than students who ranked worst in a high-quality group." (p. 372)
  - Zell & Alicke (2009, Study 3): three nested sources. "participants in each condition used the most local comparison information available for self-evaluation. [...] Finally, the three feedback types each exerted comparable effects when provided alone." (p. 372)
  - Alicke, Zell & Bloom (2010): two minimal groups of five; ranked fifth overall but worst in group vs sixth overall but best in group. "participants' overreliance on local comparison information led them to evaluate themselves more favorably when they ranked sixth than fifth. These findings indicate that the local dominance effect arises even when good members of bad groups have a lower objective rank than bad members of good groups." (p. 373) "the local dominance effect does not require a large difference between the size of the local and general groups" (p. 373)
  - Zell & Alicke (2009, Study 4): local = best/worst of a small group; general = "better or worse than more than 1,000 students at their school." "a focused contrast showed that participants, somewhat ironically, evaluated themselves more favorably, and reported greater positive affect, when they ranked best in the local group but at the 32nd percentile as opposed to worst in the local group but at the 84th percentile." (p. 374)
  - Zell & Alicke (2009, Study 5): "About 80% of the participants preferred general comparison feedback, and general comparisons were rated as substantially more useful than local comparisons for self-evaluation. Thus, participants clearly recognize the superior diagnostic value of large-sample statistics, but they nevertheless rely on local comparison information for self-evaluation when it is available." (p. 374)
- **Frog-pond (correlational) evidence cited:** "a robust negative relationship between school quality and academic self-concept is consistently obtained after controlling for student academic ability (Marsh, 1987; Marsh & Parker, 1984)." (p. 370) "Thus, the positive effect of attending a top school is insufficient to counteract the negative effect of having low status in the local environment." (p. 371)
- **Robustness claim:** "The local dominance effect is pervasive and robust. In fact, as the state of the literature currently stands, neither we nor anybody else has uncovered contextual or individual difference factors that eliminate the tendency to favor local over general comparisons." (p. 379)

### Explanations offered (mechanism)
- Two primary rationales (pp. 369–370): (1) evolutionary small-group living: "To meet the exigencies of survival, people needed to estimate how they would fare in competition with local group members (Buss, 2009)." (p. 369); (2) habitual exposure: "This tendency to make local comparisons is reinforced in adolescence and adulthood where competition for mates, jobs, and other emotional and material rewards takes place primarily with others in the immediate environment." (p. 370)
- "We believe that the local dominance effect derives largely from the habitual experience of making social comparisons in small groups such as those that occur early in development among family members, schoolmates, and friends." (p. 373)
- Alternatives tested and (per authors) ruled out: collective vs individual self (Study 4, p. 374); perceived usefulness (Study 5, p. 374); abstractness/concreteness: "Surprisingly, information concreteness had no influence on the local dominance effect." (p. 375); social categorization (Zell & Alicke 2008b, p. 375).
- Moderators with some support: self-protection ("aggregate statistical information is especially likely to lose its appeal when it has negative self-implications," p. 376); physical proximity (mixed, p. 376); entitativity (proposed, pp. 376–377).
- **(Assistant judgment:)** No formal model. "Local" is defined ordinally and relatively ("these are relative rather than absolute designations," p. 369); there is no weighting function over comparison sets that could be fit.

### Does it extend to real decisions or organizations?
- Not tested; authors flag it as open. "The local dominance effect has primarily been assessed using subjective outcome indices (i.e., Likert-type scale responses). It remains to be seen whether local dominance obtains on objective outcome measures including task performance and decision making. [...] yet research is needed to specifically show that the local dominance effect extends to objective outcomes." (p. 380)
- Scarce positions, explicitly: "Accurate calibration of these self-components requires comparisons with the population of people with whom one might compete for scarce positions. To become a fiction writer, dancer, or musician, for example, requires comparison with the people who are likely to compete for the available positions in these occupations." (p. 377)
- Promotion, explicitly: "When it comes to such ordinary goals as making a Little League team, advancing in a company, or succeeding in courtship, local comparisons are the coin of the realm." (p. 377)
- Future direction tied to selection: "A high school athlete who wishes to play basketball at a major college program and a graduate student who hopes to land a research position in a university would be wise to avoid exclusively relying on local comparisons to assess their prospects. Whether such people actually display such wisdom and cast their social comparison net more broadly, or continue to rely on comparisons with their immediate peers, is a question that awaits further research." (p. 379)
- Organizations (pay): "Although the relative impact of local versus general pay comparisons has not been assessed, research indicates that wage comparisons with local group members are more influential than wage comparisons with other targets. Specifically, wage comparisons with colleagues in the same organization have a greater impact on pay satisfaction than wage comparisons with people in other organizations (Law & Wong, 1998)." (p. 378) "Thus, employee attitudes and behaviors can be powerfully shaped by the local environment in which they find themselves, and our findings suggest that dissatisfaction at the local level is unlikely to be assuaged by relatively good outcomes in a broader context." (p. 378)
- Consequences named (not measured): "Overestimation entails pitfalls such as overconfidence, or the selection of situations, interaction partners, and careers for which one is ill suited [...]. Underestimation, on the other hand, can lead to lack of self-confidence and missed opportunities" (p. 377).

### Separating sorting from effects
- Lab experiments randomize feedback, so sorting is not an issue there. For the field frog-pond evidence the authors acknowledge it: "Despite these statistical controls, it is possible that in some samples, good students at average schools are actually more capable, and therefore should evaluate themselves more positively, than bad students at prestigious schools. Excellent students are occasionally drawn to mediocre schools (e.g., to stay close to home), whereas top schools often have some weak students" (p. 372); this motivated the Alicke et al. (2010) design.

### Limitations (tagged)
- Correlational field base: "the correlational nature of most of this research leaves important questions about the more general local dominance phenomenon unanswered. Most importantly, these studies cannot provide direct contrasts between the relative impact of local comparison standards versus more diagnostic data sources" (p. 371) **[imperfect measure]**
- Early aggregate feedback was vague: "the general or aggregate comparison was nonspecific, informing participants only that they were better or worse than average." (p. 371) **[imperfect measure]**
- Subjective outcomes only; extension to objective performance and decisions untested (p. 380, quoted above). **[outcome is not selection] [imperfect measure]**
- Pay application untested: "the relative impact of local versus general pay comparisons has not been assessed" (p. 378). **[single system]**
- Task importance unclear: "it is reasonable to assume that such abilities are at least moderately important to college students. Nevertheless, it is possible that people will make greater use of diagnostic, large-sample data [...] for characteristics that are ideographically vital to their goals and prospects." (p. 379) **(Assistant judgment:)** This is exactly the stakes condition of a promotion board. **[outcome is not selection]**
- Boundary condition: general comparisons alone can have larger effects than local alone (Klein 2003; Locke 2007), so dominance "may not occur when people receive the feedback types separately." (p. 373)
- **(Assistant judgment:)** Undergraduate lab samples, one-shot sessions, bogus tasks, Ns unreported in the review. **[small data] [single system] [no model of mechanism] [model not fit to data]**

### Channel and source (Assistant judgment)
- **Channel:** EVALUATION (self-evaluation). Implied downstream effect on DEVELOPMENT and on self-selection into contests ("career strivings," p. 369; "missed opportunities," p. 377), but untested.
- **Source:** PEERS — specifically the local, small comparison set, even of strangers ("local dominance arises even when the local group consists of complete strangers who by chance happen to be taking part in the study at the same time," p. 376). Organization appears only in the pay discussion (colleagues "in the same organization," p. 378). **Key for dissertation:** the authors themselves frame local comparison as the relevant one for "advancing in a company" (p. 377), i.e., the comparison set that matters psychologically coincides with the competitor set that matters for selection — but no study measures selection.

## 4. Tesser (1988) — Toward a Self-Evaluation Maintenance Model of Social Behavior

### Citation
Tesser, A. (1988). Toward a self-evaluation maintenance model of social behavior. In L. Berkowitz (Ed.), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* (Vol. 21, pp. 181–227). San Diego, CA: Academic Press. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(08)60227-0

(Scan with no text layer; OCR'd at 300 dpi. Page mapping: PDF 1 = p. 181; each later PDF sheet is a two-page spread, PDF n = pp. 2n+178 and 2n+179, e.g., PDF 3 = pp. 184–185, PDF 22 = pp. 222–223.)

### Data / setting / sample
- Programmatic review of the "Georgia group" studies (Tables I–III, pp. 198–201). Mix of lab experiments with undergraduates (pairs of friends, confederates), school field studies (fifth/sixth graders; high school students), archival/biographical data (William Owens's University of Georgia freshman biographical data; scientists' biographies), and one couples study (Giuliano & Wegner, "50 couples," p. 192).
- Sample sizes are mostly not reported in this chapter. Exceptions: Tesser & Smith (1980) counts sessions ("10 of the 13 sessions," p. 185); Giuliano & Wegner "50 couples" (p. 192).

### Design
- Model: "The SEM model assumes that (1) persons behave in a manner that will maintain or increase self-evaluation and (2) one's relationships with others have a substantial impact on self-evaluation." (pp. 181–182)
- Studies manipulate two of the three model variables (closeness, performance, relevance) and measure the third (p. 184: "The studies look at changes in relative performance as a function of the relevance and closeness of the other person, changes in closeness as a function of the relevance and performance of the other, and changes in relevance or self-definition as a function of the other's closeness and performance.").

### Main findings (verbatim)
**Comparison vs reflection; closeness, performance, relevance**
- "The SEM model is composed of two dynamic processes. Both the reflection process and the comparison process have as component variables the closeness of another and the quality of that other's performance. These two variables interact in affecting self-evaluation but do so in quite opposite ways in each of the processes." (p. 182)
- Reflection: "One's self-evaluation may be raised to the extent that a close other performs very well on some activity, that is, one can bask in the reflected glory of the close other's good performance." (p. 182)
- Comparison: "The outstanding performance of a close other can, however, cause one's own performance to pale by comparison and decrease self-evaluation. [...] And, again, the better the other's performance and the closer the psychological relationship, the greater the loss in self-evaluation through the comparison process." (p. 182)
- Closeness: "Psychological closeness is like unit relatedness (Heider, 1958): friends are closer than strangers, persons with more characteristics in common are closer than persons with fewer characteristics in common, and so on." (p. 182)
- Relevance: "Another's performance is relevant to an individual's self-definition to the extent that the performance is on a dimension that is important to the individual's self-definition and to the extent that the other's performance is not so much better or worse than the individual's own performance that comparisons are rendered difficult." (p. 183) "If the other's performance is highly relevant, then the comparison process will be relatively important and one will suffer by comparison to the close other's better performance. If the other's performance is minimally relevant the reflection process will be relatively important" (p. 183)

**Worked example built on a selection event (band tryout)**
- "Suppose Alice and her good friend Barbara try out for the high school symphonic band and only Barbara is selected. Suppose further that doing well in music is an important part of Alice's self-definition. [...] To prevent this loss, Alice can do a variety of things: she can alter the closeness of her relationship with Barbara. [...] Alice can also change her self-definition. [...] Finally, Alice can attempt to affect Barbara's performance. By reducing Barbara's performance she also reduces the threat of comparison. She can break Barbara's reed or hide her music for the next tryout or she can come to believe that Barbara's good performance was based on luck, etc. Or, she can attempt to alter her own performance by practicing more." (p. 183)

**Helping vs hindering another's performance (Tesser & Smith 1980; sabotage)**
- Prediction: "when relevance is high the closer the other the less help one would expect the other to be given. On the other hand, when relevance is low, [...] the closer the other the more help should be given to the other." (p. 184)
- Design: "Male subjects were recruited and asked to bring a friend to the lab with them. Each session was composed of two pairs of friends. [...] For half the subjects, the task was described as measuring important verbal skills, leadership, et cetera (high relevance). The remaining subjects were told that the task was not related to verbal intelligence or leadership or anything of importance that we could determine (low relevance). The task was actually based on the game Password." (p. 184) "If they want to help the other perform well (i.e., better than themselves), they could give clues that are easy; if they want to "hurt" the other (i.e., make him perform less well), they could give him difficult clues." (p. 185)
- Result: "The prediction from the SEM model was strongly upheld. When relevance was low the friend was helped more than the stranger in 10 of the 13 sessions. When relevance was high, the stranger was helped more than the friend in 10 of the 13 sessions." (p. 185)
- Cognitive version (Tesser & Campbell 1982): "when the task is irrelevant, subjects are more charitable toward the friend than toward the stranger. When the task is relevant, however, just the opposite is the case. Subjects are more charitable toward the stranger than toward the friend." (p. 187)
- Strong vs weak prediction relative to exchange theory: "The model, however, makes the strong prediction that when performance is on a highly relevant dimension, a psychologically distant other is more likely to receive help than a close other." (p. 202)

**Choosing/avoiding close others who outperform you (Pleban & Tesser 1981)**
- "when the confederate outperformed the subject, each of the expectations from the SEM model was sustained. [...] as the confederate's performance improved from the 60th percentile to the 80th percentile the subject's distance increased when the topic was one of high relevance; the subject's distance decreased or closeness increased when the topic was of low relevance." (p. 191) "It should be noted at the outset that level of performance made no difference when the subject outperformed the confederate." (p. 191)
- Table II summary: "The more decisively one is outperformed by another on a high-relevance dimension the further one sits and the less willing one is to work with the other. Relation reversed on low-relevance dimension." (p. 199)
- Siblings (archival): "When the respondent believes he is outperformed by his sibling, then the closer (in age) the sibling the less the identification with the sibling." (p. 196); father–son (Table II): "The more similar the son's accomplishment to the father's profession the more distant the relationship." (p. 199)

**Own performance and peer GPA (development channel)**
- Tesser, Campbell & Campbell (high school): "Within these groups, respondents' own "residualized" GPA was correlated with the GPA of a classmate that the respondent nominated as a friend and a classmate that the respondent did not nominate as a friend. [...] The only correlation which was significantly more positive than zero is that among high-relevance respondents and their friends." (pp. 188–189)
- Table III (nonlaboratory, described in Tesser & Campbell 1987): "The only significant predictor of additional school desired was grade point average relative to similar sex/race classmates." (p. 201)
- Relevance shifts (Tesser & Paulhus 1983): "the better another does relative to the self, the less relevant should be the performance dimension, particularly when that other is close. This is precisely the pattern that was found and the interaction is significant." (p. 194)

### Mechanism or model (formal? fit to data?)
- Systemic, three-variable feedback model: "As can be seen in Fig. 5, each of the variables is at the same time both a cause and an effect. [...] Each of the variables is systemically and interactively tied up with the other two." (p. 197)
- Only ordinal, interaction predictions: "Making such interaction predictions from the SEM model has two important liberating qualities for theory testing. First, it allows for the detection of SEM effects even in the presence of other, sometimes contrary, effects [...]. Second, interaction predictions also allow for making meaningful predictions given only ordinal information about the independent variables." (p. 203) "We can, however, make a specific interaction prediction: the greater the relevance, the more negative (less positive) should be the relationship of closeness to performance." (p. 203)
- Self-evaluation never measured: "Previous research intended as direct tests of the model has focused only on indicants of performance, closeness, and relevance. There has been no attempt to measure "self-evaluation."" (p. 208) Quoting Tesser & Campbell (1983): "a hypothetical construct, a theoretical fiction which is used to organize and make comprehensible the relationships among the variables that have empirical indicants" (p. 208).
- **(Assistant judgment:)** Named and structured ("model," with defined variables and signed interactions), but not mathematical; no functional form, no parameters, nothing fit. Tests are sign/interaction tests in factorial ANOVAs or counts of sessions. It is the most model-like of the five sources, and the one whose variables (closeness = network tie; performance; relevance = shared dimension) map most directly onto a network model of competitors. **[model not fit to data]**

### Separating sorting from effects
- Lab studies randomize relevance/performance, so the helping/hindering and seating-distance effects are causal within the lab. Field studies are correlational and the author says so (below). **(Assistant judgment:)** The friend–GPA correlation (pp. 188–189) cannot distinguish peer influence from friend selection by GPA — the same homophily-vs-influence problem Festinger raised.

### Limitations (tagged)
- "Although these studies are consistent with the present viewpoint, they are correlational and there are a number of plausible alternative explanations of the results [OCR: "?"]. What is needed is a more detailed theoretical analysis and more focused research." (p. 189) **[imperfect measure] [no model of mechanism]**
- Self-handicapping arm: "Clearly, this line of thinking is speculative." (p. 190)
- Construct never measured: "There has been no attempt to measure "self-evaluation."" (p. 208); causal role of affect: "I know of no work addressing this question." (p. 220) **[imperfect measure] [no model of mechanism]**
- Gender/culture scope (n. 2): "on the few occasions on which gender effects have been found the SEM effects have been stronger for males than for females. [...] the formulation may work best for people with a desire to enhance their self-evaluation individualistically, such as Western or even American society. Societies with a more collectivist orientation [...] might not show the same kind of effects." (p. 195) **[single system]**
- "There were no effects for females." (sibling study, p. 195)
- Family: "some of the fascinating and fundamental questions about the applicability of comparison and reflection processes in parent-child relationships have yet to be dealt with in any definitive way." (p. 196)
- **(Assistant judgment:)** No study in the chapter is set in a work organization; the outcomes are help/hurt in a lab game, seating distance, ratings, identification, desired schooling. The only selection event (band tryout) is a hypothetical illustration. **[outcome is not selection] [small data] [single system]**

### Group / organization implications
- The chapter's stated implications are prosocial behavior, own performance, relationships, development, depression: "It has implications for prosocial behavior, the helping and hurting of others to affect their performance. It has implications for one's own personal performance as well. There are implications for interpersonal relationships, attraction, unit formation, and the like" (p. 222). No organizational application is developed. Groups appear via BIRGing ("persons are more likely to bask in the reflected glory of a group with which they are associated or to "blast" a group with which they are not associated," p. 220).
- Negotiated complementary identities (speculative): "one might speculate that persons negotiate their self-identity with those around them [...]. The result of such a process would be a kind of bargain in which the participants agree on a set of complementary identities." (p. 205) **(Assistant judgment:)** a micro-mechanism for niche differentiation among close peers that would reduce direct competition for the same slot.

### Channel and source (Assistant judgment)
- **Channel:** EVALUATION (self-evaluation via comparison/reflection) driving behavior that affects others' and one's own DEVELOPMENT (effort; GPA co-movement with friends) and, in the hindering result, the inputs to SELECTION (making a close rival perform worse). Tesser's own illustrative case is a selection event with sabotage (p. 183), but the outcome studied is never a scarce slot.
- **Source:** PEERS, specifically close peers (friends, siblings, similar others); the model is about dyads, not organizations.

## 5. Eden & Shani (1982) — Pygmalion Goes to Boot Camp

### Citation
Eden, D., & Shani, A. B. (1982). Pygmalion goes to boot camp: Expectancy, leadership, and trainee performance. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 67*(2), 194–199. https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.67.2.194

(PDF pp. 1–6 = printed pp. 194–199.)

### Data / setting / sample
- "The sample included 105 soldiers, all men, in a combat command course in the Israeli Defense Forces. They were selected into the course on the basis of ability and motivation, and all had at least 11 years of schooling." (p. 196)
- "Instructors were four experienced training officers. Each commanded a group of about 30 trainees. The instructors were subordinate to a course commander who reported to the base commander. This intensive course involved an average of 16 hours of instructor-trainee contact daily for 15 weeks." (p. 196)
- Cell sizes (Table 1, p. 198): High expectancy n = 36; Unknown n = 32; Regular n = 37.
- Footnote 1 (p. 197): "Actually each group of trainees was commanded by an instructor and an assistant instructor, both of whom were officers. The expectancies of all eight (four instructors and four assistants) were manipulated in the same manner in this session."

### Design
- "Trainees were assigned to three expectancy conditions in an "after only" randomized block experimental design (Campbell & Stanley, 1966). They were blocked by general aptitude as measured by a standard army battery. Instructor effects were controlled since a third of each instructor's trainees were in each condition. We induced in each instructor different levels of expectancy with regard to different trainees' "command potential."" (p. 196)
- Abstract: "One-hundred and five trainees in a 15-week combat command course were matched on aptitude and randomly assigned to high, regular, and unspecified instructor-expectancy conditions." (p. 194)
- Timing of induction: "Four days before the trainees arrived at the training base, prior to any acquaintance between instructors and trainees, we induced differential expectations [...]" (pp. 196–197). Instructors were told (in part): "Based on this information we have predicted the command potential (CP) of each soldier. Experience with other classes has shown that course grades predict trainee CP in 95% of the cases. On the basis of these data we have classified your trainees into 3 groups; high, regular, and unknown CP, the latter due to insufficient information." (p. 197)
- Manipulation check: "To ascertain whether the induction "took," instructors rated their trainees' CP on a 9-point scale 1 week after the course began. The induction did influence mean CP ratings as intended (6.83, 5.31, and 4.29 for high, unknown, and regular CP induction, respectively, F(2, 102) = 25.03, p < .01)." (p. 197)
- Outcome measurement (evaluator blind): "Learning performance in four content areas was measured objectively by paper-and-pencil multiple-choice examinations and by performance tests administered by a commander who was not the trainee's instructor and was uninformed of the experiment." (p. 196) The four scores "were averaged to form a single overall measure of performance (Cronbach's coefficient a = .89)." (p. 196)
- Attitudes: "three items concerning how much the trainee desired to go on to the next course, the extent to which he would recommend the course to his friends, and overall satisfaction with the course (a = .84)." (p. 196)
- Leadership: trainee ratings on 10 Bowers & Seashore items plus nine added items, combined "into a single reliable measure (a = .93)." (p. 196)

### Main findings (verbatim)
- "Trainees of whom instructors had been induced to expect better performance scored significantly higher on objective achievement tests, exhibited more positive attitudes, and perceived more positive leadership behavior. Instructor expectancy, the manipulated independent variable, explained 73% of the variance in performance, 66% in attitudes, and 28% in leadership." (Abstract, p. 194)
- Table 1 (p. 198), means (SD) for High / Unknown / Regular; F; eta²:
  - Trainee performance: 79.98 (3.16) / 72.43 (3.63) / 65.18 (4.40); F = 132.04*; eta² = .73
  - Trainee attitudes: 4.55 (.43) / 3.76 (.42) / 3.11 (.44); F = 101.57*; eta² = .66
  - Instructor leadership: 3.97 (.41) / 3.57 (.51) / 3.23 (.57); F = 19.76*; eta² = .28
  - "* p < .01." Note: "All pairs so tested differed significantly beyond the .01 level."
- "The results strongly support the hypotheses. Trainees whose instructors were led to expect more did indeed learn more. We produced the Pygmalion effect among adult trainees." (p. 197)
- "This additudinal [sic] gap between the experimental groups implies that those trainees who fared well in the present course, due in large measure to high expectancy on the part of their instructors, are destined to begin the next course with more enthusiasm than their control comrades. Thus, the Pygmalion effect may constitute a self-perpetuating, as well as a self-fulfilling prophecy." (pp. 197–198)
- "The present experiment shows that superiors' expectancy regarding performance, rather than actual performance, may influence their leadership." (p. 198)
- "In nonexperimental situations natural expectations probably favor subordinates known for high past performance, relegating ordinary subordinates to mediocre leadership. Viewed as a scarce resource, leadership may be suboptimally allocated due to interpersonal expectancy effects." (p. 198)
- Closing: "Managers get the performance they expect." (p. 199)

### Mechanism or model (formal? fit to data?)
- Proposed mediator = instructor leadership: "By positing instructor leadership to be a medium by which expectancies might manifest themselves in interpersonal interaction, we sought to integrate research on Pygmalion and leadership. We hypothesized that inducing higher performance expectations influences instructors' leadership behavior in ways likely to fulfill their prophecy." (p. 195)
- "If these reported perceptions reflect actual instructor behavior, then leadership may be a means by which superiors unwittingly fulfill their own prophecies." (p. 198)
- Two candidate channels, not tested: "One possibility is that outside the classroom in private, one-on-one counseling sessions, each instructor treated different trainees differently, in accordance with his prior expectations. A second possibility is that instructors treated trainees differently in class sessions." (p. 198)
- No formal mediation analysis was run; leadership is analyzed as a separate dependent variable ("We analyzed leadership as a dependent variable influenced by expectancy," p. 195). **(Assistant judgment:)** Verbal causal story, no formal model, nothing fit beyond one-way ANOVA.

### Separating sorting from effects
- This is the design's strength: expectancy labels were randomly assigned within aptitude blocks and within instructor, induced before any contact, and outcomes were scored by a blind commander (pp. 196–197). **(Assistant judgment:)** Selection into the course ("selected into the course on the basis of ability and motivation," p. 196) restricts range but does not confound the within-course contrast.

### Limitations (tagged)
- Leadership measure ambiguity, stated by authors: "Caution is urged in the interpretation of the results obtained with this measure. Whereas the measure may reflect actual leadership behavior on the part of the instructor, it may be only an indicator of nonveridical trainee perceptions of the instructors' behavior." (p. 196); "The fact that the items were all highly intercorrelated blurs the conceptual meaning of the scale and might mean that it merely measures trainee satisfaction with instructor." (p. 196); "future research should employ measures more unequivocally interpretable as indicators of superiors' leadership behavior." (p. 198) **[imperfect measure] [no model of mechanism]**
- Generalization, stated by authors: "further replication is now needed to determine whether or not the present results generalize to other settings and to other interpersonal relationships of interest to organizational psychologists." (p. 197) **[single system]**
- Restricted manipulation (ethics): "We abstained from negative expectancy induction, for example, categorizing some trainees as having low CP, and used instead the weaker high, regular, and unknown categories." (p. 197)
- **The outcome was deliberately decoupled from selection.** Ethics step (d): "As an exception, the base commander agreed that grades and instructor evaluations in this course would not determine these trainees' admission to the next course for which they were slated" (p. 197). Step (c): the base commander committed "to interview personally every soldier who failed the course at any stage and to investigate the circumstances [...] in order to ascertain that the failure in no way resulted from the expectancy induction" (p. 197). **[outcome is not selection]** **(Assistant judgment:)** This is a quotable hinge for the dissertation: the one military Pygmalion experiment had to switch off the very selection consequence the dissertation studies.
- **(Assistant judgment:)** 105 trainees nested in 4 instructor groups; the ANOVA treats trainees as independent, so effective sample for instructor-level variation is 4. **[small data]** The eta² of .73 is unusually large for a field expectancy manipulation; later management-Pygmalion meta-analyses (e.g., McNatt 2000 — not in this source set; verify before citing) report smaller averages.
- **(Assistant judgment:)** Single course, all-male, one army, one 15-week cycle. **[single system]**

### Channel and source (Assistant judgment)
- **Channel:** DEVELOPMENT (trainees actually learned more on blind-scored tests) driven by an EVALUATION input (the organization's prior label of "command potential"). The authors' own "self-perpetuating" argument (pp. 197–198) points toward a cumulative-advantage path into SELECTION, but the study blocked that path by design.
- **Source:** ORGANIZATION (the label came from "the army" via superiors; the mediator is the superior's leadership). Peers are present (trainees in all conditions "were mixed together in each instructor's group," p. 198) but not studied as a channel.

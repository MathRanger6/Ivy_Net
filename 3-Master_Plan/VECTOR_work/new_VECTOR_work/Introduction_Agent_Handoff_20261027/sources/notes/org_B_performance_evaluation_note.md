## 1. Groysberg, Lee & Nanda (2008) — portability of star security analysts' performance

**Citation.** Groysberg, Boris, Linda-Eling Lee, and Ashish Nanda. 2008. "Can They Take It With Them? The Portability of Star Knowledge Workers' Performance." *Management Science* 54(7): 1213–1230. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1070.0809. (JSTOR copy: printed p. N = PDF p. N − 1211; e.g., p. 1213 = PDF p. 2.)

**Text-layer note.** The JSTOR text layer is an OCR layer with interleaved columns. Printed p. 1224 (PDF p. 13) could not be read from the text layer, so it was OCR'd with tesseract (250 dpi) and the quotes from that page below are tesseract output checked against the table numbers.

**Data / setting.** United States, Wall Street sell-side research. Stars = analysts ranked in *Institutional Investor*'s (II) annual All-America Research Team poll, 1988–1996: "799 equity analysts and 254 fixed-income analysts across 78 firms over a period of nine years" (p. 1214); 4,200 analyst-years (3,514 equity, 686 fixed income) (p. 1219). Moves: 546 analyst-year moves, of which 366 (by 316 analysts) were "switches to competitors' research departments"; average annual turnover 11.9%; 100 of 366 moves were team moves ("lift outs") (p. 1219). Event study: 111 exit and 101 hiring announcements after exclusions (p. 1221). Unit: analyst-year.

**Outcome = a relative ranking by evaluators.** II asks buy-side clients to rate analysts; "In each industry sector, the four analysts with the highest aggregate ratings are ranked first, second, third, and runner-up. [...] In 1996, less than 3% of all U.S. analysts were ranked." (p. 1219) Ranking matters inside the firm: "The II survey constitutes a core component of the internal performance evaluation for security analysts at every investment bank." and "Within the firm, being ranked by II wins an analyst enhanced credibility, greater power, and substantially higher compensation" (p. 1219). The dependent variable has "five levels [...]: the first, second, third, and runner-up ranks and unranked" (p. 1220).

**Design.** Movers vs. stayers ("control group") in panel ordered probit models of Rank at t+1 through t+5, with analyst type, firm tenure, experience, star tenure, current rank, firm, sector, and year dummies; robust standard errors clustered by analyst (pp. 1220–1221). Firm capability = bulge-bracket (six large banks) vs. non-bulge; moves coded weaker / comparable / better (p. 1220). Team move = "moving in teams" if the analyst moves "with other professionals (junior and senior research analysts, institutional salespeople, or traders who specialize in the same sector)" (p. 1220). Stock-market event study of hiring announcements, window −1 to +1 days (p. 1221).

**Main findings (verbatim).**

> "Star analysts who switched employers experienced an immediate decline in performance that persisted for at least five years. This decline was most pronounced among star analysts who moved to firms with lesser capabilities and those who moved solo, without other team members." (abstract, p. 1213) [text layer garbled in the first lines of the abstract; wording taken from a tesseract OCR of PDF p. 2.]

> "For example, for a ranked analyst who does not move, the probability of being ranked first in year t+1 is 0.106. If a ranked analyst changes employers, the predicted probability of being ranked first in year t+1 declines to 0.056 (=0.106−0.050)." (p. 1222)

- Analyst move coefficients (Table 2, p. 1223): −0.340 (t+1, p < .01), −0.263 (t+2), −0.229 (t+3), −0.218 (t+4), −0.263 (t+5). "Even after five years at a new firm, ranked analysts who switched employers underperform ranked analysts who stayed put. Thus, H1 is supported." (p. 1222)

> "The findings suggest that star analysts who move between two comparable firms (firms of equivalent capabilities) also exhibit a drop in performance but only for two years. Because the origin and destination firms have similar capabilities, differences in firm capability cannot account for this drop in performance." (p. 1222)

> "Ranked analysts who move solo show an immediate decline in performance that persists for at least five years (see Table 3). Switching firms has no significant effect, by contrast, on the performance of ranked analysts who move with teammates. [...] For example, the probability of achieving the first rank in year t+1 is only 0.053 (=0.106 − 0.061) for analysts who changed firms solo, but 0.100 (=0.106 − 0.006) for those who switched employers with teammates" (p. 1224; tesseract OCR)

- Firm capability (Table 3, p. 1224): moving to a weaker firm −0.338 (t+1) to −0.590 (t+4), N = 90 at t+1; comparable −0.409*** at t+1, n.s. by t+3, N = 209; better firm n.s. throughout, N = 67. "If an analyst switches to a firm with better capabilities, for example, the probability of achieving the first rank in year t+1 is 0.083 (=0.106 − 0.023), compared with a probability of 0.058 (=0.106 − 0.048) for an analyst who moves to a firm with worse capabilities." (p. 1224; OCR)

> "The performance of an outstanding worker is not owned by the worker alone; it is a property of the worker/firm combination, and encompasses firm-specific human capital embedded in colleague relationships and firm capabilities." (p. 1226)

- Hiring firm value: hiring firms show "significant negative abnormal return (−0.74%)", "a loss of about $24 million for the acquiring firm"; by direction −1.67% (to weaker), −0.63% (comparable), −0.26% (better) (p. 1225).

**Mechanism / model.** No formal model. The mechanism is verbal: firm-specific human capital (Becker), colleague relationships, and firm capabilities (pp. 1214–1217). The ordered probit is a statistical model of rank, not a model of how colleagues produce performance. Authors on what is unmeasured: "Although our data do not pinpoint the specific components of firm capability that help mitigate performance decline" (p. 1226); "This study has determined that colleague relationships constitute an important firm-specific resource [...]" but does not examine "the structure or the content of such ties" (p. 1227). Team move is binary: "we are not measuring how many colleagues moved. We only measure whether any colleagues moved." (footnote 6, p. 1224; OCR)

**Separating sorting from effects.** Authors acknowledge selection into moving: "workers do not move at random. Changing employers is endogenous if the decision to do so correlates with unobservables that affect performance." Their method "takes into account the self-selection process by which workers become movers or stayers" (p. 1218), but the selection correction is in the online e-companion, which is not in this PDF (so not verified here). Within the paper, sorting is handled by controls (current rank, star tenure, firm/sector/year dummies) and the comparable-firm contrast. Selection on who is hired, and which destination firm, is not modeled in the main text. (Assistant judgment: team moves are also chosen, so the solo vs. team contrast may partly reflect who can bring a team.)

**Limitations (tagged).**

- [single system] "Our results are based on a sample of star analysts and may not be generalizable to other knowledge workers or to star employees in other professions." "Our findings need to be replicated in other settings to determine their generalizability." (p. 1227)
- [imperfect measure] Performance is the II poll rank (a client vote), capability is a bulge / non-bulge binary, and team move is binary (fn. 6, p. 1224). Only stars are observed; unranked analysts are not in the sample. "Disentangling the effects of firm status and firm capability on performance would also be a valuable addition" (p. 1227).
- [no model of mechanism] No model of how colleagues or firm resources produce performance (p. 1226–1227 quotes above).
- [small data] Some cells are small: moves to better firms N = 67 at t+1 and 35 at t+5; to weaker firms 90 at t+1 and 32 at t+5 (Table 3, p. 1224).
- [outcome is not selection] Partly applies. (Assistant judgment) The outcome *is* a rank among competitors for four slots per sector, but the paper treats it as a performance measure, and the mover's position relative to the other contenders in the sector is not modeled.

**Channel / source (Assistant judgment).** Main channel: **development / production**. A star's output depends on colleagues and the firm, so "individual performance" is partly a property of the environment. Source: **organization** (firm capabilities) and **peers** (colleagues, teams). Secondary channel: **evaluation**. The performance measure is a relative ranking by outside evaluators that is also the firm's internal evaluation, so any environment effect on output reaches advancement through a contested ranking. Useful for the dissertation as evidence that performance is not portable, which undermines treating a person's score as fixed apart from their environment.

## 2. Huckman & Pisano (2006) — firm specificity of cardiac surgeons' performance

**Citation.** Huckman, Robert S., and Gary P. Pisano. 2006. "The Firm Specificity of Individual Performance: Evidence from Cardiac Surgery." *Management Science* 52(4): 473–488. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1050.0464. (JSTOR copy: printed p. N = PDF p. N − 471; e.g., p. 473 = PDF p. 2.)

**Text-layer note.** Printed p. 482 (PDF p. 11) has interleaved columns in the text layer; quotes from that page are from a tesseract OCR of the page image. All other pages read cleanly with `pdftotext -layout`.

**Data / setting.** Pennsylvania (US), every coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) patient in 1994–1995, from the Pennsylvania Health Care Cost Containment Council (PHC4): "38,577 procedures performed by 203 surgeons operating at 43 hospitals" (p. 477). Regression samples 28,173–34,173 cases after lags (fn. 6, p. 477). Unit: patient, nested in surgeon × hospital × quarter. Key variation: surgeons who "split" cases across hospitals: "roughly 30% of the cardiac surgeons in the sample were splitters during the average quarter" (p. 478). Outcome: in-hospital death; mean 3.1% (Table 2, p. 481).

**Design.** Patient-level logistic regression of death on the surgeon's prior-quarter volume *at this hospital* (HOSPCASE) and *at other hospitals* (OTHCASE), with prior-quarter surgeon and hospital risk-adjusted mortality (RAMR), patient clinical risk factors, quarter fixed effects, standard errors clustered by surgeon (eqs. 3–4, p. 479). The test: "if the performance of surgeons were not at all firm specific, then one would expect volume at Hospitals A and B to have identical effects on the surgeon's outcomes at Hospital A" (p. 479). Advantage claimed: they "examine a given worker across several firms at a single time, thereby avoiding the need to control for factors, such as increased experience or changes in required job tasks" (p. 474).

**Main findings (verbatim).**

> "Using patient mortality as an outcome measure, we find that the quality of a surgeon's performance at a given hospital improves significantly with increases in his or her recent procedure volume at that hospital but does not significantly improve with increases in his or her volume at other hospitals. Our findings suggest that surgeon performance is not fully portable across hospitals (i.e., some portion of performance is firm specific)." (abstract, p. 473)

> "an increase of one case per quarter is associated with a 0.018 percentage point decline in the mortality rate. For OTHCASE, a similar increase of one case is correlated with a decline of only 0.001 percentage points that, again, is insignificant at conventional levels." (p. 482; tesseract OCR)

> "The strongest evidence of firm specificity in performance is that the coefficient on HOSPCASE is not only significantly different from zero, but is also significantly different from the OTHCASE coefficient at the 1% level." (p. 482; tesseract OCR)

- Table 4 (p. 483): HOSPCASE −0.0103 (SE 0.0023) vs OTHCASE −0.0003 (SE 0.0037), N = 34,173; marginal effects −0.018% vs −0.001% at a predicted mortality of 1.76%. (Assistant computation: one extra local case per quarter ≈ 1% relative reduction in predicted mortality.) Splitters only (Table 5, p. 484): N = 10,150, HOSPCASE −0.0112 vs OTHCASE −0.0007; difference significant at 6% (11% with six-month quality lags).

> "This finding points to the importance of familiarity—above and beyond influence—in explaining the hospital-specific performance of surgeons." (p. 484)

> "Regardless of whether our results are due to matching or increased familiarity over time, they still highlight the key point that the performance of pivotal freelancers does depend on organizational context." (p. 486)

**Peers / competition inside the hospital.** Influence is defined relative to the other surgeons at the same hospital: a high-volume surgeon may "command preferential access—relative to other cardiac surgeons—to the hospital's resources" (p. 476); "To the extent that cardiac surgeons compete for resources in terms of operating room scheduling or team members, they thus compete against one another." (p. 480) Influence proxies: SHARE (surgeon's share of hospital CABG volume) and CITATIONS (share of the hospital's CABG citations). Both have the predicted negative sign but are insignificant, and the HOSPCASE–OTHCASE gap survives (Table 6, p. 485; text p. 484).

**Mechanism / model.** No formal model. Verbal mechanisms are (1) familiarity with hospital-specific assets and teams (tacit knowledge) and (2) influence over hospital resources relative to other surgeons (pp. 475–476). The regressions test which proxy carries the effect. The authors call the mechanism evidence "preliminary" (abstract, p. 473) and say "The specific nature of these key organizational assets—which may be specific employees, team structures, or operating routines—represents an area for further research." (p. 485)

**Separating sorting from effects.**
- Patient sorting to better surgeons: handled by PHC4 risk adjustment, because "higher quality surgeons may attract patients with more severe forms of coronary disease" (p. 477).
- Reverse causality (bad outcomes cause less volume): volumes are lagged one quarter "To reduce concerns regarding the direction of causality" (p. 479). Footnote 16 regresses the change in HOSPCASE on the change in surgeon RAMR; "the coefficient on the change in mortality in this regression is very low (−0.30) and is insignificant." (fn. 16, pp. 482–483)
- Why surgeons split is not observed: "The PHC4 data does not provide information as to which, if any, of the above factors explain splitting behavior. As such, this paper focuses on the effects, rather than the causes, of this activity." (p. 478)
- Match quality vs. learning is not separated: cumulative volume "would help us separate volume-outcome effects due to increased familiarity over time from similar effects due to an idiosyncratically good match between a surgeon and a hospital. While pure matching could theoretically explain our result, practical examples of pure matching are rare in this setting." (p. 486)

**Limitations (tagged).**
- [single system] "our results are based on only one type of freelance worker. It is, therefore, possible that the pattern of organization-specific performance that we identify for cardiac surgeons may not be as strong in other settings." (p. 485) Also one state.
- [small data] Two years only; "our analysis might benefit from a deeper panel with additional years of data for each surgeon and hospital" (p. 486). The splitters-only sample is about 10,000 cases, and significance falls to 6–11% (p. 483).
- [imperfect measure] Recent (quarterly) volume rather than cumulative experience (p. 486). Data are identified only by calendar quarter, so within-quarter splitting is assumed even (fn. 11, p. 478). Influence proxies "would benefit from additional proxies for surgeon influence" (p. 486); SHARE "may be correlated with our measure of familiarity (HOSPCASE)" (p. 480).
- [no model of mechanism] Familiarity vs. influence vs. match quality are only partly separated (quotes above).
- [outcome is not selection] The outcome is patient mortality, a performance measure. Nobody is selected into a slot. (Assistant judgment: the influence story is a within-hospital competition for scarce operating rooms and teams, but it is proxied, not studied as an allocation.)

**Channel / source (Assistant judgment).** Main channel: **development / production**. A surgeon's skill is partly specific to the hospital's teams and assets. Source: **organization** (hospital assets, staff) and **peers** in two forms: non-surgeon team members (familiarity) and competing surgeons at the same hospital (influence over scarce resources). Relevance to evaluation: because public "report cards" rate surgeons, a surgeon's measured quality depends on where they operate, so an evaluation that treats performance as a personal trait mixes person and organization. The authors also note that surgeons cannot easily see this themselves: "a surgeon who does a large number of procedures in a given period may not be able to distinguish a 1% mortality rate from a 3% rate." (p. 485)

## 3. Scullen, Bergey & Aiman-Smith (2005) — forced distribution ("rank and yank") simulation

**Citation.** Scullen, Steven E., Paul K. Bergey, and Lynda Aiman-Smith. 2005. "Forced Distribution Rating Systems and the Improvement of Workforce Potential: A Baseline Simulation." *Personnel Psychology* 58(1): 1–32. DOI 10.1111/j.1744-6570.2005.00361.x. (Wiley PDF: printed page = PDF page.)

**Data / setting.** No empirical data. A computer simulation (Visual Basic in Excel, p. 7) of hypothetical US-style firms: "The number of organizations in each cell was 100. The workforce in each organization consisted of 100 workers, organized as 10 groups of 10 workers." Each run lasts 30 years (p. 6). Design: 2 (percentage fired: 5%, 10%) × 2 (rating reliability: .50, .70) × 2 (selection validity: .30, .50) × 2 (selection ratio: .10, .33) × 5 (voluntary turnover: 0–20%) = 80 scenarios (p. 7). Outcome: "workforce performance potential, defined as the average performance potential among a firm's employees in a given year" (p. 7).

**Design (the model).** Each person has a fixed true Potential ~ N(0,1): "The Potential assigned to an individual applicant remained with that person for the duration of the simulation." (p. 7) Hiring: the applicant with the highest AppScore is hired, where (Eq. 1, p. 7; the square-root signs are lost in the text layer and restored here) AppScore = Validity·Potential + √(1 − Validity²)·Error. Annual rating (Eq. 3, p. 9): Rating = Potential·√Rxx + Error·√(1 − Rxx), with error "redrawn every year" (p. 9). Voluntary turnover follows a Poisson schedule and is random with respect to potential (p. 9). Then "Employees were then ranked within their workgroups according to their Ratings. Note that the employees with the lowest Ratings were not necessarily those with the lowest Potential values." (p. 10) The lowest-rated person in each group of 10 is fired (10% condition) and replaced from a fresh applicant pool (p. 10).

**Relative evaluation against coworkers (explicit).** The firing rule ranks each person against the other nine people in their own workgroup, not against the whole firm:

> "We assumed in this simulation that the supervisor of each work group would be required to apply the percentage fired to his or her group. [...] We point out that this procedure will not, in general, result in exactly the same set of people being fired as would firing the bottom 10% (i.e., 10 people) of the entire workforce of 100 workers. It is likely that there will be cases where workers who are fired from the better groups would have been retained if they had worked in some of the poorer groups. Similarly, some of the workers who retain their jobs in poorer groups would have been fired if they had been in better groups." (p. 4)

Critics' version of the same point: FDRS "often penalize good employees who happen to work on good teams and reward mediocre employees who are members of poor teams" (p. 2). The authors note their design "captures that phenomenon" (p. 5) but do not report how much of the firing error comes from group composition versus rating noise. (Assistant judgment: this is the cleanest statement in the set that within-group ranking makes a person's fate depend on who their coworkers are, even when coworkers have no effect on the person's true ability.)

**Main findings (verbatim).**

> "Results suggest that a FDRS could lead to noticeable improvement in workforce potential, that most of the improvement should be expected to occur over the first several years, and that improvement is largely a function of the percentage of workers to be fired and the level of voluntary turnover." (abstract, p. 1)

> "The average improvement over the duration of the simulation was 0.44 SD units (range = 0.19 to 1.02 SDs)." (p. 11)

> "Under those conditions (i.e., in Scenario 79), average potential rose by the end of the simulation to 1.14 SDs, an increase of 0.37 SD units, or about 48%." (p. 17) [Scenario 79 = reliability .70, validity .50, selection ratio .10, 10% fired, 20% turnover.]

> "Annual improvement averaged approximately 16% for the first 2 years, but fell quickly to about 2% in year 6 and 1% in year 10. After Year 20, there was no improvement." (p. 20)

> "When voluntary turnover was 20%, the average firm experienced its first decrease at about 3.5 years when 5% were fired annually, and at about 4.5 years when 10% were fired." (p. 24)

- Gains by firing condition, regardless of validity and selection ratio (p. 17): about 0.40 SD (10% fired, reliability .70), 0.34 (10%, .50), 0.22 (5%, .70), 0.20 (5%, .50). "These results suggest that the amount of expected improvement from a FDRS is more a function of the firing variables (percentage fired and reliability) than of the hiring variables (validity and selection ratio)." (p. 17)

**Role of rater reliability.** Reliability matters, but less than the share fired: "The percentage fired had more effect than did reliability" (p. 17). At year 30, correlations with workforce potential were percentage fired .51, turnover −.51, selection ratio −.50, validity .39, "and reliability was only .12" (p. 19). Interaction: "poor reliability led to more firing mistakes when larger numbers of people were being fired" (p. 18). Reliability values come from a meta-analysis: "the mean interrater correlation for supervisory ratings of job performance is .52 [...] we included values of .50 and .70" (pp. 5–6).

**Role of turnover.** Random voluntary turnover works against the system as the workforce improves: "as a firm's workforce improves, the average potential of the fired employees rises and, therefore, the gain that can be realized by replacing those employees falls. [...] as the workforce improves, random instances of voluntary turnover will tend to result in more significant losses of worker potential." (p. 25) Firms reached about 0.11 SD higher potential with 10% vs. 20% voluntary turnover (p. 21). Adding a retention program to the FDRS + selection bundle *lowered* gains (29% vs. 36% in year 1) because fewer new, better hires came in (pp. 23, 25).

**Mechanism / model — fit to data?** Formal simulation model: yes. Fit or calibrated to data: **no**. Parameters are set to "research-based values" (p. 2) taken from published averages: interrater reliability from Viswesvaran et al. (1996), validities from Schmidt & Hunter (1998), selection ratios from Kirnan et al. (1989), turnover from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (pp. 5–6). Simulated outputs are never compared to any firm's observed workforce or ratings. The authors call it "a baseline" that "holds the many complicating factors constant and models what might be expected to happen if the ripple effects were neutral" (p. 26), citing "not having data to indicate how certain effects should be modeled" (p. 26).

**Separating sorting from effects.** Not applicable in the usual sense: in the model, potential is fixed and coworkers have no effect on anyone's potential or rating, so all change is composition (who is fired and hired). (Assistant judgment: the model has selection and evaluation but no development channel and no peer effect on ratings.)

**Limitations (tagged).**
- [model not fit to data] "Like all simulations, ours is an incomplete representation of reality. [...] We have tried to choose representative values for the variables we included in this study but must recognize that the external validity of our results will be a function of how well we succeeded in that." (pp. 28–29)
- [no model of mechanism] Morale, cooperation, and recruiting effects are left out: "our study does not include factors (e.g., effects on employee morale) that are likely to affect individual and firm performance" (p. 26). Teamwork: "If employees know they will be individually ranked, they might be motivated to compete more and cooperate less with each other" (p. 28), but this is not modeled.
- [imperfect measure] Rater effects are not modeled: "raters themselves are a significant source of variance in performance ratings. This problem is especially pronounced in situations in which ratees who come from different workgroups, and are supervised by different managers, must be ranked against one another." (p. 29) Turnover is set uncorrelated with performance "because the relationship is negative under some circumstances and positive under others" (p. 27).
- [single system] One organizational form (100 workers, groups of 10, no movement between groups): "In this study, all workers stayed in the workgroups to which they were originally assigned." (p. 29) Pooling groups before ranking is not modeled (p. 29).
- [outcome is not selection] Partly applies. The selection rule (fire the bottom 1 of 10) is modeled, but the outcome is firm-average true potential, not an individual's chance of being selected. "Our results should not be taken to mean that the workforce will necessarily perform at a particular level" (p. 26).

**Channel / source (Assistant judgment).** Channels: **evaluation** (noisy rating, reliability .50/.70) and **selection** (rank within group, remove bottom K, hire top applicant). This matches the dissertation's "score, then select top K" split: Eq. 3 is the score, and within-group ranking and firing is the select step. Source: **organization** (the rule, group size, firing quota) and, through the within-group ranking, **peers**: whether you are fired depends on who else is in your group of 10. No **development** channel, because potential is fixed for life. The paper is the closest to a formal score-then-select model in this set, but it is not fit to data and measures only the firm-level average, not who advances.

## 4. Staw & Hoang (1995) — sunk costs and NBA draft order

**Citation.** Staw, Barry M., and Ha Hoang. 1995. "Sunk Costs in the NBA: Why Draft Order Affects Playing Time and Survival in Professional Basketball." *Administrative Science Quarterly* 40(3): 474–494. DOI 10.2307/2393794. (Crossref lists only the first page, 474; the end page 494 is from the article itself. JSTOR copy: printed p. N = PDF p. N − 472; e.g., p. 474 = PDF p. 2.)

**Data / setting.** United States, National Basketball Association (NBA). "all players selected in the first two rounds of the 1980-1986 drafts", restricted to those who "received contracts and played at least two years in the NBA": N = 241 for playing time and trades (p. 478); N = 275 (played at least one year) for career survival, of whom "184 of the 275 players in this sample were eventually cut from the league" (p. 482). Careers followed to the 1990–1991 season. Unit: player-season. Sources: *Official NBA Encyclopedia* and *Sports Encyclopedia* (p. 478).

**Design.** Draft number (pick order) is the measure of sunk cost: "the order in which players are taken in the draft represents an expenditure teams make to attain the services of a particular player. Salary contracts extended to players are roughly in line with their draft order" (p. 476). Three analyses: (1) OLS of minutes played in years 2–5 on the prior year's performance (factor indices "scoring," "toughness," "quickness," per minute and standardized by position), injury, trade, team win percentage, position, and draft number (pp. 478–481); (2) event-history (exponential hazard) model of being cut from the league (pp. 482–484); (3) hazard of the first trade (pp. 485–486). Null hypothesis: "after controlling for productivity on the court, draft order will add little to the prediction of playing time in the NBA." (p. 477)

**Main findings (verbatim).**

> "Results showed that teams granted more playing time to their most highly drafted players and retained them longer, even after controlling for players' on-court performance, injuries, trade status, and position played." (abstract, p. 474)

> "The regressions showed that every increment in the draft number decreased playing time by as much as 23 minutes in the second year (β1 = −22.77, p < .001, one-tailed test). [β restored; the text layer drops Greek letters] Likewise, being taken in the second rather than the first round of the draft meant 552 minutes less playing time during a player's second year in the NBA." (p. 481) Draft number remained significant through year 5 (β = −16.21, −16.46, −13.77 in years 3–5; N = 241, 211, 187, 165) (Table 2 and text, p. 481).

> "Using this method, every increment in the draft number raised the hazard rate function by 3 percent. A first-round draft pick would therefore stay in the league approximately 3.3 years longer than a player drafted in the second round." (p. 484)

> "The hazard rate function was raised by 3 percent with every increment in the draft number. Moving from the first to the second round of the draft therefore increased a player's chances of being traded by 72 percent." (p. 486)

- Performance does matter too: a one-SD rise in scoring "decreased the hazard rate function by 37 percent, or added approximately 4.6 years to a player's career" (p. 484).

> "Teams did play their most expensive players more and hang on to them longer than players they expended fewer resources to obtain. Such observations would be obvious, of course, if it were not for the fact that the effects were always beyond those explained by performance variables." (p. 489)

**Peers / team context.** Team quality is entered as a competition-for-minutes control: "Being drafted by a winning team, composed of other quality players, may make it more difficult for the incoming player to receive significant playing time." (p. 480) Its coefficient on minutes is small and insignificant (−1.37, −6.15, 6.69, .93; Table 2, p. 481), but winning teams trade players less: "by playing on a team that increased its win percentage by 10 percent, a player would reduce his risk of being traded by 20 percent." (p. 486)

**Mechanism / model.** No formal model of the decision. The mechanism is the psychology of sunk costs and escalation of commitment. The authors say several processes may be involved and that the study does not choose among them: "The main purpose of the present study was to validate the sunk-cost effect in a natural organizational setting, not to sort out the competing theoretical processes that may underlie this effect." (p. 491) Candidate processes: judgment error, mental budgeting, framing, self-justification, public commitment (pp. 490–492). The draft is described as "a very visible public commitment" (p. 492). Statistical models only: OLS and exponential hazard ("We also analyzed the data using the Weibull model [...] The results were not significantly different," fn. 4, p. 483).

**Separating sorting from effects (alternative explanations).** The key alternative is that draft order carries real information about future ability:
> "The results showed draft number to be a significant predictor of subsequent performance during players' second and third years in the NBA, but not for their fourth or fifth years. Thus, while draft order does appear to contain some useful information on players' early performance, it is not a significant predictor over longer periods of time." (p. 488)

Development alternative: performance in years 2–4 rises with prior playing time, "But none of the interactions of draft choice and playing time proved to be significant predictors of subsequent performance." (p. 489) Salary cap and fan appeal are argued against verbally (pp. 487–488). The effect persists after trades: "sunk costs can actually be passed from one team to another, with each transaction being influenced by an overestimate of the performance of the traded player." (p. 491) (Assistant judgment: the performance controls are box-score indices from the prior year, so unmeasured skills such as defense or the coach's private information could still correlate with draft order. The draft-predicts-performance test helps but does not remove this concern.)

**Limitations (tagged).**
- [small data] 241 / 275 players from seven draft classes.
- [single system] One league, 1980s. The authors frame it as one of the first field tests: "The study does not resolve all the problems of the escalation area" (p. 475).
- [imperfect measure] Performance = per-minute box-score indices. The authors themselves note team-oriented skills are poorly rewarded: coaches "are actually more likely to use individual scoring in their important personnel decisions" (p. 487). Survivor sample: "Because players left the league over time, there were changes in our sample across the five periods. [...] our population became increasingly elite over time. Due to the restriction in range [...]" (pp. 481–482). One-tailed tests throughout.
- [no model of mechanism] No model of the coach's or general manager's decision. Processes are not separated (p. 491 quote).
- [outcome is not selection] Does **not** apply: minutes, roster survival, and trades are allocations of scarce positions. (Assistant judgment: this is the only study in the set whose outcomes are selections in the dissertation's sense.)

**Channel / source (Assistant judgment).** Channel: **selection**, with an **evaluation** bias inside it. A past selection decision (the draft) shifts later selections (minutes, retention) net of measured performance, so the score used to select is not only current performance. Source: **organization** (the team's own past investment, management's public commitment). Peers enter only through team quality as a competition-for-minutes control. For the dissertation: selection is path-dependent. Being selected early makes later selection more likely, independent of measured output, which is one way an environment can affect advancement without changing what the person becomes. There is also a development caveat: playing time raises later performance (p. 489), so biased allocation can make itself partly true.

## 5. Bothner, Kang & Stuart (2007) — competitive crowding and risk taking in NASCAR

**Citation.** Bothner, Matthew S., Jeong-han Kang, and Toby E. Stuart. 2007. "Competitive Crowding and Risk Taking in a Tournament: Evidence from NASCAR Racing." *Administrative Science Quarterly* 52(2): 208–247. DOI 10.2189/asqu.52.2.208. (Publisher PDF without a cover page: printed p. N = PDF p. N − 207; e.g., p. 208 = PDF p. 1.)

**Data / setting.** United States, NASCAR Winston Cup Series, 1990–2003: a season-long tournament of weekly races, "the number having risen almost linearly with time from 29 races in the 1992 season to 36 races in the 2001 season, each typically with 43 drivers" (p. 216). Unit: driver-race; N = 18,617 in the descriptive table (Table 1, p. 222), 15,454–17,846 in the models (Table 2, p. 226). Outcome: whether the driver failed to finish because of an accident; mean .087 (Table 1, p. 222). "the rate of crashing, which is observable, is treated as a proxy for the unobservable tendency of a competitor to take risks." (p. 211)

**Tournament structure.** "rivals in tournaments receive resources or rewards as a function of their relative ranking in a performance hierarchy rather than on the basis of their absolute performance. [...] Second, competition in a tournament is typically zero-sum: a lower-ranked rival's significant advancement on the continuous metric(s) on which the ranking is established necessarily results in an adjacent, higher-ranked actor's loss." (pp. 208–209) Points per race are piecewise linear (winner 175, 43rd place 34, up to 10 bonus points), while season prize money is "a highly nonlinear (convex) payoff schedule" in season rank (pp. 216–217).

**Definition of crowding.** Before each race, the "striking distance" S_t is "the difference between the points accruing to the driver who finishes last and the maximum number of points any driver can collect" (usually 151 with 43 drivers) (p. 219). Crowding from below CB_it counts lower-ranked drivers j with 0 ≤ P_i,t−1 − P_j,t−1 < S_t:

> "Consequently, CB_it captures the number of lower-ranked drivers capable of passing driver i in the points distribution in race t. Contestant j is theoretically able to surpass i if i places last, and j receives the maximum number of points. This information is transparent to driver i before race t and therefore serves as an appropriate metric of the risk of loss in rank." (p. 219)

Crowding from above CA_it is the mirror count of higher-ranked drivers whom i could pass, i.e., "the opportunity of driver i to surpass rivals ranked higher in the points distribution" (p. 219). Worked example: Kyle Petty, 23rd before race 25 of 2002, had CB = 3 and CA = 5 (p. 220). Rank change RC = number of drivers whose rank changed in the prior race (p. 220).

**Design.** Logit of crash on CB and CA, controlling for prior accidents, prior non-crash failures, multicar team, owner's rank, pole (starting) position, a full set of points-rank dummies, experience, age, race number and its square, race and track length, year dummies; then driver fixed effects (model 4) and race fixed effects for "over four hundred races" (model 5) (pp. 221–225, 228).

**Main findings (verbatim).**

> "Findings show that drivers crash their vehicles with greater frequency when their positions are increasingly at risk of displacement by their nearby, lower-ranked counterparts; the effect of crowding from below exceeds that of crowding from above; and the effect of crowding by lower-ranked contestants is greatest when there is relatively little race-to-race change in the rank ordering of drivers." (abstract, p. 208)

> "With each standard deviation increase in the count of lower-ranked rivals in position to outstrip a chosen driver in a given race, the odds of crashing go up by 19 percent." (p. 227)

- Coefficients on crowding from below (Table 2, p. 226): .0203 (SE .0084) model 2; .0181 (.0085) with CA = −.0102 (.0080) model 3; .0190 (.0091) with driver fixed effects, model 4; .0340 (.0120) with driver and race fixed effects, model 5. The CB − CA difference "significantly exceeds zero at the .01 level of confidence (p < .0035, one-tailed test)" (p. 228).

> "Using the parameters from model 8, a standard-deviation increase in crowding from below leads to a 70 percent increase in the hazard of crashing when rank change is at a standard deviation below its mean value; by contrast, the same shift only leads to a 29 percent increase in the rate when the rank change covariate is a standard deviation above its mean value." (p. 233)

> "one of our primary contributions has been to show that the conduct of actors in a tournament is shaped not only by their relative positions but also by the level of crowding around those positions." (p. 233)

- Rank itself: "a given driver will crash more frequently when he is positioned toward the bottom of the hierarchy than when he is near the top" (p. 225). Organization: "the odds of crashing rise by 1.5 percent for each drop in the rank of a driver's owner" (p. 225; not significant once driver fixed effects are added, Table 2).

**Mechanism / model — fit to data?** Yes, a formal model (Appendix, pp. 239–247): a piecewise subjective value function with losses weighted more than gains, V_L(x) = −log_α(1 − x) and V_G(x) = log_β(1 + x) with 1 < α < β, and arctangent weights on the probability of losing or gaining rank as functions of crowding cb = CB/43 and ca = CA/43. **Not fit to data.** The parameters are set for illustration: "we use the natural log in equation (A1) and the common log in equation (A2), thus setting α = e and β = 10" (p. 239). The appendix only checks the signs of the derivatives under these values. It is presented as a restatement of the verbal theory: "Our objective is deliberately focused. We proceed in the spirit of recent work in organizational sociology whose goal is to express with greater precision mechanisms previously posited or implied in the course of verbal theory construction" (p. 239). One by-product: a positive crowding-from-above effect requires a "confidence parameter" between about 1.44 and 1.645, i.e., "the typical driver must generate a non-trivial degree of overconfidence in the face of crowding from above for it to affect the hazard of crashing positively." (p. 245) [Greek letters are dropped in the PDF text layer and restored here from context.] The empirical logit coefficients are not used to estimate α, β, or the confidence parameter.

**Separating sorting from effects.**
- Stable driver traits: "we guarded against the possibility that crowding from below correlates with unobserved driver ability by including in model 4 a separate dummy variable for each driver." (p. 228) Footnote 5: about 121.8 observations per driver, and a conditional fixed-effects estimator gives CB = .0186 (p < .039) (p. 228).
- Race-level shocks (weather, rules, the spread of the points distribution): race fixed effects in model 5 (p. 228).
- Neighbors' ability and neighbors causing the crash (the "sabotage" alternative): controls for career points per race of drivers above and below, and their rank-weighted, pole-weighted, and conditional accident rates (pp. 230–232). "Most important, our main measure of interest, crowding from below, retains its significance with these added controls." (p. 232)
- Not available: what happens within a race. "Unfortunately, the data available to us do not provide information about the happenings in any given race" (pp. 228–229). They proxy it with the finishing positions of the crowding drivers (model 6, p. 229).

**Limitations (tagged).**
- [single system] "one limitation of the findings may be that NASCAR is an idiosyncratic context and that the findings we report may not generalize to other competitive arenas. Needless to say, no other organization operates according to the rules and incentive schemes NASCAR has implemented." (p. 234)
- [imperfect measure] Crash = proxy for risk taking (p. 211); no in-race data (pp. 228–229). Rankings here are public and objective; elsewhere "there may well be disagreement among evaluators and rivals about the main criteria by which rivals are to be judged and ranked. Such differences are especially likely whenever evaluators are free to bring their subjective inclinations to bear on their rankings of contestants" (p. 234).
- [model not fit to data] The Appendix model has illustrative parameter values and only sign checks (p. 239 quote above).
- [outcome is not selection] The outcome is a behavior (crashing), not who wins the final season ranks or prize money. Crashing presumably lowers a driver's rank, but that link is not estimated. Also, "the Winston Cup Series was not designed as an elimination tournament" (p. 234), so there is no fixed set of slots to survive into.
- Scope: findings may not hold where "positional changes are quite infrequent" (p. 234). The authors propose law-firm partnership races and investment-bank league tables as tests (pp. 235–236). The law-firm example is explicitly a promotion contest: more rivals poised to pass an associate means "his or her job security and likelihood of achieving partnership have declined" (p. 235).

**Channel / source (Assistant judgment).** Channel: the **selection** contest acting back on **behavior** (a behavioral response to the selection structure). This is not development, evaluation, or the select step itself. Under relative ranking, one's position is threatened by specific nearby competitors, and that changes conduct in ways that can hurt one's own performance. Source: **peers**, defined positionally as the competitors within striking distance in the ranking (not colleagues or friends), plus the **organization** through the tournament rules (convex payoffs, points schedule) and, weakly, owner resources. For the dissertation: this is the clearest evidence in the set that the *local* configuration of competitors' scores, not just one's own rank, matters for behavior in a ranked contest. It also offers a portable crowding measure (count of rivals within one period's maximum swing), which could be applied to any score-then-select-top-K setting.

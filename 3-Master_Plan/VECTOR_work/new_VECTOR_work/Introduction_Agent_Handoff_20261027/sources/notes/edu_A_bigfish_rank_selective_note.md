## ASSISTANT SYNTHESIS (my interpretation, not a quotation)

1. **The paradigm.** Davis (1966) framed the question as relative deprivation. At equal aptitude, a more selective college gives lower grades, and grades rather than school quality drive career aspirations: "big frogs in little ponds or little frogs in big ponds" (p. 21). Marsh (1987) named it the big-fish-little-pond effect (BFLPE): equally able students have lower academic self-concept, and also lower GPA, in high-ability schools.
2. **Self-concept replicates widely.** Marsh & Hau (2003) found the effect negative in all 26 countries (mean β = −.20; abstract only). Fang et al. (2018) pooled 33 studies to β = −0.28, but with I² = 99.78%.
3. **Ordinal rank reframes the paradigm as "same fish, different pond".** Murphy & Weinhardt (England, 2.25 million students), Elsner & Isphording (US AddHealth), Elsner, Isphording & Zölitz (random sections at a Dutch business school), and Denning et al. (Texas, linked to earnings) agree: at identical achievement, a higher local rank raises later scores, confidence, subject or major choice, college going, and earnings.
4. **Elite-school effects at the admission cutoff conflict.** In US exam schools, effects are near zero for scores and college placement (Abdulkadiroğlu et al. 2014; Dobbie & Fryer 2014, abstract only). In Romania they are small and positive (0.02–0.10 SD; Pop-Eleches & Urquiola 2013), and in Trinidad and Tobago large and positive (Jackson 2010). Rank papers offer lower rank as one reason for the US nulls (Murphy & Weinhardt WP p. 38; Denning et al. p. 1428), but none of the cutoff papers tests this.
5. **What remains open for selection among competitors for scarce slots:**
   - Almost every outcome is development (self-concept, scores, own choices), not a selection decision by an organization [outcome is not selection].
   - Elsner & Isphording explicitly raise admission on within-school rank (for example, top-10% rules) and cannot test it, because admission type is unobserved (p. 790).
   - Mechanisms are verbal or agnostic [no model of mechanism]. Murphy & Weinhardt derive one sign test from a model but do not fit it [model not fit to data].
   - Each estimate comes from one country or system [single system].
   - Early work relies on self-reports and proxies [imperfect measure: Davis's self-reported GPA and retrospective freshman plans; Marsh's self-reported GPA].
   - Some samples are small or sampled [small data: Elsner & Isphording, 13,645 students with rank computed from sampled cohorts; Marsh, 2,213 boys; Paulsen, 430 players].
   - Pop-Eleches & Urquiola alone show the **organization** responding to the pond, by sorting teachers within schools.

_Unread or partial sources:_ Marsh & Hau 2003 (no PDF; OpenAlex abstract only) and Dobbie & Fryer 2014 (library PDF is the online appendix; Crossref abstract only). Marsh 1987 and Murphy & Weinhardt 2020 are cited from pre-publication versions (an ERIC manuscript and NBER WP 24958), so their page numbers are **not** journal pages.

## 1. Davis (1966): The campus as a frog pond

**Citation.** Davis, James A. 1966. "The Campus as a Frog Pond: An Application of the Theory of Relative Deprivation to Career Decisions of College Men." *American Journal of Sociology* 72(1): 17–31. DOI 10.1086/224257. (The PDF is a 15-page JSTOR scan; PDF page 1 is printed page 17.)

**Data and setting.** United States. NORC survey of graduating college seniors (spring 1961) from 135 colleges and universities.

- p. 17: "the National Opinion Research Center (NORC) has been studying the careers of some 35,000 young men and women from 135 colleges and universities, a representative probability sample of persons receiving the bachelor's degree from an American college or university in spring, 1961."
- p. 20, Table 1 note: "it is necessary to weight up the original 33,782 questionnaires of a total of 56,664 to provide an unbiased estimate of the universe".
- **Measures.** GPA is self-reported. "School quality" is the average National Merit test score of entering freshmen at the school, grouped into levels I–IV. There is no individual pre-college aptitude score; Davis uses self-reported National Merit finalist or semifinalist status (about 3% of the sample) as a proxy.
- **Outcome.** Career preference at graduation for "high-performance" fields (physical, biological, and social sciences; humanities and fine arts; law; medicine), controlling for freshman preference. Freshman preference is collected retrospectively.
- The analyses are men only: "Women have been excluded for the good reason that the trends reported in the remainder of this paper do not hold among them." (p. 21)

**Design.** Cross-sectional survey with retrospective freshman measures. The analysis is cross-tabulation with Goodman–Kruskal gamma and partial gammas. Observational; there is no causal identification.

**Main findings (verbatim).**

1. Abstract, p. 17: "grade-point averages show stronger correlations with career choice than a measure of the intellectual caliber of the student body, although both are equally strongly related to tests of scholastic aptitude. The finding suggests the operation of "relative deprivation," that is, a tendency for students to evaluate their academic ability by comparison with their fellows on the same campus, not in terms of criteria which allow for school differences in ability level."
2. pp. 20–21: "when scholastic aptitude is controlled, the relationship between school quality and grade-point average will be negative. Among high-school graduates with a given level of scholastic aptitude, the more selective the college they attend, the lower their grades will be. The diversity of American colleges and universities means that students in the middle ranges of academic aptitude can choose to be big frogs in little ponds or little frogs in big ponds."
3. p. 21, among National Merit students (Table 3): "The values of γ (−.333 for men and −.433 for women) are both negative. Among the men, two-thirds of those graduated from Level IV schools had GPA's of B+ or better, while among those who were graduated from schools in Levels I and II, it is reversed—about two-thirds had less than a B+ average."
4. p. 23, effect sizes: "For the partials, the coefficient for GPA (+.311) is twice as large as that for school quality (+.151)."
5. p. 24, among male National Merit finalists (Table 7): "school quality is essentially independent of career choice, in contrast to the positive values seen in the previous tables and the positive γ of .264 for GPA."

**Mechanism or model.** Davis proposes a verbal mechanism: relative deprivation through local social comparison, by analogy to Stouffer's soldiers. He tests it only indirectly, using a "flair" survey item as an intervening variable. There is no formal model.

- p. 24: "In this sense, the elite colleges and universities are the Military Police of higher education, because a given level of scholastic aptitude (longevity) is least likely to yield high grades (promotions) in such schools."
- p. 25: "(b) In the absence of any objective evidence, students tend to evaluate their academic abilities by comparison with other students. (c) Most of the other students one knows are those on one's own campus, and since GPA's are reasonably public information, they become the accepted yardstick."
- p. 30, a general contextual hypothesis: "The greater the proportion of a group possessing or indorsing some characteristic X, the more likely it is that a newcomer will tend to become favorable toward X and the less likely it is that he will view himself as possessing X to any unusual degree."

**Separating sorting from effects.** Weak. Davis argues that GPA and school quality are independent (γ ≈ .05) and about equally associated with aptitude, so the difference between them "cannot be explained by the distribution of academically promising Freshmen" (p. 20). He controls for freshman career preference. He does not control for socioeconomic status: "our data will tend to overstate the effects of school quality for variables correlated with socio-economic status" (p. 20, footnote 12).

**Limitations.**

- [imperfect measure] p. 24: "in the absence of scholastic-aptitude measures across the total sample, this hypothesis cannot be fully tested with the data."
- [imperfect measure] p. 18, footnote 6: "The data are treated as if they constituted a panel study, but the Freshman-year measures are retrospective. The amount and nature of error introduced is unknown." The "flair" item was "not designed for our purposes" (p. 25).
- [no model of mechanism] The mechanism is only partly tested. p. 25, footnote 20: "This strategy admittedly leaves out the question of whether it is actually comparisons with this-that-or-the-other students which generate the process or whether the "GPA yardstick" is transmitted through student culture, via the faculty, or perhaps through parents. The question is left out because we have no data to tackle it."
- p. 29: "not every seam has been joined so securely that we may say that the data "prove" our interpretation."
- [single system] US colleges, one cohort (1961), men only.
- [outcome is not selection] The outcome is a stated career *preference*, not admission or placement. (Assistant judgment.)

**Policy line (useful framing, p. 31).** "Counselors and parents might well consider the drawbacks as well as the advantages of sending a boy to a "fine" college, if, when doing so, it is fairly certain he will end up in the bottom ranks of his graduating class. The aphorism "It is better to be a big frog in a small pond than a small frog in a big pond" is not perfect advice, but it is not trivial."

**Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** Davis bears on the **evaluation** channel: the same aptitude yields lower local grades in a selective pond, so grades are relative. He also bears on **development** through self-evaluation and career aspiration. The source is the **peers** (the composition of the student body), operating through an **organizational** device, the local grading curve. This is the earliest statement of "same fish, different pond," and Davis's own analogy (soldiers and promotions) explicitly connects it to selection.

## 2. Marsh (1987): The big-fish-little-pond effect on academic self-concept

**Citation.** Marsh, Herbert W. 1987. "The big-fish-little-pond effect on academic self-concept." *Journal of Educational Psychology* 79(3): 280–295. DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.79.3.280.

**Source caveat.** The library PDF is **not** the journal version. It is the ERIC manuscript (ED 278 685, dated "8 May, 1986", "Revised: 15 September, 1986"). Page numbers below are the **manuscript's running-head page numbers ("ms p.")**, and manuscript page = PDF page − 3. **Journal page numbers cannot be given from this file.** Quotes come from tesseract OCR of the scan; I silently fixed obvious single-character OCR errors (e.g., "schaols" → "schools"). Before quoting in print, verify against the published article.

**Data and setting.** United States. Youth in Transition longitudinal study (Bachman), a representative sample of boys from 87 high schools.

- ms p. 14: "Five subgroups that comprise the total sample of 2213 students surveyed at Time 1 are considered; white students in all-white schools (n=1080), white students in mixed schools (n=832), black students in all-black schools (n=169), black students in mixed schools (n=87), and "others" (n=45)."
- ms p. 11: "a large representative sample of boys from 87 high schools".
- **Measures.** Academic ability is the average of three standardized tests. GPA is self-reported in an interview. Academic self-concept consists of self-ratings "in comparison with others of their own age" (ms p. 15). School-average ability and school-average SES are the means over each school's students.
- **Outcomes.** Academic self-concept, global esteem, and GPA at Time 1 and Time 2. Survey years are not stated in the passages I extracted.

**Design.** Observational. Path models (structural models) at Time 1 and a longitudinal Time 1 → Time 2 model, estimated on the 1,472 students who did not change schools. School-average ability enters as a contextual predictor after controlling for individual ability.

**Main findings (verbatim).**

1. Abstract (manuscript abstract page, unnumbered; PDF p. 3): "Marsh and Parker (1984) described the big-fish-little-pond effect (BFLPE) whereby equally able students have lower academic self-concepts in high-ability schools than in low-ability schools. [...] Second, equally able students earned higher grades in lower-ability schools. This frame-of-reference effect for grades was distinct from, but contributed to, the BFLPE for academic self-concept. Third, a longitudinal analysis demonstrated that academic self-concept had a direct effect on subsequent school performance beyond the effects of academic ability and prior school performance. About one-quarter of this effect could be explained in terms of the BFLPE."
2. ms p. 22, effect size: "the negative impact of school-average ability on academic self-concept (-.232) is substantial, and similar for Times 1 and 2."
3. ms p. 23: "its total causal effect -- including the indirect effect through GPA (-.265 = -.229 + -.036) is larger. Thus there are two separate frame-of-reference effects that each contribute to the BFLPE. Students in low-ability schools compare themselves with less able students which leads to a higher academic self-concept. Students in low-ability schools earn higher grades than equally able students in high-ability schools and this also contributes to higher academic self-concepts."
4. ms p. 19, the grading curve: "Thus teachers apparently graded on a "curve" that was relatively independent of external standards -- one that was primarily determined by the immediate context of the ability levels of students in the school. Equally able students tended to have higher GPAs in low-ability schools than in high-ability schools."
5. ms p. 25: "attending a high-ability school produces a lower academic self-concept which subsequently produces poorer academic performance."

**Mechanism or model.** Yes. Marsh uses the Marsh and Parker (1984) "frame of reference model" (ms p. 10): a social-comparison mechanism in which the comparison group is one's classmates. The model is operationalized as path models that are **estimated on data**; the signs of the paths are the test. This is a statistical structural model, not a generative or formal behavioral model. (Assistant judgment.)

**Effect size depends on the pond contrast (useful for "same fish, different pond"), ms p. 26.** "These complications make it difficult to establish the size of the BFLPE in any absolute sense, but they do illustrate that its size will depend on the particular application. If a parent is deciding whether to send their child to a mediocre or an academically elite school, the size of the BFLPE will be much larger in relation to this decision than a population estimate of the BFLPE."

**Separating sorting from effects.** Marsh controls for individual ability and SES and treats the remaining association with school-average ability as the contextual effect. There is no quasi-random assignment to schools. Unobserved selection into schools is not addressed. (Assistant judgment.)

**Limitations.**

- [imperfect measure] ms p. 30: "A potential problem with this conclusion that requires further consideration is that GPA was a self-report measure rather than an objective index." The same page: "even though causal inferences based on correlational data must always be viewed with caution."
- [imperfect measure] ms p. 19: "Since a similar sort of frame-of-reference effect influences both GPA and academic self-concept, path models that contain both variables must be interpreted cautiously."
- [single system] US boys, a single cohort. On generalizing across ages, ms p. 27: "More rigorous tests of the proposed relation between the size of the BFLPE and age require a longitudinal study that spans preadolescent and adolescent years".
- [model not fit to data] (partial; Assistant judgment) The path model is fit to data, but the comparison process itself (who compares with whom) is not modeled.
- [outcome is not selection] The outcomes are self-concept and GPA.

**Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** Marsh bears on **evaluation**: local grading curves give equally able students different GPAs depending on the pond. It also bears on **development**: lower self-concept leads to lower later performance. The source is **peers** (school-average ability), with grading as an organizational transmission device. This is direct evidence that the *same* ability level produces different evaluations in different ponds.

## 3. Marsh & Hau (2003): BFLPE, 26-country test (ABSTRACT ONLY)

**Citation.** Marsh, Herbert W., and Kit-Tai Hau. 2003. "Big-Fish--Little-Pond effect on academic self-concept: A cross-cultural (26-country) test of the negative effects of academically selective schools." *American Psychologist* 58(5): 364–376. DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.58.5.364.

**Source status.** There is no PDF in the library. Crossref (`api.crossref.org/works/10.1037/0003-066x.58.5.364`) returned metadata but **no abstract**, and Semantic Scholar reports the abstract as elided by the publisher. The text below is the **OpenAlex** abstract (`api.openalex.org`), rebuilt from OpenAlex's word-position index. Word order is reliable, but punctuation and capitalization may differ slightly from the published abstract, and OpenAlex abstracts are sometimes truncated. **No page numbers are available. Verify before quoting.**

> "Academically selective schools are intended to affect academic self-concept positively, but theoretical and empirical research demonstrates that the effects are negative. The big-fish-little-pond effect (BFLPE), an application of social comparison theory to educational settings, posits that a student will have a lower academic self-concept in an academically selective school than in a nonselective school. This study, the largest cross-cultural study of the BFLPE ever undertaken, tested theoretical predictions for nationally representative samples of approximately 4,000 15-year-olds from each of 26 countries (N = 103,558) who completed the same self-concept instrument and achievement tests. Consistent with the BFLPE, the effects of school-average achievement were negative in all 26 countries (M beta = -.20, SD = .08), demonstrating the BFLPE's cross-cultural generalizability."

- **Data.** Nationally representative samples of about 4,000 15-year-olds from each of 26 countries, N = 103,558. The abstract does not name the dataset; I believe it is PISA 2000, but that is **not confirmed** from the abstract.
- **Design.** Multilevel (students within schools) contextual effect of school-average achievement, as I infer from "effects of school-average achievement". The details are not verifiable from the abstract alone.
- **Finding.** The school-average achievement effect is negative in all 26 countries: mean β = −.20, SD = .08.
- **Mechanism.** Social comparison theory, as stated in the abstract.
- **Limitations.** [imperfect measure] Self-concept is a self-report, and the data are cross-sectional (Assistant judgment). [outcome is not selection]. This study answers the [single system] critique of Marsh (1987) with 26 countries. Authors' own limitations are **not available**, because I did not read the full text.
- **Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** **Development and evaluation** (self-concept) driven by **peers**. Its importance for the storyline is cross-national replication of "same fish, big pond → lower self-concept".

## 4. Fang et al. (2018): BFLPE meta-analysis

**Citation.** Fang, Junyan, Xitong Huang, Minqiang Zhang, Feifei Huang, Zhe Li, and Qiting Yuan. 2018. "The Big-Fish-Little-Pond Effect on Academic Self-Concept: A Meta-Analysis." *Frontiers in Psychology* 9: 1569. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01569. The article is 11 pages; "p." refers to the article page numbers printed in the footer.

**Data and setting.** Meta-analysis of articles published January 1, 1984 – January 1, 2018. The articles are in English, full-text and peer-reviewed, and drawn from ERIC, PsycINFO, and other databases.

- p. 3: "After excluding the studies using the same data resource, we got 33 studies in total with 56 effect sizes (N = 1,276,838) in the end."
- p. 5: "Thirty-nine of the ESs were based on Large-scale assessments (7 for PISA, 6 for TIMSS, and 26 for other assessments like TOSCA), other 17 were retrieved from studies collecting data independently."
- **Inclusion rule.** Studies had to use "the classic BFLPE model that test the class/school effect after controlling for student effect" (p. 3).
- **Outcome.** Academic self-concept (ASC).

**Design.** Random-effects meta-analysis of multilevel regression coefficients (converted to Fisher's Z), followed by moderator analyses.

**Main findings (verbatim).**

1. Abstract, p. 1: "Thirty-three studies with fifty-six effect sizes (total N = 1,276,838) were finally included. The random effects model led to a mean of the BFLPE at β = −0.28 (p < 0.001). Moreover, moderator analyses revealed that the Big-Fish-Little-Pond effect is an age-based process and an intercultural phenomenon, which is stronger among high school students, in Asia and when verbal self-concept is considered."
2. p. 5: "The homogeneity test results were Q = 25,478.88 (df = 55, p < 0.001), I 2 = 99.78%, so the random effects model was chosen. The integrated results showed a significant negative effect of class/school average achievement on student ASC: β = −0.28 (Z = −13.84, p < 0.001, 95% CI = [−0.32, −0.24]), which means that students in class/school with an average ability level one standard deviation above the mean have ASC that is 0.28 of a standard deviation below the average ASC level."
3. p. 5, moderators: "students in high school indicate the strongest effect (β highschool = −0.32), while middle school and college students show a moderate effect (β middleschool = −0.28, β college = −0.23), and primary school students show the weakest effect (β primaryschool = −0.21)." Location (from Table 5 and the surrounding text, p. 6): Asia −0.35; North America −0.20.
4. p. 5: "There was no significant influence of comparison target" (class versus school; Q = 0.01).

**Mechanism or model.** The study adopts social comparison as the assumed mechanism (p. 2: "Marsh (1987) argued that this social comparison mechanism lies at the heart of the BFLPE."). The meta-analysis does not test the mechanism; it only summarizes the size of the contextual coefficient.

**Separating sorting from effects.** This is inherited from the primary studies, which use a covariate-adjusted multilevel model (controlling for individual achievement). The meta-analysis does not distinguish quasi-experimental designs from observational ones. (Assistant judgment.)

**Limitations.**

- Authors' own statement, p. 9: "There exists an apparent gap between the number of different comparison targets (39 for school-average achievement and 17 for class-average achievement). This may result in the insignificant result in the moderation analyses [...]. Furthermore, the dependence of ESs caused by deriving more than one ES from a study or from studies conducted by the same research team was not examined here, which can be further discussed using a multilevel model."
- [imperfect measure] The outcome is self-reported self-concept, and the pooled coefficients come from observational controls (Assistant judgment). I² = 99.78% indicates very large between-study heterogeneity.
- [no model of mechanism] The mechanism is assumed, not estimated.
- [outcome is not selection].
- On future work, p. 9, the authors raise heterogeneity by ability: "some researches (Marsh and Rowe, 1996; Trautwein et al., 2009) found that the ASC of relatively high-achieving students appear to be less affected by BFLPE than those of relatively low-achieving students."

**Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** **Development** (self-concept) driven by **peers**. Fang et al. establish that the big-fish effect on self-concept is robust and roughly −0.2 to −0.3 SD per SD of peer average. The study does not reach behaviors or selection outcomes.

## 5. Murphy & Weinhardt (2020): Top of the class: the importance of ordinal rank

**Citation.** Murphy, Richard, and Felix Weinhardt. 2020. "Top of the Class: The Importance of Ordinal Rank." *Review of Economic Studies* 87(6): 2777–2826. DOI 10.1093/restud/rdaa020.

**Source caveat.** The library PDF is **NBER Working Paper 24958 (August 2018)**, not the REStud version. **Page numbers below are working-paper pages ("WP p.")**, not REStud pages. Effect sizes may differ slightly in the published version. Check before citing numbers in print.

**Data and setting.** England. National Pupil Database, the census of state-school students.

- WP p. 3: "we use administrative data on five cohorts of the entire English state school sector covering over 2.25 million students from the end of primary school to the end of secondary school."
- WP p. 3: "These national assessments are marked externally from the school and are intended to be absolute measures of individual achievement; hence, scores are not set to a curve at class, school, district, or country level."
- **Treatment.** Rank in age-11 test scores within the primary school-subject-cohort.
- **Outcomes.** Age-14 and age-16 national test scores; A-level subject choice at age 18; self-reported subject confidence at age 14, from a linked LSYPE survey subsample of 11,558 student observations (WP p. 15).
- WP p. 3: "In England, students moving from primary to secondary school have on average 87 percent new peers."

**Design.** Within-class differences between ordinal rank and cardinal score, plus idiosyncratic variation in test-score distributions across classes. Specifications include primary school-subject-cohort fixed effects and, in some, student fixed effects (variation across subjects within a student). WP p. 2: "One can consider this to be analogous to a regression discontinuity approach with primary school test scores as the running variable and rank as treatment status".

**Main findings (verbatim).**

1. Abstract (WP, unnumbered page): "This paper establishes a new fact about educational production: ordinal academic rank during primary school has long-run impacts that are independent from underlying ability. [...] We find large effects on test scores, confidence and subject choice during secondary school, where students have a new set of peers and teachers who are unaware of the students' prior ranking. The effects are especially large for boys, contributing to an observed gender gap in end-of-high school STEM subject choices."
2. WP p. 17: "Column 1 shows that the effect of being ranked top compared to bottom ceteris paribus is associated with a gain of 7.946 national percentile ranks (0.29 standard deviations). [...] A one standard deviation increase in rank is associated with increases in later test scores by 0.084 standard deviations or 2.35 national percentile points." With student fixed effects (same page): "for any one standard deviation increase in rank test scores increase by about 0.056 standard deviations."
3. WP p. 3, subject choice: "conditional on achievement, being at the top of the class in a subject during primary school rather than at the median increases the probability of an individual choosing that subject by almost 20 percent."
4. WP p. 18: "students at the top of the class in a subject compared to being ranked at the bottom are 2.5 percentage points more likely to choose that subject as an A-Level. On average, roughly one in ten students complete these subjects at A-Level."
5. WP p. 32, confidence: "Moving from the bottom of the class to the top increases confidence by 0.196 points on a five-point scale, which corresponds to 20 percent of a standard deviation of this confidence measure."

**Mechanism or model.** Yes, with a derived test. The authors build a two-period effort-allocation model (WP pp. 32–35) that nests a "learning about ability" mechanism and a "non-cognitive skills" mechanism (lower cost of effort). They derive one testable implication, about a misinformation measure, and estimate it.

- WP p. 35: "we fail to reject the null hypothesis that the degree of misinformation does not cause students to misallocate effort. Therefore, we conclude that the learning mechanism alone is unlikely to generate our results."
- The model is **not structurally estimated**. Only a reduced-form implication is tested. (Assistant judgment.)

**Separating sorting from effects.** School-subject-cohort fixed effects absorb all group-level factors. Student fixed effects absorb individual and school-wide factors. The robustness section tests whether parents sort to primary schools on rank, and checks balance on observables. WP p. 3: "schools are not allowed to choose students based on ability or rank."

**Limitations.**

- [single system] England only. Rank is measured in a primary-school class with externally marked tests.
- [imperfect measure] The confidence measure is a single survey item in a non-representative subsample. WP p. 32: "This is despite there being very few LSYPE respondents per primary school (4.5 students conditional on at least one student being in the survey), which severely limits the degrees of freedom in each primary school subject group." WP p. 15: "While this is more basic than surveys that focus on confidence, it does capture the essence of the concept".
- [model not fit to data] The model is used only to derive a sign test.
- [outcome is not selection] The outcomes are test scores and the student's own subject *choice*. A-levels are a gateway: WP p. 37: "A-Level choices are linked directly to university admissions." Actual admission is not observed.

**Bridge to the elite-school literature (useful), WP p. 38.** "The common puzzle is that many papers find no benefit from attending these selective schools. However, our findings would speak to why the potential benefits of prestigious schools may be attenuated through the development of a fall in confidence among these marginal/bussed students, who are also necessarily the low-ranked students."

**Policy line, WP p. 36.** "Should my child attend a "prestigious school" or a "worse school" where she will have a higher rank? Rank is just one of the many factors in the education production function. Therefore, choosing solely on the basis of rank is unlikely to be a correct decision."

**Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** Murphy & Weinhardt bear on **development** (confidence and effort, with lasting effects on scores) caused by **peers** (local rank among classmates). This is the cleanest "same fish, different pond" evidence: identical scores, different rank, different later outcomes, measured after the pond has been left. It touches selection only through the pipeline (A-level choice gates university admission).

## 6. Denning, Murphy & Weinhardt (2023): Class rank and long-run outcomes

**Citation.** Denning, Jeffrey T., Richard Murphy, and Felix Weinhardt. 2023. "Class Rank and Long-Run Outcomes." *Review of Economics and Statistics* 105(6): 1426–1441. DOI 10.1162/rest_a_01125. The library PDF is the published version, and the page numbers below are journal pages.

**Data and setting.** Texas, United States. Administrative records from the Texas Education Research Center, linking K–12 records (TEA), public higher education (THECB), and unemployment-insurance earnings (TWC).

- p. 1433: "the primary estimating sample will focus on the thirteen cohorts of 1995 to 2007."
- p. 1433: "For K–12 and initial college-attended outcomes, we have thirteen cohorts of students who took their third-grade tests between 1995 and 2007, giving 6,117,690 student-subject observations." Earnings at ages 23–27 come from 8 cohorts, 3,597,340 student-subject observations (same page).
- p. 1427: "we extend the literature by using administrative data on 3 million individuals".
- **Treatment.** Third-grade rank (ages 8–9) within grade-at-school, based on state test percentile.
- **Outcomes.** Grade retention, eighth-grade scores, AP courses, high-school graduation, public college enrollment (2-year and 4-year), STEM major, college graduation, and earnings at ages 23–27.

**Design.** Rank conditional on achievement, with school-subject-cohort (class) fixed effects, using idiosyncratic sampling variation in test-score distributions. The paper adds a formal identification discussion.

- p. 1428: "random classroom allocation is not sufficient for the identification of rank effects."
- p. 1440: "we discuss active sorting, passive sorting, and misspecification of the achievement mapping as potential sources of bias in this literature."

**Main findings (verbatim).**

1. Abstract, p. 1426: "students with a higher third-grade academic rank, conditional on achievement and classroom fixed effects, have higher subsequent test scores, are more likely to take AP classes, graduate from high school, enroll in and graduate from college, and ultimately have higher earnings nineteen years later."
2. p. 1437: "moving from the 25th to 75th percentile in rank increases the probability of graduating by 4 percentage points." College: "Moving from the 25th to 75th percentile in rank leads to an approximately 4 percentage point increase in college going."
3. p. 1438: "Low-ranked students have meaningful earnings penalties, earning on average $1,850 per year less than the median-ranked student. High-ranked students see increases in earnings, with the highest-ranked students earning $1,200 more per year on average than the median student."
4. p. 1440: "We find that a student enrolling in a class where they are at the 75th percentile rather than the 25th in third grade increases their real wages between ages 23 and 27 by $1,500 per annum, or approximately 7%."
5. p. 1428, the school-choice trade-off: "We find that if parents were to choose schools solely on the basis of mean peer achievement, rank effects would reduce 39% of the potential gains for median-performing students in the state. In contrast, when choosing based on value added, rank effects reduce the gains from choosing a better school only by 12%."

**Mechanism or model.** None. The authors are explicitly agnostic. p. 1427: "In this paper, however, we are agnostic about what is driving the effects. We define the rank effect to include any reactions to the rank of a student by any individual—the student, parents, or teachers."

- p. 1440 hints at an organizational channel: "Some of the effect of rank may come via teacher and administrator interactions with students. We document that students are more likely to be retained in third grade, a decision made not by the student but by teachers, administrators, and families."

**Separating sorting from effects.** Class fixed effects; balance tests on covariates; alternative functional forms; a small-school subsample with one class per grade; and measurement-error arguments. p. 1426 (intro): to the extent test scores are noisy, "our estimates should be interpreted as a lower bound."

**Limitations.**

- [single system] Texas public schools and Texas public colleges only. p. 1433, footnote 19: "We do not observe out-of-state enrollment or enrollment at private institutions in Texas. Hence, if higher rank tends to cause students to leave the state, we will underestimate the effect of rank on college attendance." Footnote 20 makes the same point for out-of-state employment and earnings.
- [imperfect measure] Rank is measured on a low-stakes test. p. 1434: "we interpret our test score rank measure as a proxy for students' day-to-day academic ranking in their class."
- [no model of mechanism] Agnostic by design (p. 1427).
- [outcome is not selection] (partly) College *enrollment* and grade *retention* are observed, but not admission decisions or competition for slots. Retention is a decision made by others (teachers and administrators), so it is the closest outcome to "evaluation by an organization". (Assistant judgment.)
- On counterfactual re-sorting, p. 1440: "this sort of exercise warrants caution because the changes in classroom distribution may be out of sample for the estimates in this paper (Carrell et al., 2013)."
- Future work, p. 1440: "Future research should also consider the effect of rank in groups outside school settings."

**Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** Denning et al. bear mainly on **development**, with a possible **evaluation** channel through teacher and administrator retention decisions. The source is **peers** (local rank). The study links "same fish, different pond" all the way to adult earnings. The explicit "better peers versus lower rank" trade-off (39%) is the quantitative version of Davis's frog-pond advice.

## 7. Elsner & Isphording (2017): A big fish in a small pond: ability rank and human capital investment

**Citation.** Elsner, Benjamin, and Ingo E. Isphording. 2017. "A Big Fish in a Small Pond: Ability Rank and Human Capital Investment." *Journal of Labor Economics* 35(3): 787–828. DOI 10.1086/690714. The library PDF is the published version, and the page numbers below are journal pages.

**Data and setting.** United States. AddHealth (National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health).

- p. 792: wave 1 was "administered in 1994–95, when students were between 13 and 18 years old". Wave 4 was in 2008–2009.
- p. 793: "Our final sample comprises 13,645 students in 130 schools and 432 school-cohort combinations."
- **Ability measure.** Age-specific standardized Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test (PPVT-R). Rank is computed within the school-cohort.
- **Outcomes.** High-school completion, college attendance, and 4-year degree completion (wave 4). Mechanism variables: perceived intelligence, career expectations, effort, mental distress, and support from others.

**Design.** Within-school, across-cohort variation in cohort composition. p. 788: "We argue that, conditional on attending a given school, the cohort composition is as good as random for a particular student because school entry is determined by a student's birth date and is beyond the influence of parents or students." Robustness: controls for the cohort's mean and variance of ability, school-by-cohort fixed effects, and a subsample in the "right" age cohort.

**Main findings (verbatim).**

1. Abstract, p. 787: "We find that a student's ordinal rank significantly affects educational outcomes later in life. Students with a higher rank are significantly more likely to finish high school and to attend college. Exploring potential channels, we find that students with a higher rank have higher expectations about their future career, as well as a higher perceived intelligence."
2. p. 788: "An increase in the ordinal rank by one within-school standard deviation increases the likelihood of going to college by 1.2 percentage points."
3. p. 808: "A one decile increase in the ordinal rank increases the probability of finishing high school by half a percentage point. The coefficient of college attendance is large and statistically significant. A one decile increase in the ordinal rank increases the likelihood of attending college by 1.26 percentage points. The effect on college completion is smaller, and it is imprecisely estimated."
   - Note: the introduction states the effect per within-school SD, and the results section states it per decile. I report both as written; I did not reconcile them.
4. p. 819: "Conditional on absolute ability, students with a 10 percentage points higher rank are 0.22 percentage points more likely to believe that they are more intelligent than the average."
5. p. 822: "The ordinal rank provides students with noisy information about their own ability, which in turn distorts the trade-off between the short-run costs and the long-run benefits from schooling."

**Mechanism or model.** Five candidate mechanisms are listed verbally (p. 789): incomplete information about ability, intrinsic motivation, a responsive environment, tracking, and **selective college admission policies**. These are tested with suggestive mediation regressions. No formal model is fit.

- p. 790: "While we are unable to fully disentangle these mechanisms, we exploit the rich survey information in AddHealth to provide suggestive evidence that some channels are more important than others. We find strong evidence for the expected returns channel."

**Selection into scarce slots, directly addressed (pp. 789–790).** "Finally, the result could be explained by selective college admission policies. Colleges often observe a student's grade point average (GPA) rank within a cohort, which is correlated with our rank measure. If admissions officers give priority to students with a higher rank regardless of the school quality, or if colleges automatically admit the top 10% of a school cohort, then this can explain the effect." Their test, p. 790: "while we have no information on the type of college to which students are admitted, we can exclude the notion that the effect is purely driven by selective college admissions. When we run our baseline model and additionally control for GPA, the effect of the ability rank on educational attainment remains large and statistically significant, indicating that GPA-based college admissions explain only a fraction of the effect."

**Separating sorting from effects.** Comparison is within school and across cohorts. Strategic school-entry delay is checked with an age-cohort subsample. p. 789 on measurement error: "Based on a Monte Carlo experiment, we show that our estimates would be around 20% higher in the absence of measurement error."

**Limitations.**

- [small data] Sample of 13,645 students across 130 schools. Rank is computed from a *sample* of each cohort (stratified random sampling), which introduces measurement error (p. 789).
- [imperfect measure] Rank is based on a vocabulary test whose scores students do not see. p. 798: "While we cannot directly infer students' knowledge of their exact ranks from the survey, we have two pieces of evidence showing that students have knowledge about their relative ability in their cohort."
- [no model of mechanism] p. 790: "we are unable to fully disentangle these mechanisms".
- [outcome is not selection] / admission is not observed. p. 790: "we have no information on the type of college to which students are admitted".
- [single system] US high schools, a single survey.
- Authors' caveat, p. 823: "the results should be taken with the caveat that rank is only one input in educational production. Our results reflect local effects, which we obtained by comparing students within the same school but in different cohorts."

**Policy line (zero-sum nature of rank), p. 823.** "It is difficult to think of a Pareto-improving algorithm that reduces this inefficiency by regrouping students into schools because the ordinal rank is a relative measure and improving one student's rank means worsening the rank of another."

**Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** Elsner & Isphording bear on **development** (beliefs and expectations driving investment). They explicitly raise, but cannot isolate, a **selection** channel: organizations (colleges) admitting on within-school rank, such as top-10% rules. The source is **peers** (cohort composition). This is the clearest education paper flagging that rank effects may partly *be* a selection rule that rewards local standing.

## 8. Elsner, Isphording & Zölitz (2021): Achievement rank affects performance and major choices in college

**Citation.** Elsner, Benjamin, Ingo E. Isphording, and Ulf Zölitz. 2021. "Achievement Rank Affects Performance and Major Choices in College." *Economic Journal* 131(640): 3182–3206. DOI 10.1093/ej/ueab034. The library PDF is the published version, and the page numbers below are journal pages.

**Data and setting.** The Netherlands. One business school with repeated **random assignment** of students to teaching sections (the school is not named in the text I extracted).

- p. 3185: "Our estimation sample consists of five adjacent cohorts who entered the business school between 2009 and 2013."
- p. 3186: "estimation sample of 3,920 students and 23,526 student-course observations".
- **Treatment.** Rank in pre-determined GPA within a randomly assigned section (section size about 12.6).
- **Outcomes.** Course grades, dropout, pass rates, follow-up course choice, math-intensive electives, related major at graduation, graduation, and self-reported earnings from a graduate survey.

**Design.** Randomized section assignment, flexible controls for own GPA, and section (peer group) fixed effects. Dynamic analyses exploit repeated reassignment. Exams are centrally graded. p. 3185: "This, together with the fact that exams are centrally graded, minimises concerns that section teachers may have a direct impact on grades and alleviates the concern that the rank effect may mechanically result from grading on a curve."

**Main findings (verbatim).**

1. Abstract, p. 3182: "By exploiting data with repeated random assignment of students to teaching sections, we find that a higher rank increases performance and the probability of choosing related follow-up courses and majors. We document two types of dynamic effect. First, earlier ranks are less important than later ranks. Second, responses to rank changes are asymmetric: improvements in rank raise performance, while decreases in rank have no effect. Rank effects partially operate through students' expectations about future grades."
2. p. 3183: "A one SD increase in rank reduces the risk of dropping out of the course by 2.3 percentage points while increasing the chance of passing the course by 3.2 percentage points. It also increases the grade in the respective course by 6.7% of an SD and the grade in a related follow-up course by 4% of an SD."
3. p. 3200: "we find a strong effect on the probability of graduating in a related major. An increase in rank by one SD increases this probability by 2.2 percentage points. [...] For a one SD increase in rank, the choice probability increases by 3.1 percentage points." (The 3.1 points refer to choosing high-math-intensity electives.)
4. p. 3200: "While we find a significant positive effect on the probability of graduation, we find neither significant effects on study satisfaction nor earnings."
5. p. 3204: "Because peers are randomly assigned, this signal carries substantially more noise than signal. Nevertheless, when making important career decisions, students appear to rely on their rank as a heuristic, thereby placing considerable weight on noisy information."

**Mechanism or model.** Verbal: belief formation through rank as a noisy signal, plus asymmetric updating. This is tested with survey measures (expected grades and satisfaction with peer interaction). No formal model is fit.

**On the BFLPE literature (useful for the gap), p. 3203, footnote 16.** "BFLP effects are typically estimated from the negative correlation between own academic self-concept and average peer ability while holding own ability constant. As such, many studies do not explicitly separate the effect of the ordinal rank from the mechanically related confounding effect of average peer quality."

**Separating sorting from effects.** Random assignment to sections removes sorting into peer groups. Rank variation within a given GPA comes from chance section composition.

**Limitations.**

- [single system] p. 3188: "We recognise that our data come from only one institution and that students at this business school may not be representative of the higher education population in other countries." (The authors argue that small-group teaching is common: "63% of OECD institutions use small-group teaching".)
- [imperfect measure] Earnings, p. 3200: "The earnings data are based on a non-random subsample and are self-reported, which means that they are likely subject to measurement error and selective misreporting."
- [small data] Moderate size: 3,920 students in one school.
- [no model of mechanism] / [model not fit to data] The belief mechanism is supported by survey evidence only.
- [outcome is not selection] Major choice is the student's own choice; the school is non-selective (p. 3188: "the school we study is a non-selective institution").

**Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** **Development** (beliefs, effort, specialization) driven by **peers** (section rank), with the **organization** (random assignment, central grading) holding evaluation fixed. Because peers are randomly assigned, this is the strongest causal "same fish, different pond" design in the set. The finding of asymmetric responses (gains from rank increases, nothing from decreases) is a distinctive dynamic.

## 9. Pop-Eleches & Urquiola (2013): Going to a better school: effects and behavioral responses

**Citation.** Pop-Eleches, Cristian, and Miguel Urquiola. 2013. "Going to a Better School: Effects and Behavioral Responses." *American Economic Review* 103(4): 1289–1324. DOI 10.1257/aer.103.4.1289.

**Source note.** I used the JSTOR copy of the published article (`files/635/Going_to_a_better_school.pdf`), where journal page = PDF page + 1287 (PDF p. 1 is a JSTOR cover sheet). The library also holds a "Submitted Version", which I did not use for quotes. On p. 1322 the right margin of the scan is cut off, so I took no quotes from that page.

**Data and setting.** Romania. National, centralized high-school admission based on a transition score (national test plus GPA). Students list ranked school/track choices and are assigned subject to slot constraints.

- p. 1290: "this mechanism generates about 2,000 cutoffs".
- p. 1296: "(i) The 2001-2003 cohorts, for which we linked admissions data with information on whether students took the Baccalaureate exam and how they performed (these cohorts took the exam in 2005-2007)."
- p. 1297: "These cohorts contain about 334,000 students attending about 800 high schools in 135 towns."
- **Survey** (2009) in towns with two or three schools. p. 1299: "we randomly sampled 19,878 children (about 65 percent of the total) out of this population. From this target sample, we obtained 12,590 parent and child surveys."
- **Outcome.** Baccalaureate exam performance. p. 1290: "Passing this exam is a requirement for application to university, and the grade is used by many institutions as a crucial admission criterion." The survey adds teacher assignment, parental help, and students' perceived relative standing and peer interactions.

**Design.** Regression discontinuity at the admission cutoffs, plus survey-based RD estimates of behavioral responses. A conceptual framework extends Todd and Wolpin (2003) with school-side responses (p. 1292).

**Main findings (verbatim).**

1. Abstract, p. 1289: "First, students who have access to higher achievement schools perform better in a (high stakes) graduation test. Second, the stratification of schools by quality in general, and the opportunity to attend a better school in particular, result in significant behavioral responses: (i) teachers sort in a manner consistent with a preference for higher achieving students; (ii) children who make it into more selective schools realize they are relatively weaker and feel marginalized; (iii) parents reduce effort when their children attend a better school."
2. p. 1291, effect size: "relative to individuals who just miss scoring above a school cutoff, those who succeed display a statistically significant 0.02 to 0.10 standard deviation advantage in Baccalaureate performance." (The first part of the sentence is on p. 1290.) Footnote 4: "In contrast, there is no evidence of an effect on the probability of taking the Baccalaureate exam."
3. p. 1291, rank and marginalization: "children who just score above a cutoff on average perceive themselves as weaker relative to their peers. [...] Additionally, however, this is associated with greater frequency of negative interactions with peers, providing some evidence that getting into a higher achievement school leads to marginalization."
4. p. 1291, organizational response: "although students who score just above a cutoff attend schools that on average have more certified teachers, the marginal (actual) teachers assigned to them are not observably different from those assigned to students who score just below the cutoff. In short, more qualified teachers are matched with higher achieving students."
5. p. 1291, dynamics: "the RD-estimated feelings of stigmatization and reduction in parental help are strongest earlier in students' high school careers, and diminish over time."

**Mechanism or model.** A conceptual production-function framework (pp. 1292–1293) motivates the behavioral responses. It is not estimated structurally; the responses are reduced-form RD estimates. (Assistant judgment.)

**Separating sorting from effects.** RD at admission cutoffs compares students just above and just below each cutoff. p. 1290: "the setting gives students incentives to truthfully reveal their preference rankings."

**Limitations.**

- [single system] p. 1292: "As stated, such behavioral responses and equilibrium effects may well be setting specific." p. 1323: "While the magnitude and even the direction of these responses may reflect institutions that are specific to Romania, their existence reflects that educational markets are complex settings in which several agents interact."
- [imperfect measure] Behavioral responses come from a survey in a subset of towns with a 63% response rate. p. 1299: "While the resulting sample is not completely representative of the population of these schools".
- [model not fit to data] The framework is illustrative only.
- [outcome is not selection] (partly) The Baccalaureate is a gateway to university admission, but admission itself is not observed in the extracted passages. RD estimates are local to marginal admits.

**Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** This paper covers all three channels: **development** (small positive achievement gains); **evaluation**/allocation by the **organization** (teachers sorted toward higher-achieving students *within* schools, so marginal admits do not receive the better teachers); and **peers** (marginal admits feel weaker and marginalized, which is the big-fish effect at an admission cutoff). It is a key bridge: the *selection* event (admission at a cutoff) changes both peer rank and organizational treatment.

## 10. Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist & Pathak (2014): The elite illusion

**Citation.** Abdulkadiroğlu, Atila, Joshua Angrist, and Parag Pathak. 2014. "The Elite Illusion: Achievement Effects at Boston and New York Exam Schools." *Econometrica* 82(1): 137–196. DOI 10.3982/ECTA10266. The library PDF is the published version, and the page numbers below are journal pages. The bib entry has an empty author field (`noauthor_elite_2014`), which should be fixed in the bib.

**Data and setting.** United States, Boston and New York City. The six traditional exam schools: Boston Latin School, Boston Latin Academy, and O'Bryant in Boston; Stuyvesant, Bronx Science, and Brooklyn Tech in NYC.

- p. 143: the Boston sample covers students "who applied for exam school seats in 7th grade from 1997 to 2008 or in 9th grade from 2001 to 2007." Note: these are application years. The 7th-grade years in the appendix tables are 1999–2008, as stated in a table note; the reason for the difference is not explained in the passages I read.
- p. 165: New York data cover "four 9th grade applicant cohorts (from 2004–2007), with follow-up test score information through 2009."
- **Outcomes.** MCAS state tests, PSAT, SAT, AP, and college enrollment and selectivity (from National Student Clearinghouse records). Boston admission uses ISEE scores plus GPA under deferred acceptance; NYC uses the SHSAT.

**Design.** Fuzzy regression discontinuity at the admission cutoffs, with exam-school offers used as instruments for peer characteristics (peer achievement and racial composition) in two-stage least squares. The paper builds a sharp RD from a deferred-acceptance match.

**Main findings (verbatim).**

1. Abstract, p. 137: "Successful applicants near admissions cutoffs for the least selective of these schools move from schools with scores near the bottom of the state SAT score distribution to schools with scores near the median. Successful applicants near admissions cutoffs for the most selective of these schools move from above-average schools to schools with students whose scores fall in the extreme upper tail. [...] Our estimates suggest that the marked changes in peer characteristics at exam school admissions cutoffs have little causal effect on test scores or college quality."
2. p. 142, precision: "Importantly, most of the 2SLS estimates reported here are reasonably precise; we can rule out relatively modest peer composition effects."
3. p. 163, college: "Table VI, which reports estimated effects on post-secondary outcomes, shows little evidence of a positive exam school treatment effect on college enrollment or quality. Despite the positive grade 10 English and AP score results for some O'Bryant applicants, applicants who clear the O'Bryant cutoff appear to be less likely to attend a competitive college or highly competitive college than they otherwise would have been, though only the nonparametric estimates here are significant."
4. p. 178: "The results reported here suggest that an exam school education produces only scattered gains for applicants, even among students with baseline scores close to or above the mean in the target school. Because the exam school experience is associated with sharp increases in peer achievement, these results weigh against the importance of peer effects in the education production function. [...] Overall, however, while the exam school students in our samples typically have good outcomes, most of these students would likely have done well without the benefit of an exam school education."
5. p. 179, on parents' beliefs: "The fact that we find little support for causal peer effects suggests that parents either mistakenly equate attractive peers with high value added, or that they value exam schools for reasons other than their impact on learning."

**Mechanism or model.** The paper has no behavioral model of why the effects are null. It provides an econometric argument that 2SLS is free of omitted-variables bias if resource and curriculum changes are themselves caused by peer composition (p. 142). The paper does not examine rank or big-fish channels in the passages I extracted. (Assistant judgment.)

**On why correlational peer studies find effects (useful for the "imperfect design" gap), p. 178.** "it is worth emphasizing the high risk of specification error in peer analysis. As noted by Manski (2000) and Angrist and Pischke (2009), among others, the relationship between an individual-level variable and any group average of this variable is essentially mechanical. [...] Random assignment to groups fails to solve either of these problems."

**Separating sorting from effects.** RD at admission cutoffs compares near-identical applicants on either side. Applicant-side sorting is held fixed because everyone in the sample applied.

**Limitations.**

- [single system] Two US cities, six schools. p. 178: "Every context is different, and the absence of peer effects in one setting does not prove that such effects are unimportant elsewhere."
- [imperfect measure] Outcomes are tests and college enrollment/selectivity. p. 178: "Of course, test scores and peer effects are only part of the exam school story. [...] It is also possible that exam school graduates earn higher wages, a question we plan to explore in future work."
- External validity: RD estimates are local to applicants at the cutoffs. The authors address this partly with a second running variable (p. 179: "information on a second variable with content similar to the running variable facilitates an exploration of the external validity of regression discontinuity estimates").
- [no model of mechanism] There is no account of why large peer changes produce no effect (for example, offsetting rank effects). Murphy & Weinhardt (WP p. 38) and Denning et al. (p. 1428) later propose rank as one explanation.
- [outcome is not selection] (partly) "College quality" is the closest outcome to selection: the selectivity of the college attended. Effects on it are null or negative for O'Bryant marginal admits.

**Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** **Development** from **peers**, with the null result at the cutoff. Winning a scarce slot (exam-school admission) did not raise achievement or college placement for marginal winners. This is the core "elite illusion" finding: the elite pond does not make the same fish bigger. It also shows that being selected into an elite organization is not the same as being developed by it.

## 12. Jackson (2010): Do students benefit from attending better schools? Trinidad and Tobago

**Citation.** Jackson, C. Kirabo. 2010. "Do Students Benefit from Attending Better Schools? Evidence from Rule-based Student Assignments in Trinidad and Tobago." *Economic Journal* 120(549): 1399–1429. DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0297.2010.02371.x. The library PDF is the published version, and the page numbers below are journal pages. The text layer renders apostrophes as "Õ"; I normalized them to ' in quotes.

**Data and setting.** Trinidad and Tobago. National assignment to secondary schools after grade 5, based on SEA scores and students' ranked school choices.

- p. 1403: "The data used come from two sources: the official SEA (Secondary Entrance Assessment) test score data (from 5th grade) for the 2000 cohort and the official 2004 and 2005 CSEC test score data."
- p. 1404: "Of the 31,593 SEA test takers in 2000, 22,876 students were linked to CSEC examination data five years later (or four years for early takers)."
- p. 1410: "the final data set used comprises 23,322 students at 98 schools."
- **Outcomes.** CSEC (end-of-secondary) exam taking, number of exams passed, and obtaining a certificate. p. 1419 defines the certificate as having "passed 5 examinations including mathematics and English – the prerequisite to pursuing tertiary education".

**Design.** Instrumental variables built from the deterministic part of the assignment rule: regression discontinuity at simulated cutoffs, difference-in-differences, and a "full" rule-based instrument. The analysis conditions on incoming scores and school preferences.

- p. 1400 (setting advantage): "there is a national curriculum so that school effects are not confounded with large curricular differences".

**Main findings (verbatim).**

1. Abstract, p. 1399: "I use instrumental variables based on the discontinuities created by the assignment mechanism and exploit rich data which include students' test scores at entry and secondary school preferences to address self-selection bias. I find that attending a better school has large positive effects on examination performance at the end of secondary school. The effects are about twice as large for girls than for boys."
2. p. 1419: "the clean 2SLS-D based on the non-assisted schools, the 2SLS-DID and the 2SLS-Full models suggest that attending a school where peer test scores are half a standard deviation higher is associated with passing between 0.28 and 0.34 more CSEC examinations."
3. p. 1419, on the selection-relevant outcome: "attending a school where peer test scores are half a standard deviation higher is associated with being between 5 and 7 percentage points more likely to obtain a certificate. [...] These estimates imply that a student who misses their top choice school would be between 9 and 12 percentage points less likely to obtain the prerequisites to pursue tertiary education."
4. p. 1419, effect on taking the exam: "suggesting little or no effect on CSEC taking."
5. p. 1427: "The results indicate that the marginal effect of attending a school with higher-achieving peers is non-linear so that the benefits to attending schools with marginally brighter peers are low at the lower end of the peer achievement distribution."

**Mechanism or model.** None. "Better school" is a bundle of peers and inputs that the paper does not separate. p. 1399: "the quality of school inputs may be endogenous to the quality of peers because schools with bright, motivated students may attract better teachers and may have more affluent alumni networks leading to better facilities and better funding." In the passages I searched, there is no rank or self-concept analysis.

**Separating sorting from effects.** Rule-based instruments; the deterministic portion of the assignment "cannot be manipulated by students or school principals" (p. 1409). The analysis also conditions on stated preferences.

**Limitations.**

- [single system] One small country and one cohort (SEA 2000).
- [no model of mechanism] Peer effects and school-input effects are confounded by design (p. 1399, quoted above).
- [imperfect measure] Outcomes are exam-based. Selection into tertiary education is proxied by the certificate prerequisite, not observed directly.
- [outcome is not selection] (partly) The certificate is an eligibility threshold for tertiary education, which is close to selection. Admission itself is not observed.
- The author calls for future work on gender, p. 1427: "further research is needed to understand these gender differences better."

**Conflict with the US nulls.** Jackson finds *positive* effects at the cutoff, as Pop-Eleches & Urquiola do (small positive), whereas Abdulkadiroğlu et al. and Dobbie & Fryer find near-zero effects. The policy reading on p. 1428: "the finding that attending a more selective school improves one's outcomes implies that practices that group students by ability will tend to reinforce and exacerbate pre-existing differences in academic achievement."

**Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** **Development** from a bundle of **peers and organization** (school inputs). Winning a more selective slot raises the probability of clearing the next selection threshold (tertiary prerequisites). This is evidence that access to selective organizations can compound into later selection.

## 13a. Thiemann (2017): The persistent effects of short-term peer groups in higher education (brief)

**Citation.** Thiemann, Petra. 2017. "The Persistent Effects of Short-Term Peer Groups in Higher Education." IZA Discussion Paper No. 11024 (September 2017). 43 pp. This is a working paper with no journal version in the bib. Page numbers below are the DP's printed page numbers.

**Data and design.** Switzerland, University of St. Gallen. **Random assignment** to freshmen-week teams of about 15–16 students.

- p. 3: "detailed information on academic achievement (grades in mandatory first-year courses, enrollment, dropout, and graduation records) for 8,000 undergraduates (cohorts 2003-2012)."
- **Selection context**, p. 6: "The first year serves as a selection and orientation period. [...] Academic performance by the end of the first year determines whether students are admitted to the second year. On average, 66% of students pass the first year in their first attempt."

**Findings (verbatim).**

- Abstract (DP abstract page, unnumbered): "Ability peer effects are non-linear, i.e. very high or low levels of average peer ability in a group harm students' grades. These effects are most pronounced for low-ability students."
- p. 4: "for students in the bottom 10% of the peer ability distribution, increasing peer ability by one standard deviation leads to an increase in first-year GPA by 0.07 standard deviations, a non-negligible effect. Low-ability students are most vulnerable to being in groups with extremely high or extremely low peer ability, indicating that they benefit from positive peer spillovers, but might also suffer from discouragement effects in the presence of high-ability peers."

**Mechanism.** Verbal only ("discouragement effects"). Peer ability is an *imputed* measure (p. 4: "I use an imputed measure of peer ability, which is a weighted combination of a set of characteristics that predict ability").

**Limitations.** [single system] One university. [imperfect measure] Imputed peer ability. [no model of mechanism]. Working paper, so the results are provisional.

**Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** **Development** from **peers**. A non-linear (inverse-U) peer effect in a setting where first-year grades decide **selection into year 2**. The implication is that peers can move a student across an organizational survival threshold.

## 13b. Paulsen (2022): Peer effects and human capital accumulation — college and NBA productivity (brief)

**Citation.** Paulsen, Richard J. 2022. "Peer effects and human capital accumulation: Time spent in college and productivity in the National Basketball Association." *Managerial and Decision Economics* 43(8): 3611–3619. DOI 10.1002/mde.3617. The library PDF is the published version.

**Data and design.** All NBA draftees 2006–2014 (the "one-and-done" era). p. 3615: "Over the 9-year draft sample, 430 of the 540 players drafted spent at least 1 year playing college basketball." Regressions of NBA performance and persistence on years in college and a **power-conference indicator** (the peer-quality proxy), controlling for draft position, high-school recruiting ranking, and freshman statistics. Observational.

**Findings (verbatim).**

- Abstract, p. 3611: "Additional years in college are found to have an insignificant impact on persistence in the NBA at various points post draft, a positive impact on early career performance, and a negative impact on midcareer performance, while peer quality does not have a significant impact on performance."
- p. 3612: "Playing in a power conference is not found to have a significant impact on performance, which suggests that peer quality in college is not an important determinant of NBA success."

**Limitations (Assistant judgment).** [small data] 430 players. [imperfect measure] Peer quality is a binary power-conference indicator. [no model of mechanism]. [single system] NBA only. Selection into college programs and conferences is handled only by controls.

**Channel and source (Assistant judgment).** **Development** from **peers** (college conference), with a null effect on post-selection performance. This bridges education and sports: the "pond" before a draft (a scarce-slot selection event) does not predict post-draft productivity once draft position is controlled. Note that draft position is itself the selection outcome, so controlling for it conditions on selection.

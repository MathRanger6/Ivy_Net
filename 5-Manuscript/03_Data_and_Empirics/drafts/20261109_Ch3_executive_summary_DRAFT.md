Chapter 3 — Data and empirics

Chapter 2 ended by saying what the question needs: settings in which an individual's performance can be measured and held fixed while the quality of the peer group varies. This chapter supplies them. Its first half describes four systems and how their records were built. Its second half presents what the data show in each, before any model is introduced. The four are not a convenience sample. Each was chosen because it fits a short list of criteria, and the criteria, not the data sets, are the claim of the chapter.

A system fits when five things hold. A person's performance is observed and recorded, not inferred from the advancement it earns. People belong to groups, and the groups are not a random draw, so some people are surrounded by stronger peers than others. Selection is global: the opportunities are filled from across the whole system, not separately inside each group. The number of opportunities is limited, so the bar for advancing moves with the number of places and is not a fixed score that everyone could clear. And the system is mostly closed, with few people entering partway through, so the peers a person is compared with are the people competing for the same places. A general business setting usually fails the first test because no common performance measure exists. The same criteria say why other systems, such as competitive online games, could be added later.

The Army is the clearest match and has the most extensive records. Officers are rated on a recurring schedule, and a senior rater records a mark for each officer in a pool. Performance is how often an officer receives the top mark, the group is the officers who share a senior rater, and peer quality is how often that rater gave the top mark to the others in the group. Advancement is promotion from captain to major, counting any promotion. Leaving before promotion is a second outcome, because the Army does not fill the rank from outside. The records are the Army's personnel snapshots and evaluation reports, held in the Person-Event Data Environment run by the Army Analytics Group. What the Army cannot give us is a pure measure of performance: marks are assigned relative to the other officers in the pool.

Romanian education shows the same structure in a different institution. In 2001, students leaving lower secondary school were assigned to high-school programs by a national, centralized process with a fixed number of places in each program (Pop-Eleches and Urquiola 2013). Performance is the admission score [confirm what it combines in this data set], the group is the lower secondary school, called a gymnasium, from which the student applied, and peer quality is the average score of the other applicants from that school. Advancement is admission to a county's top program, or to one of its top two. The records are archived county admission results, linked to outcomes by name and score because the archival identifier is masked. What they cannot give us is the whole country or the whole school: the analysis covers 22 counties so far, and the peers are the applicants in the admission round, not every student in the school.

College basketball and the National Basketball Association (NBA) draft are the third system. Performance is points per minute in a season, the group is the team, and peer quality is the average points per minute of a player's teammates, leaving the player out. Advancement is being drafted, and the draft has a fixed number of places. Box scores for the seasons from 2009 through 2021 [confirm the seasons used in the chapter] are linked to draft lists by matching names and schools. What this system cannot give us is a rich measure of performance. Points per minute is one box-score statistic and leaves out much of what a draft decision may weigh.

Academic tenure is the fourth system, and the one we added. Performance is publications per year of career, the group is the department, and peer quality is the publication rate of the other faculty in the department in the year of the decision. Advancement is tenure at the up-or-out decision, usually in the fifth or sixth year as an assistant professor, and the alternative is leaving. Department rosters come from archived web pages of computer science departments at research universities in the United States, matched to publication records. What this system cannot give us is a clean count of places or complete records. No quota is published, so the chapter treats a fixed number of places as an assumption, and rosters recovered from archives are incomplete [add the treatment of missing outcomes once it is fixed].

In all four systems the chapter builds the same four things: a performance measure, a group, a measure of peer quality that leaves the person out, and an advancement outcome. Each performance measure is a proxy, and in each system an institution defines the group, not us. For each system the chapter says where the records came from, how they were cleaned, and which variable in the data corresponds to which of these concepts. This description is the science of the chapter. In a journal article it would become the supplementary data section.

The second half presents the results. For each system we show how often people advance, grouped by the quality of their peers and, where the data allow, within bands of their own performance. This is the display that bears on the question, because it shows whether more talent among one's peers is always better for the person or whether the advancement rate stops rising or turns down. In the Army, two panels sit side by side: the share promoted and the share who left without promotion. [State the pattern across the four systems here, as what the data show, once the results sections are settled.] These are descriptive comparisons. They contain no model, no estimate of a causal effect of peers, and no account of how performance develops. A longer analysis of timing in the Army, using survival methods, is mentioned in the data section and not developed. The next chapter asks what a simple model of selection predicts when the group is added to the score.

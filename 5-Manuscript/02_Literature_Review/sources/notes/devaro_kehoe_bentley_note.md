## DeVaro, Gürtler, Gürtler, and Deutscher (2024). "Big Fish in Small (and Big) Ponds: A Study of Careers." *Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization* 40 (1): 76–107. (Advance access 2022.)

**What it is.** A theoretical model of promotions and wages ("No new data were generated or analyzed," 97). There are two periods and two job levels. Firms are identical before the game starts. Workers privately observe a noisy signal of their own ability and choose an employer. Employers learn ability after period 1 and decide whom to promote; outside firms see only who was promoted and bid accordingly (promotion as a signal, following Waldman 1984).

**Main result.** An asymmetric "fish-and-ponds" equilibrium. Big ponds use stringent promotion rules (only high-ability workers are promoted); small ponds promote everyone. Workers with favorable signals choose big ponds, those with unfavorable signals choose small ponds. Promoted workers earn more in big ponds, because promotion there is a stronger signal.

**Quotes (verified pages)**
- p. 77: talented workers' "chances of rising to the upper echelons of the job hierarchy are relatively low in big ponds, but the potential rewards of doing so are high."
- p. 77: fish-and-ponds ideas "have been absent from the theoretical literature on promotion and wage dynamics."
- p. 81: "Each firm's number of positions in both types of jobs is unconstrained, so that all workers who perform sufficiently well in job 1 can and will be promoted. Under this assumption, all of the workers are hired and promoted independently of each other." → **In the baseline, coworkers do not compete for slots.** Extension 4.1 (pp. 91–92) adds a constraint on the number of managers, but uses it to explain why big ponds are bigger, not to make peers compete.
- p. 86: "conditional on ability, the overall promotion probability is weakly lower in the big than in the small pond."
- p. 86: "An implication is that ponds are signals."
- p. 86: "Employer screening and selection is a relevant consideration that should be explored in future work." (Their selection of workers into firms is by worker choice.)
- p. 94: "Most of what is believed concerning the fish-and-ponds phenomenon is not based on systematic empirical evidence but rather on anecdotes and widely accepted conventional wisdom."
- p. 95: "the concept of pond size has not been operationalized empirically"; they use firm size as a proxy.
- p. 80 (related literature worth knowing): Damiano et al. (2010, 2012), sorting when "workers care about the average ability of their peers" and their relative ranking; Deutscher et al. (2020), where the employer's only role is "to promote the best performer of its two employees"; Gong et al. (2018), soccer relegation, where "young players actually benefited from their teams being relegated."

**Where it fits.**
- **The closest existing model** of the big-fish question for careers: a strong environment (big pond) lowers the chance of promotion at a given ability while raising the reward and the signal value. That gives advantage and disadvantage in one model, which is exactly Alex's point that the trade-off is not new.
- **Self-assignment:** workers sort into ponds on private information (Charles's binding point on self-assignment).
- **How it differs from the dissertation (stated as differences, not as the dissertation's caveats):** pond strength is a firm's promotion *rule*, not the performance of the peers; coworkers do not compete for a fixed number of slots in the baseline (p. 81); employer selection is left for future work (p. 86); the model is not fitted to data and pond size has not been measured (pp. 94–95).
- **Placement:** Economics (with tournaments and promotion), the Introduction's big-fish paragraph, and synthesis paragraph 4 (what is already known).

## Kehoe and Bentley (2021). "Shadows and Shields: Stars Limit Their Collaborators' Exposure to Attributions of Both Credit and Blame." *Personnel Psychology* 74: 573–610. (Online 2020.)

**Zotero metadata to check (Charles):** the PDF footer reads "Personnel Psychology. 2021;74:573–610"; Zotero has year 2020. The byline reads "Rebecca R. Kehoe" and "F. Scott Bentley"; Zotero has "Rebecca" and "Frederick."

**What it is.** An empirical study of US hedge fund managers, 2005–2019: 32,278 nonstar managers across 9,311 comanaged funds at 2,872 firms (580). Stars are managers nominated for *Institutional Investor* awards in the prior three years, about 3% of managers (582). The outcome is "professional status attainment": the rank, by assets under management, of the firm a manager joins after changing employers (581). Panel regressions with random effects; tests for sample selection and endogeneity of comanaging with a star (583).

**Findings (verified pages)**
- p. 574: stars "also receive disproportionate credit when these collaborations succeed."
- p. 577: association with a star "may present a double-edged sword": knowledge and status spillovers on one side, crowding out of contributions and recognition on the other.
- p. 597: "the involvement of a star comanager dampens the positive effect of comanaged fund performance on FMs' professional status attainment—but, importantly, only among FMs who lack other credible signals of their own value."
- p. 598: "collaboration with a star comanager not only weakens but actually reverses the negative effect of fund failure, resulting in improved professional status attainment."
- p. 598: "nonstars receive more credit for the same collaborative performance when not working with a star."
- p. 599: cumulative advantage "rests largely on presumptions of stars' disproportionate influence."
- p. 600: performance is "extremely visible to prospective employers, raising the question of generalizability."

**Where it fits.**
- **Evaluation channel, with a selection-like outcome:** the same performance is credited differently depending on who one worked with, and the outcome is which employer hires the person next.
- **Strength as exceptional performers, not the average:** the environment variable is the presence of a star, which supports Alex's point 3 (the mean is not the only measure).
- **Both directions at once:** shadow on success, shield on failure, plus spillovers. A clean example of opposing forces.
- **Placement:** Organizational Studies (with Groysberg, Lee, and Nanda; Kanter) and synthesis paragraphs 2–3.

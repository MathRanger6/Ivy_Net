# Army officers B — mentors, raters, retention, and selection: source note

**Purpose:** verbatim, page-cited extracts on U.S. Army officers for the dissertation introduction. Not dissertation prose. Written 2026-11-02.

**Conventions**

- "p. N" = the page number printed on the page. Where a PDF has a cover page or front matter, the PDF-to-printed offset is given under each citation. Every page was confirmed with `pdftotext -f N -l N` against the running header or footer.
- Quotes are copied from the PDF text layer. Hyphenation at line breaks is rejoined; omissions are marked [...]. Nothing inside quotation marks is paraphrased.
- Lyle & Smith's JSTOR text layer renders "(" and ")" as "ð" and "Þ", "−" as "2", and "+" as "1". These are restored to the printed symbols in the quotes below.
- Baldwin 1996 has **no PDF on disk**. It is quoted from the Zotero full-text cache (`DE62RML4/.zotero-ft-cache`), and its pages are inferred from the running footers kept in that cache. Several of its pages are column-scrambled in the cache, so only clean sentences are quoted.
- "Channel" lines are **assistant judgment**, not the authors' claims. Channels: development (what a person becomes), evaluation/scoring (how a given performance is rated), selection (who gets a scarce slot).
- **NOT FOUND** = searched the full text (`rg` plus reading the relevant sections) and the item is not in the source.

**Contents** — §1 Lyle & Smith 2014; §2 Spain, Mukunda & Bates 2021; §3 Evans & Robinson 2020; §4 Evans & Bae 2019; §5 Wardynski, Lyle & Colarusso 2010; §6 Spain, Lin & Young 2020; §7 Dabkowski et al. 2010; §8 Peck 1994; §9 Baldwin 1996.

## 1. Lyle & Smith (2014) — high-performing mentors and early promotion

**Citation (as printed).** David S. Lyle and John Z. Smith, "The Effect of High-Performing Mentors on Junior Officer Promotion in the US Army," *Journal of Labor Economics*, 2014, vol. 32, no. 2, pp. 229–258 (April 2014). JSTOR stable URL 10.1086/673372. Both authors: US Military Academy. File: `Zotero/storage/AUH6SR3A/` (same 2,137,821-byte file in 627P4YRA, T6ULUPXL, PQZI2FTF). JSTOR cover page, so printed p. = PDF p. + 227.

- **Design/data.** Army administrative data on officers from 1974 through 2010, linked through officer evaluation reports from 1998 to 2008 (p. 235). Mentors are battalion commanders (lieutenant colonels) and protégés are their company commanders (captains) (p. 232). The protégé sample is male combat-arms officers commissioned through the US Military Academy (USMA) or the Reserve Officer Training Corps (ROTC), who were captains in 1998–2008, completed company command, and stayed to 10 years of service (p. 237; Table 2 note, p. 238). N = 4,142 company commanders, of whom 1,812 ever had a high-performing mentor; 2,131 battalion-commander mentors (Table 1, p. 236; Table 2, p. 238). Linear probability model with year-group, branch, demographic, and unit (to brigade) controls; standard errors clustered by mentor group (pp. 241–242).
- **Abstract** — "Military assignment mechanisms provide a unique opportunity to estimate the impact of high-performing mentors on job advancement of their subordinates. Combining US Army administrative data with officer evaluation reports, we find that high-performing mentors positively affect early junior officer promotion and that early promotion probabilities rise as the duration of the high-quality mentorship increases. These effects are largest for high-ability protégés. Junior officers who were exposed to multiple high-performing mentors did not experience an additional increase in promotion rates." (p. 229)
- **Definition of high-performing mentor: a battalion commander who was himself promoted early (below the zone) to major** — "we define high-performing mentors as mentors who were previously promoted early to the rank of major. This early promotion decision is a signal of high performance potential within the army officer corps and is granted several years prior to the senior officer mentors in our sample interacting with their junior officer protégés." (p. 231, n. 2). Also: "Battalion commanders were selected early to the rank of major some 8 years prior to serving as a battalion commander." (p. 235, n. 8)
- **Outcome: the protégé's own early (below-the-zone) promotion to major** — "In addition to using early (below the zone) promotion to the rank of major as a measure of mentor quality, we also use it as our outcome variable for the captains in company command positions." (p. 235). Early promotions "constitute roughly 8% of a cohort, and they are limited by policy to no more than 10% of that cohort." (p. 234)
- **How protégés are matched to mentors: the Army assigns them through a chain of third-party decisions** — "Junior officers receive their battalion commander mentor through a sequence of independent, third-party decisions. Military mentor relationships occur much like most other things related to the military—the army assigns them." (p. 233). "The army's Human Resources Command assigns these officers to an installation, the installation assigns them to a division, the division assigns them to a brigade, and the brigade assigns them to a battalion" (p. 234).
- **Why assignment is argued to be as good as random** — "Within a given rank and occupational branch, the army treats officers as generic for assignment purposes; talents and experiences of officers do not factor into assignments." (p. 234). "our designation of a battalion commander as a high-performing mentor is determined prior to the assignment of the lieutenant colonel to a battalion. Additionally, battalion commander assignments do not regard information on current or future junior officers" (p. 234).
- **Balance evidence** — "The lack of substantial differences across the demographic variables in table 2 supports our assertion that the assignment of company commanders to battalion commander mentors is as good as random with regard to observable characteristics." (p. 239). Covariates explain "only 1% of the total variation in the probability of ever having had a high-performing mentor" (p. 239). A prior Above Center of Mass (ACOM) rating does not predict mentor assignment: the estimate "is small and insignificant." (p. 241)
- **Key result (29%)** — "We find that junior officers who serve under a high-performing mentor are 29% more likely to be selected for early promotion to the rank of major." (p. 232). In points: "the estimate remains stable at 2.7 percentage points" (p. 242). Against a 9.2% base rate, "the treatment effect of serving under a high-quality mentor results in a 29% increase in the likelihood of early promotion to major." (p. 243)
- **Months of exposure** — "1 additional month spent with a high-performing mentor increases the likelihood of early promotion to major by 0.19 percentage points." (p. 244). Full specification: 0.17 points per month; at the 12.97-month average this "predicts a 2.2 percentage point, or 24%, increase in the likelihood of early promotion." (p. 244)
- **Multiple mentors** — the effect of a second high-performing mentor "is not statistically significant because only 5.72% of our company commanders had more than one high-quality mentor." (p. 246)
- **By protégé ability (SAT)** — Bottom half of SAT: the effect "is roughly one-half that found in the pooled sample, and it is not significant." (p. 246). Top half: "increasing the likelihood of early promotion by more than 4 percentage points in the full specification" (p. 246). "We interpret these findings as suggestive that the impact of high-performing mentors is increasing with protégé ability." (p. 246)
- **What the promotion board sees** — "Battalion commanders provide a majority of the input for company commander evaluation reports, but the only rater (mentor) information visible to the promotion board is the rater's name." (p. 235). "Brigade commanders must maintain a rating profile where at any point in time less than 50% of officers can receive an Above Center of Mass (ACOM) rating." (p. 235). Major boards are "made up of 18–20 senior officers." (p. 235)
- **Why early promotion to major matters later** — "early promotion to major increased the likelihood of subsequent early promotion to lieutenant colonel by a factor of nearly 8 and subsequent selection to battalion command by a factor of 3.5." (p. 233, n. 4)
- **Mechanisms are left open; the paper lists development, signaling, networks, preference, and role-model pathways** — "Although we are unable to identify the exact pathways through which mentor effects work" (p. 232). "The reduced-form mentorship estimates that we report should be interpreted as net effects." (p. 246). On the rating pathway: "high-performing mentors may better be able to recognize high potential in their protégés, as well as be more skilled at touting their protégés in written evaluations and recommendations." (p. 248)
- **Causal language.** The paper claims a causal effect in reduced form, and says so explicitly: "a plausible source of variation to identify a reduced-form causal effect of mentor quality on protégé promotion." (p. 241). The basis is Army assignment rules, balance tables, and the fact that mentor quality is fixed years before the pairing. They also concede the assumption is untestable: "Although we are unable to directly test this assumption, tables 2 and 3 provide supporting evidence" (p. 242). They do not claim to know which mechanism (development versus how the protégé is rated) carries the effect (pp. 232, 246).
- **Limits.** Male combat-arms USMA/ROTC officers only (p. 237). The effect may be specific to the Army: "our findings are probably an upper-bound estimate on the impact of mentoring on promotion. In professional organizations outside the military, results may be less pronounced." (p. 250). They cannot separate development from rating, and the mentor rates the protégé, so the outcome runs partly through the mentor's own evaluation (p. 248). Two protégé statistics in the note are worth double-checking: the Table 2 note prints the raw gap as "12.9 percentage points" (p. 238), but the table values (.109 vs .080) and text (p. 239: "2.9 percentage points") imply +2.9. The leading "1" is the text layer's rendering of "+".
- **Channel (assistant judgment).** The organization's assignment sets the environment (which commander). The outcome is selection (a capped below-the-zone slot), so the effect could run through development or evaluation, and the paper does not separate them.

## 2. Spain, Mukunda & Bates (2021) — the Battalion Commander Effect on lieutenant retention

**Citation (as printed).** Everett Spain, Gautam Mukunda, and Archie Bates, "The Battalion Commander Effect," *Parameters* 51, no. 3 (Autumn 2021): 101–114. US Army War College Quarterly; article 10 of vol. 51 iss. 3, dated "Fall 8-25-2021" on the USAWC Press cover. **Venue: published journal article, not a working paper.** The filename (`20210825_Spain_Bates_...`) matches the 25 Aug 2021 release date. Section heading "Leadership and Professionalism." Cover page, so printed p. = PDF p. + 99. The first article page (p. 101) carries no printed number; it is inferred from p. 102 that follows.

- **Design/data.** Army officer personnel database (monthly records since 1991, via the Office of Economic and Manpower Analysis, Army G-1), "traditional deployable battalions of the 1990s and early 2000s" (p. 105). N = 265 deployable battalions, 1,745 battalion commanders, 36,032 lieutenants (p. 105). Excludes aviation battalions, battalions moving under Base Realignment and Closure, and commanders with fewer than 10 or more than 60 lieutenants (p. 105). Outcome: whether the lieutenant is still in the Army past the end of the sixth year of service (p. 106). Regression with battalion-commander identity as the explanatory variable plus 13 controls (year group, branch, post, commissioning source, gender, race/ethnicity, age, undergraduate quality, marital/child status, Airborne/Air Assault/Ranger) (p. 106). Exact calendar years of the lieutenant cohorts: **NOT FOUND**.
- **Abstract: battalion commanders are associated with whether their lieutenants stay** — "Statistical evidence suggests that Army battalion commanders are significant determinants of the retention of their lieutenants—especially high-potential lieutenants." (p. 101)
- **Headline result** — "The results revealed—with a 99.9 percent confidence level—a significant battalion commander effect on the Army's retention of lieutenants." (p. 106)
- **Effect size reported as explanatory power, not a coefficient** — "the identity of the battalion commander explained at least 22 percent and 58 percent more of the lieutenants' retention decisions than any of the control variables, in terms of main effects and incremental effects, respectively (measured with the adjusted R2 statistic)." (pp. 106–107). Regression coefficients or standard errors: **NOT FOUND** (no tables in the article).
- **Spread across commanders** — "the average battalion commander in the study retained 59 percent of his or her lieutenants, and most battalion commanders retained between 46 percent and 72 percent (each one standard deviation from the mean) of their lieutenants." (p. 108). The standard deviation is 13 percent; the default text layer garbles this as "136 percent," and the raw layer reads "13 percent." "Some battalion commanders had a BCE of less than 20 percent while some were over 90 percent." (p. 108)
- **Commander versus battalion** — "the study still found that the battalion commanders' variable remained statistically significant while the battalion dummy variable did not." (p. 107)
- **Duration and commissioning source** — "the battalion commander under whom the junior officer served the longest was more predictive than the order (first, second, or third) of the commander." (p. 107). "On average, the battalion commander effect is significant for ROTC and USMA lieutenants, but not for OCS lieutenants." (p. 107)
- **High-potential lieutenants** — high potential is coded as USMA graduates, ROTC graduates from highly competitive schools, or distinguished military graduates: "In all, 12,239 (37.9 percent) of the 36,032 lieutenants were classified as high-potential officers." (p. 110). "the identity of the battalion commander explains more of the variance in high-potential lieutenants' retention decisions than it does for average and non-HIPO lieutenants." (p. 110)
- **Claim that commanders shape subordinates' careers** — "battalion commanders significantly influence their lieutenants' decision to stay in or leave the Army." (p. 101). "This finding infers that the battalion commander may be the most impactful factor in a typical lieutenant's decision to stay or go." (p. 107)
- **Outcome is retention, not promotion.** Promotion of lieutenants as an outcome: **NOT FOUND**. The paper cites Lyle & Smith for the claim that "battalion commander quality can predict the performance of junior officers" (p. 102).
- **Causal language.** The abstract and conclusion use causal-sounding words ("determinants," "influence," "shape"). The method is described as predictive: "This model enabled the study to measure each battalion commander's unique predictive effect on the retention decision of the average lieutenant who served under him or her." (p. 106). The paper makes no argument that lieutenants are assigned to commanders at random, and no identification strategy is stated (assignment mechanism: **NOT FOUND**). Its basis is sample size, controls, and a battalion dummy.
- **Limits (stated by the authors)** — "one could imagine a battalion commander who receives a cohort of lieutenants who are of unusually high or low quality or predisposed to want to stay in or leave the Army." (p. 113). They also list a strong economy and hard deployments as confounders, and good subordinate leaders who could raise a weak commander's score (p. 113). The article is a policy piece with no tables, model specification, or fit statistics beyond the adjusted-R² comparison.
- **Channel (assistant judgment).** The organization's choice of commander sets the environment. The outcome is exit, which shrinks the pool later selection draws from. The authors propose using the effect as an evaluation input for brigadier-general selection (pp. 111–112), which is their policy view, not a finding.

## 3. Evans & Robinson (2020) — "Evaluating Our Evaluations"

**Citation (as printed).** Lt. Col. Lee A. Evans, PhD, U.S. Army, and Lt. Col. G. Lee Robinson, PhD, U.S. Army, "Evaluating Our Evaluations: Recognizing and Countering Performance Evaluation Pitfalls," *Military Review*, January-February 2020, pp. 89–99. The USMA Digital Commons cover gives: "Evans, Lee A., and G. Lee Robinson. ... *Military Review* 100, no. 1 (January 2020): 89–99." **Author-order flag:** the printed byline lists **Evans first**, so the request's "Robinson & Evans" reverses the printed order. The file is a ResearchGate copy of the Digital Commons PDF, so printed p. = PDF p. + 86.

- **Design/data.** Essay plus an analytic calculation. It uses a hypergeometric model with officers assigned at random to rating pools, a population of 5,000 officers, and the top one-third deserving "most qualified" (pp. 90–91). No officer-level data are analyzed. The cited figures come from Army Regulation 623-3, Human Resources Command (HRC) guidance, and prior studies.
- **Why the senior rater matters: boards have little time** — "the senior rater comments are widely acknowledged to carry the most weight for promotion and selection decisions due to the small amount of time available to evaluate a soldier's file." (p. 89). "Since selection boards have little time to review files and consider a relatively minimal amount of information, reducing the effects of cognitive bias can make a difference in the identification and selection of officers" (p. 96)
- **Mentor link (citing Lyle & Smith)** — "Previous studies demonstrate that exposure to a high-quality mentor increases an officer's likelihood of an early promotion to major by 29 percent, perhaps because high-quality mentors are skilled at communicating their protégé's potential in their performance evaluations." (pp. 89–90). Their note 5 misprints Lyle & Smith's year as "April 2004" (p. 99).
- **Profile constraint (forced distribution)** — "DA Form 67-10 uses a forced distribution technique where senior raters of lieutenant colonels and below can award "most qualified" evaluations to fewer than half of their subordinates." (p. 90)
- **Pitfall 1: structure alone produces errors, especially in small pools** — "even under a best-case scenario (with the absence of cognitive biases), system structure induces error in a forced distribution performance appraisal system." (p. 90). "an often overlooked downside of this practice is the presence of errors resulting from a forced distribution system, especially in small rating pools." (p. 90)
- **An officer's rating depends on who else is in the pool** — "the rater may be obligated to award an evaluation that is not commensurate with a subordinate's level of performance due to forced distribution requirements. For example, if a rater has a pool size of five, but has more than two top one-third performers, at least one rated officer will receive an inaccurate evaluation due to the rater's profile constraint." (p. 91)
- **How large the structural error is** — "If five thousand officers are randomly placed into pools of five, even under conditions of perfect clarity [...] we would expect that 259 officers per year do not receive the evaluation they deserve." (p. 91). "Doubling the rating pool size from five to ten thus results in a 56 percent decrease in Type II errors." (p. 96)
- **Ratings also depend on which rater you get** — "senior raters who place these thresholds below those of other raters disadvantage some of their subordinates who would have received "most qualified" evaluations in other rating pools." (p. 92)
- **Timing/sequence: profiles carry over across years, and seniority matters** — "A conscious decision to award a "most qualified" evaluation to an undeserving officer can have compounding effects since rating profiles are cumulative." (p. 93). "recent research shows how an officer's seniority affects the evaluations they receive in the evaluation process." (p. 93)
- **Cognitive pitfalls named** — halo effects, first impression error, "similar to me" effect, central tendency error, and duration neglect (pp. 97–98).
- **Performance is hard to separate from the group** — "How much of performance is due to a person's talent versus the interactive effects from the group? And how does their performance compare to their peers who faced similar tasks but did so under different conditions with different teammates?" (p. 96)
- **Near the cutline** — "there is likely a small talent differential between officers just above and just below the cutline in promotion and selection boards." (p. 96)
- **"Center of mass" language: NOT FOUND** in this article. It uses the current DA Form 67-10 categories ("most qualified," "highly qualified"). Center of mass appears in Lyle & Smith (p. 235) and Evans & Bae (p. 192).
- **Causal language.** It makes no empirical causal claim. Its claims about structural error follow mathematically from the assumptions (random pools, perfect rater discernment). The cognitive-bias claims are framed as risks ("may affect," p. 98).
- **Limits.** No data. It assumes random allocation to pools and a known top one-third. The authors say the recommended pool size "cannot be generalized across nonhomogeneous units" (p. 93).
- **Channel (assistant judgment).** Evaluation/scoring. The organization (pool size, profile rule, rater) and the peers in the same pool both change an officer's rating for the same performance. This is a score effect, separate from the board's later selection.

## 4. Evans & Bae (2019) — simulation optimization of the Army appraisal system

**Citation (as printed).** Lee A Evans and Ki-Hwan G Bae, "US Army performance appraisal policy analysis: a simulation optimization approach," *Journal of Defense Modeling and Simulation: Applications, Methodology, Technology* 2019, Vol. 16(2) 191–205. © The Author(s) 2018 (online first). DOI 10.1177/1548512918787969. The PDF starts at the article, so printed p. = PDF p. + 190; p. 191 is unnumbered.

- **Design/data.** Discrete-event simulation (Simio) calibrated to HRC data "on majors who faced promotion boards in 2015 and 2016" (p. 195). Scope is limited to functional-area officers, who lack key developmental jobs (p. 196). There are 300 officers per year, each assigned a fixed true "performance percentile" Qi ~ Uniform(0,1) (p. 196). The median rating pool is 15 (p. 196). Raters rank by a seniority-weighted function and award top ratings up to the profile cap (p. 197). Each officer exits after five annual evaluations (p. 197). Ranking-and-selection optimization (Nelson-Swann-Goldsman-Song and Kim-Nelson procedures) searches 81 configurations of pool-change frequency, profile cap, pool size, and seniority weighting (p. 200).
- **Abstract: rating depends on more than performance** — "Aside from job performance, multiple factors contribute to the rating an individual receives in such systems. These factors include a rater's span of control (the number of subordinates being rated), the frequency at which individuals change raters, regulatory constraints pertaining to the number of top evaluations a rater can award, and the rater behavior." (p. 191)
- **Abstract headline** — "Our results show the potential of reducing the number of officers not receiving the number of above average evaluations commensurate with their performance level by as much as 24%." (p. 191)
- **Rating depends on who else is in the pool** — "These factors include the officer's initial performance percentile, the officer's seniority, the performance percentiles of other officers in the same rating pool, the seniority of other officers in the same rating pool, and the size of the rating pool." (p. 197)
- **Top ratings track promotion (2015–2016 lieutenant colonel boards)** — "Officers receiving three or more top evaluations were promoted at a rate well over 70%, whereas officers receiving two or fewer top evaluations were promoted less than 20% of the time." (p. 192). Table 1 rates for 0 through 5 top evaluations are 0.0, 2.3, 17.3, 73.7, 96.9, and 98.2% for 2015, and 0.0, 1.3, 12.7, 80.3, 98.4, and 100.0% for 2016 (p. 192).
- **History of the profile rule** — "From 2004 to 2011, DA Form 67-9 did not include a profile constraint for raters of officers in the rank of captain and below." (p. 192). The earlier form capped "above center of mass evaluations" at 49% (p. 192).
- **Pool size** — "increasing the median rating pool size from 10 to 15 resulted in a corresponding 8.53% decrease in misidentifications." (p. 198). "seven of the eight best system configurations had a median rating pool size of 15 officers. The larger rating pools provide a more uniform distribution of performance percentiles, which results in fewer misidentifications." (p. 201)
- **Profile constraint** — "increasing the profile constraint generally reduces the number of critical misidentifications." (p. 200). The best configuration has a 59% cap, a move every 12 months, a pool of 15, and less weight on seniority (p. 202). "The proposed system has a 20.87% decrease in critical misidentifications, but has nearly three times as many officers receiving five top evaluations." (p. 203)
- **Seniority/timing effect in rater behavior** — "over 70% of the officers in the current system receive a top evaluation in their final year as a major" (p. 203). The seniority function "makes no assumption regarding the extent to which the increase in probability of receiving a top evaluation is due to the natural performance improvement as a result of experience or rater behavior." (p. 196)
- **Challenges the "boards pick the best" assumption** — they say Dabkowski et al. "assumed that promotion boards always promote the most qualified officers, an assumption we challenge as a result of our model." (p. 194)
- **Rater error.** There is no separate random rater-noise parameter. Rater behavior enters only through the seniority-weighted sorting function (pp. 196, 199). A stand-alone rater-error parameter is **NOT FOUND**.
- **Whether the best officers end up promoted.** The model scores "misidentifications" (top-evaluation counts that do not match the true percentile, pp. 197–198). Promotion itself is not simulated, but "critical misidentifications" are defined by the promotion cutoff in Table 1 (three or more top evaluations) (p. 198).
- **Causal language.** All results are simulated consequences of the assumed model. They are not estimates of effects in data. Validation is described as a match to pool-size moments and seniority patterns (pp. 196–197).
- **Limits** — "the stated results and conclusions are estimates based on functional area officers, a subset of Army officers, who faced promotion boards in 2015 and 2016." (p. 203). True performance is a fixed uniform percentile with no development over time (p. 196). One typo in the source: p. 200 lists the profile levels as "39%, 49%, or 50%," but Constraint (3) on p. 199 gives 0.39, 0.49, 0.59.
- **Channel (assistant judgment).** Evaluation/scoring, with peers (pool composition) and organization (cap, pool size, rotation) both moving the score. Selection is represented only through the empirical rule that three or more top evaluations are needed.

## 5. Wardynski, Lyle & Colarusso (2010) — retaining talent

**Citation (as printed).** Casey Wardynski, David S. Lyle, and Michael J. Colarusso, *Towards a U.S. Army Officer Corps Strategy for Success: Retaining Talent* (Carlisle, PA: Strategic Studies Institute, U.S. Army War College, January 2010). ISBN 1-58487-425-2. "This monograph is the third in a series of six" (p. ii). Front matter is numbered ii–vi; the body starts at p. 1 = PDF p. 7, so printed p. = PDF p. − 6.

- **Design/data.** Policy monograph by the Office of Economic and Manpower Analysis (OEMA) at USMA. It presents descriptive retention rates by commissioning source and by procurement program (scholarship type) across year groups from the late 1970s on, plus projections such as Year Groups 1991–2002 in Appendix A. There is no regression or causal design. N is not reported as a single figure (**NOT FOUND**).
- **High performers are among those leaving** — "Since the late 1980s, however, prospects for the Officer Corps' future have been darkened by an ever-diminishing return on this investment, as evidenced by plummeting company-grade officer retention rates. Significantly, this leakage includes a large share of high-performing officers, many of them developed via a fully-funded undergraduate education." (p. v)
- **Retention fell from about 60% to 40%** — "about 60 percent of officers commissioned in the late 1970s via Reserve Officer Training Corps (ROTC) and West Point scholarship programs remained on active duty through 8 years of service. [...] By the mid 1980s, however, only 40 percent of officers being commissioned from these scholarship sources remained on active duty through 8 years of service." (p. 10)
- **Scholarship officers leave at the highest rates** — "West Point and ROTC's 3- and 4-year scholarship officers have remained in the Army at about two-thirds to half the rate of OCS officers from the ranks and ROTC officers without scholarships." (p. 13). The authors add a caveat: "we are not arguing that scholarship officers are more talented than others" (p. 14)
- **Service obligations (USMA and ROTC)** — "Contingent upon their commissioning source, all newly commissioned officers incur an ADSO of 3 to 5 years. More than half of each year group's 20-year attrition rate occurs within 6 months of completing an ADSO. Since the mid-1990s, for example, only 55 percent of West Point graduates, who incur a 5-year service obligation, remain on active duty to 5 1/2 years of service." (p. 23)
- **Within USMA, the highest-ability cadets stay longer** — "Based upon College Board scores and cadet order-of-merit standing, those USMA cadets with the highest potential and performance as an undergraduate remain in the Army to their 10th year of service at higher rates than cadets exhibiting lower potential and performance." (pp. 52–53). **Note:** a claim that high-SAT officers leave at higher rates is **NOT FOUND**. On this point the source says the opposite within USMA.
- **The Army cannot hire laterally** — "in its core warfighting competencies, the Army cannot "buy" talent from outside." (p. 3). "there will always be significant limits on its ability to buy talent from outside." (p. 3). "Because the Army must necessarily limit lateral entry, it will always retain some of the hierarchical and bureaucratic elements of the company man era." (p. 10). For officers past 20 years of service: "Since lateral entry into the Army at this point is, of course, impossible, the Army must diligently guard against talent leakage." (p. 25)
- **The cohort only shrinks: no verbatim statement found (NOT FOUND).** The closest statements are: "we view officers as talented people moving across time through a funnel-shaped pipeline (see Figure 7)" (p. 16); and that the Army estimates "accessions required in subsequent cohorts to backfill entry level vacancies" (p. 38).
- **Consequence for selection** — low retention "strips away the Army's ability to screen, vet, and cull for talent, forcing it instead to over-access, increase promotion rates and compress promotion timing." (pp. v–vi). "Rising promotion rates reduced Army opportunities to vet officers for advancement." (p. 15)
- **Evaluation link** — "Eliminating forced distribution ratings made it extremely difficult to distinguish high-potential officers from the others" (p. 18; refers to the 2004 change for lieutenants and captains).
- **Retention programs** — Career Satisfaction Program Retention Bonus (CSRB): "With a cost to taxpayers of $500 million, there is no evidence that it improved retention." (p. 26). Officer Career Satisfaction Program (OCSP), a projection rather than an observed result: it "stands to increase 8-year continuation rates from 47 percent to above 69 percent" (p. 30)
- **Causal language.** The causal claims are asserted from descriptive trends and theory (time inconsistency, opportunity cost, market failure). For example, the Army's failure to create an internal talent market "in large part accounts for declining retention among officers commissioned since 1983." (p. 10). There is no identification strategy.
- **Limits.** The monograph is advocacy and policy. All three authors are at OEMA, and Wardynski is credited with "creating the concepts for [...] the Army's pre-commissioning retention incentives" (p. iv), the programs the monograph evaluates. Rates are shown in figures without standard errors. "High-performing" is not defined with a measure in the summary passage (p. v).
- **Channel (assistant judgment).** Organization-level: exit shrinks and reshapes the pool available for later selection, and no lateral entry means the pool cannot be refilled at mid-career.

## 6. Spain, Lin & Young (2020) — early predictors among West Point cadets

**Citation (as printed).** Everett S. Spain, Eric Lin, and Lissa V. Young, "Early predictors of successful military careers among West Point cadets," *Military Psychology*, 2020, Vol. 32, No. 6, 389–407. DOI 10.1080/08995605.2020.1801285. Received 12 Feb 2019; accepted 29 May 2020; published online 16 Nov 2020. Taylor & Francis cover page, so printed p. = PDF p. + 387.

- **Design/data.** "all of the 11,975 US graduates of the 13 West Point classes from 1992 to 2004 who remained in the Army long enough to be considered for at least early promotion to the rank of major (N = 5,505)." (p. 391). The later outcomes have smaller samples: 1,559 considered for early promotion to lieutenant colonel and 1,289 considered for battalion command (p. 392). Logistic regressions of three outcomes on SAT (SATTOT), academic GPA (AGPA), military GPA (MGPA), and physical GPA (PGPA), with controls for deployments, graduation year, branch, gender, recruited athlete, and prep school. The outcomes are early promotion to major (EPM, i.e., below the zone), early promotion to lieutenant colonel (EPL), and selection for battalion command (BC). A Heckman probit checks for selection into staying (pp. 401–403).
- **Abstract** — "regression analyses indicate that cadet military grade point average surpasses both cognitive ability and academic performance by a considerable margin in the ability to predict future professional outcomes such as selection for early promotion or battalion command. Moreover, these differences in predicting managerial career outcomes endure over 16 years." (p. 389)
- **Military GPA predicts strongly** — "a 1-point increase in MGPA was associated with a 576% increase in the odds of being selected for EPM (p ≤ 0.01)." (p. 399). "Moving one standard deviation below to one standard deviation above the mean MGPA corresponded approximately to moving from a 2.8 to 3.5 MGPA. This equated to moving from a 3.5% to a 13.2% predicted probability of EPM." (p. 399). For battalion command: "In Model 7 (BC), the coefficient of 3.44 was also both substantively and statistically significant." (p. 400)
- **Academic GPA predicts early but not late outcomes** — "a 1-point increase in AGPA is associated with a 63% (p ≤ 0.05) increased odds of being selected for EPM." (p. 397). "AGPA positively predicts future officer performance 7 to 10 years after West Point graduation but not 14 to 16 years after graduation." (p. 399)
- **SAT is a negative predictor** — "a 100-point increase in a cadet's SAT score (which is approximately one standard deviation) was associated with an 8% decrease in odds of being promoted early to major." (p. 396). "This SATTOT change equated to moving from an 11.53% predicted probability of early promotion to major to a 6.5% predicted probability." (p. 397). For battalion command the SAT odds ratio is 0.71, significant at p < .01 (p. 396)
- **Military GPA is itself rating-like** — "since the MGPA is created through cadet performance reports which are similar in style, authorship, and frequency to the OERs they will later receive as Army officers, the MGPA may be more of an early career outcome than a measure of cadet military performance." (p. 403)
- **Selection into the sample** — "Naturally, retention drives correlations between early promotions and accession to general officership, since only those who remain in the Army are considered for either." (p. 392). "Those who are selected for promotions are already filtered for strong talent, increasing the relative predictive power of other attributes." (p. 404)
- **Causal language.** The paper's language is predictive: predictors, associations, odds ratios. It occasionally uses causal wording ("how MGPA drives career success," p. 399) but makes no causal claim and states that "it does not empirically examine the reasons for the differences." (p. 405)
- **Limits** — "we acknowledge studies like this are vulnerable to selection effects (bias), since retention in the Army to the point of being considered for promotion is not a random process. Though we have employed several different tests to account for such selection effects, their cumulative results are inconclusive." (p. 405). **Internal tension to flag:** p. 403 says the Heckman test "indicated the self-selection bias did not materially affect our estimated results," while p. 405 calls the cumulative results "inconclusive." Other limits: SAT range restriction at USMA (p. 403), and outcomes that are nested in one another (EPL and BC require earlier promotions) (p. 404). Only USMA graduates are studied.
- **Channel (assistant judgment).** The predictor (MGPA) is partly an earlier evaluation by the same kind of raters, and the outcomes are selection events. The study cannot separate a person's development from persistence in how they are scored.

## 7. Dabkowski, Huddleston, Kucik & Lyle (2010) — attrition and the flow of talent

**Citation (as printed).** Matthew F. Dabkowski, Samuel H. Huddleston, Paul Kucik, and David Lyle, "Shaping Senior Leader Officer Talent: How Personnel Management Decisions and Attrition Impact the Flow of Army Officer Talent Throughout the Officer Career Model," in *Proceedings of the 2010 Winter Simulation Conference*, ed. B. Johansson, S. Jain, J. Montoya-Torres, J. Hugan, and E. Yücesan, pp. 1407–1417. IEEE, 978-1-4244-9864-2/10. ResearchGate cover page, so printed p. = PDF p. + 1405.

- **Design/data.** Discrete-event (Monte Carlo) simulation of one cohort, Year Group 1986 (YG86), using that cohort's actual attrition and promotion counts at each rank (pp. 1410–1411). It assumes one-dimensional talent ~ Normal(50, 13), fixed for life (p. 1411), and promotion boards that "perform flawlessly" (p. 1411). Three departure scenarios: no attrition, uniform attrition (independent of talent), and higher talent leaving more (pp. 1411–1413). Motivating data: YG1987 West Point graduates by order-of-merit quartile (Figure 2, p. 1408); 100 replications (p. 1413).
- **Abstract** — "The demand by corporations for these talents, coupled with a distinct feature of the Officer Career Model, limited lateral entry, create significant retention challenges for the U.S. Army. [...] This analysis employs discrete event simulation to quantify the probable impacts of attrition on the distribution of talent available for service across the Army's officer ranks." (p. 1407)
- **No lateral entry** — "Unlike industry, which can poach mid-career and senior-level leaders from other firms, the Army must build its mid-career and senior leaders out of the lieutenants that it commissions each year." (p. 1407)
- **West Point order of merit and retention** — "retention rates are relatively uniform across the order of merit quartiles." (p. 1408). The figure shows YG1987 retention at 19 and 22 years and promotion to colonel.
- **Policy shift in selectivity** — "throughout the 1980s and the early 1990s, the Army only promoted roughly 70 percent of its officers to the rank of Major and 60 percent of its officers to the rank of Lieutenant Colonel." (p. 1410). "Low retention that caused the shortage in Majors (Figure 1) has forced the Army to increase promotion rates above 90 percent through the rank of Lieutenant Colonel over much of the last decade." (p. 1410)
- **Attrition means boards choose from what is left** — "The promotion boards continue to promote the best officers presented to them in each round of boards, but, because attrition now begins to take effect, the boards are now promoting "the best of what's left."" (p. 1412)
- **Effect sizes (simulated)** — Under uniform attrition: "With an average score of 62, the average Colonel is roughly one standard deviation above the mean and at about the 82nd percentile." (p. 1413). Under higher-talent-leaves-more: "the average Colonel is only slightly above average (talent score of 52) and the worst Colonel promoted is at the 29th percentile (talent score 43)." (p. 1413)
- **The board matters less than attrition** — "when we simulate the cohort using historical attrition rates at each rank, we notice that the promotion board for Colonel has little impact on the metrics." (p. 1413). Table 1: the mean colonel talent is 52.76, "an improvement of 4.79 over the boarded cohort," and "could have been 56.91 if there had been no attrition" (p. 1414). "Attrition for YG86 exceeded 30% between promotion to Lieutenant Colonel and the board files for the Colonel promotion board." (p. 1414)
- **Policies considered** — (1) hold the colonel board before retirement eligibility; (2) access higher talent; (3) "flip the attrition model so that the most talented are the least likely to depart," which moves the mean colonel "from 62 (82nd percentile) to 69 (93rd percentile)" (p. 1415).
- **Causal language.** The results are model consequences ("quantify (in relative terms)," p. 1410). The talent measure is explicitly not real: "While the notion of measuring officer talent is entirely conceptual at this point" (p. 1410). The authors place reality between two scenarios: "The truth of the matter in the Army today almost surely lies somewhere between the very negative picture presented in the Higher Talent Greater Departure scenario and the very conservative Uniform Departure scenario." (p. 1414)
- **Limits** — "One possible limitation of this paper is the use of a single measure for talent." (p. 1416). The model assumes perfect boards and fixed talent, so it has no development and no rating error (p. 1411); Evans & Bae (2019, p. 194) explicitly challenge the perfect-board assumption. One internal inconsistency: the average colonel moves "from the 59th to the 70th percentile" in the text (p. 1414), but "from the 56th percentile to the 70th percentile" in the Figure 7 caption (p. 1415).
- **Channel (assistant judgment).** Selection only, with no evaluation error and no development. The organization's exit pattern and timing rules shape who is available to be selected.

## 8. Peck (1994) — career mobility of Army officers, 1950–1974

**Citation (as printed).** B. Mitchell Peck, "Assessing the Career Mobility of U.S. Army Officers: 1950-1974," *Armed Forces & Society*, Vol. 20, No. 2, Winter 1994, pp. 217–237. JSTOR stable 45346577. JSTOR cover page, so printed p. = PDF p. + 215. The article has **no abstract**.

- **Design/data.** "A six-wave panel design was used to gather data for the analysis. Data were collected for 2165 U.S. Army officers on active duty between 1950 and 1974 from biographical entries in the U.S. Army Register." (p. 224). This is a random sample of 2,000 officers on active duty in 1950 (1,777 retained), with follow-ups in 1955, 1960, 1965, 1970, and 1974 and new entrants added each wave (p. 224). The tables are multivariate models of promotion to each rank; the specific estimator is not restated in the quoted pages. Awards and decorations stand in for performance because "Individual officer's OER's are not available for the analysis." (p. 222)
- **Main finding: little academy advantage below general** — "That academy graduates have little or no evident mobility advantage before promotion to the general ranks is quite important." (p. 233). "the findings reported here suggest that there is little evidence that officers receive consistent, systematic preferential consideration for promotion based on source of commission." (p. 233)
- **Institutional experience matters** — "Years of service, military education, and commission cohort positively affect the likelihood of promotion." (p. 233)
- **Causal language.** The paper speaks of factors that "affect" promotion and tests the competing "academy" and "institutional experience" hypotheses, but it has no identification strategy. Effect sizes are in tables not quoted here.
- **Limits** — "the sample consists of only those officers who chose to remain in the service and survived previous screening processes." (p. 225). "Because of the limitations of the data, the results offered are tentative." (p. 234). The period is before the all-volunteer force, and "the findings may not be relevant to the present promotion process." (p. 224)

## 9. Baldwin (1996) — promotion record and glass ceilings

**Citation (as printed in the JSTOR header of the cached text).** J. Norman Baldwin, "The Promotion Record of the United States Army: Glass Ceilings in the Officer Corps," *Public Administration Review*, Mar.–Apr. 1996, Vol. 56, No. 2, pp. 199–206. JSTOR stable 977208. **Source flag:** `Zotero/storage/DE62RML4/` contains only `.zotero-ft-cache` (no PDF). Page numbers are inferred from running footers in the cache ("Public Administration Review . March/April 1996, Vol. 56, No. 2 199," etc.) and could not be checked with `pdftotext`.

- **Design/data.** Aggregate promotion counts from the Army Office of Deputy Chief of Staff for Personnel, Officer Division, for officers considered for promotion to captain, major, lieutenant colonel, and colonel, 1980–1993 (Table 1 title, p. 201): "more than 123,000" officers (p. 199). The analysis is descriptive. It compares promotion rates, cumulative and noncumulative, against the "Uniform Guidelines" 80% rule (p. 204).
- **Abstract finding** — "The data indicate women and minorities are underrepresented in the Army's middle officer ranks, female officers are promoted at lower rates than male officers, and minority officers are promoted at lower rates than majority officers." (p. 199). "However, noncumulative promotion rates for women and minorities generally do not indicate adverse effects, and male and Caucasian promotion rates decrease over time more than female and minority promotion rates." (p. 199)
- **Boards** — "Board members typically do not know the officers they evaluate." (p. 200)
- **Conclusion** — "Overall, from a conservative perspective, the Army's promotion record does not indicate problems." (p. 205)
- **Causal language.** It makes no causal claim, and the explanations offered are speculative ("most likely involve socioeconomic, educational, cultural, and institutional factors," p. 204–205).
- **Limits.** The data are aggregate, not individual. A note in the source says that "As much as half of the study's data for any one particular rank does not reflect promotion outcomes for officers included in a preceding rank" (p. 205, n. 4). Pages are inferred, with no PDF.

## Assistant synthesis (ASSISTANT SYNTHESIS, not source text)

1. **Only one study claims a causal effect of who a person serves under.** Lyle & Smith argue that Army assignment makes the match with a battalion commander as good as random on observables, and they estimate +2.7 points, or 29%, on below-the-zone promotion to major (pp. 232, 242–243). They do not separate development from rating, and they note that the mentor writes the protégé's evaluation (p. 248). Spain, Mukunda & Bates find commander identity predicts lieutenant retention but have no assignment argument (pp. 106–107).
2. **Ratings depend on the pool, not only on the person.** Evans & Robinson (pp. 91–92) and Evans & Bae (p. 197) both state that an officer's rating depends on how many strong officers share the rating pool, the pool size, the profile cap, and the rater. The Evans & Robinson result is analytic and the Evans & Bae result is simulated, with no officer-level estimate. Top-rating counts sharply separate who is promoted to lieutenant colonel (Evans & Bae, Table 1, p. 192).
3. **Selection happens on a pool that only exit and accession change.** Wardynski et al. (p. 3, p. 25) and Dabkowski et al. (p. 1407) state there is no lateral entry at mid-career. Dabkowski et al. simulate that attrition, more than the board, sets the talent of colonels (pp. 1413–1414), but only under assumed perfect boards.
4. **The "best leave" claim is mixed in these sources.** Wardynski et al. say scholarship officers leave more (p. 13), but within USMA the highest-scoring cadets stay *longer* (pp. 52–53). Dabkowski et al. show YG1987 USMA retention "relatively uniform" across order-of-merit quartiles (p. 1408).
5. **Early ratings predict later selection, and the predictor is itself a rating.** In Spain, Lin & Young, military GPA (built from cadet performance reports like OERs) strongly predicts early promotion and battalion command, while SAT is negative (pp. 396–400, 403). The authors flag selection into staying as unresolved (p. 405).

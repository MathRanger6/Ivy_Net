## ASSISTANT SYNTHESIS

*(All points are Assistant judgment, written after all six sources were read.)*

1. **Psychology and management have a strong prior that "good things" turn bad at high levels.** Grant & Schwartz (2011, p. 62: "There is no such thing as an unmitigated good") and Pierce & Aguinis (2013, p. 313: "all seemingly monotonic positive relations reach context-specific inflection points") state it as a general principle. Swaab et al. (2014) carry it to peer environments: too large a share of top-talent teammates was said to lower team performance in interdependent sports. The dissertation should present this prior as a hypothesis to test, not an assumption to build in.

2. **The flagship peer-talent inverted U does not survive a valid test.** Swaab et al. relied on a significant negative quadratic term. With the two-lines test, Simonsohn (2018, p. 553) found the soccer high-talent slope "also positive (albeit far from significant)." Gula et al. (2021, pp. 301–302) found the NBA high-talent slope flat on Swaab's own 10 seasons (b = 0.09, p = .743) and positive and significant on 64 seasons (b = 0.31, p = .013). Gula et al. conclude "We did not observe any case in which increased talent was detrimental to team success" (p. 301). The defensible empirical claim is diminishing returns, not a downturn.

3. **The method is the main problem with the TMGT literature, and it biases toward finding inverted Us.** A quadratic fit "is unable to generate a long plateau" and so produces "a spurious sign change" when the truth is concave and flattening (Simonsohn 2018, p. 539). Pierce & Aguinis recommend exactly that test and sign rule (pp. 326–327). Grant & Schwartz advise designing studies "to maximize the probability of identifying inverted-U relationships" (p. 71). Gula et al. extend the concern to the whole literature: TMGT evidence "may constitute a mere method artifact" (p. 303). This licenses treating curve shape as an empirical question and setting a minimum test standard: two lines, or explicit model comparison against monotone concave forms (log, power).

4. **Even the TMGT authors admit there are other possible shapes.** Warr's "vitamin" model distinguishes shapes that turn negative from shapes that flatten (Grant & Schwartz, p. 71). Pierce & Aguinis count asymptotes as TMGT, "In either case" (p. 317), which makes their meta-theory hard to falsify. Grant & Schwartz's two-process account (benefits that "satiate" plus costs that "escalate," p. 66; McGuire's "two opposed mediating processes, both monotonic," p. 69) is the most useful conceptual piece: the shape depends on how two monotone curves compare, so the peak can exist, move, or disappear across settings. That is a reason to estimate the shape, not to assume it.

5. **The relative age effect (RAE) is this set's clearest case of selection among competitors for scarce slots, and it is organizational, not just peer-driven.** An administrative cutoff date fixes the comparison pool. Coaches pick from that pool for "limited places on a team" (Musch & Grondin 2001, p. 159). Selection then feeds development ("better coaching and facing better opponents," p. 159), evaluation (older children "erroneously perceived as the most talented," p. 158), and dropout. Natural experiments support the cutoff as the cause, for example the Australian cutoff change (p. 154). The 15-places-versus-150-applicants thought experiment (p. 154) is in effect a top-K selection model: the effect should grow as the number of slots shrinks relative to the pool.

6. **What the set leaves open for selection:**
   - None of the curve-shape studies uses selection as the outcome. Swaab, Gula, Grant & Schwartz, and Pierce & Aguinis all model team or individual *performance*. [outcome is not selection]
   - The RAE literature has selection but no formal or fitted model. The pool-size principle is only a thought experiment, and the authors concede no "direct evidence that the level of competition directly determines the magnitude of the RAE" (p. 154). [no model of mechanism] [model not fit to data]
   - RAE mechanisms are rarely measured, and selection, development, and dropout are mixed together in one birthdate distribution (pp. 156, 162). [imperfect measure]
   - The evidence comes from a few sports leagues with thin high-talent tails. In Gula et al.'s 64-season set, only 13 team-seasons lie beyond the quadratic peak (p. 302). [small data] [single system]

7. **Implication for the dissertation's framing.** Taken together, these sources motivate a selection model in which (a) the organization sets the comparison pool and the number of slots, and (b) peers shape both the score and its noise. The curve linking environment to the chance of selection would then be *derived and fit*, not assumed. The test would be a valid shape test (two lines or model comparison), with selection itself as the outcome. Pierce & Aguinis's warning that selection truncates samples (range restriction, p. 328) also applies: data on those already selected cannot show the shape of the selection curve.

## 1. Swaab, Schaerer, Anicich, Ronay & Galinsky (2014) — The Too-Much-Talent Effect

**Citation.** Swaab, R. I., Schaerer, M., Anicich, E. M., Ronay, R., & Galinsky, A. D. (2014). The too-much-talent effect: Team interdependence determines when more talent is too much or not enough. *Psychological Science, 25*(8), 1581–1591. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614537280

**Page mapping.** Printed p. = PDF p. + 1580 (PDF p. 1 = p. 1581).

### Data / setting / sample
- **Study 1a (lay beliefs, firm):** "Thirty-seven participants (21 men; mean age = 33.92 years, SD = 11.75) predicted the success of a firm on the basis of the firm's percentage of top-talented employees." (p. 1583) Ratings at 10%, 20%, … 100% top talent.
- **Study 1b (lay beliefs, team selection task):** "37 participants (25 men; mean age = 32.49 years, SD = 10.16) assumed the role of a national football (soccer) team manager and selected the combination of players that would maximize their team's chances of winning an international competition." (p. 1583) Two failed an attention check and were omitted.
- **Study 2 (FIFA national teams):** 2010 and 2014 World Cup qualification periods; 207 and 209 national teams; Table 2 "n = 415" (p. 1585). Outcome = averaged FIFA ranking points.
- **Study 3 (NBA):** "10 most recent seasons (2002–2012) [...] a total of 297 team-level observations" (p. 1585); "4,285 player-level observations" (p. 1585). Outcome = end-of-year win percentage.
- **Study 4 (MLB):** "30 MLB teams over the 10 most recent seasons (2002–2012) [...] a total of 300 team-level observations" (p. 1588); "13,750 player-level observations" (p. 1588).

### Talent measures
- **Football:** "Top talent was indexed by the percentage of players within each national team who had contracts with one of the world's elite club teams. To assess elite status, we used the Deloitte Football Money League ranking of clubs by revenue generated from football activity" (p. 1583). Validated against the FIFA 2010 All-Star team (p. 1584).
- **NBA:** "we coded top talent using the estimated wins added (EWA) for all individual players" (p. 1585); "we coded whether a player was in the top third (33.3%) of the overall cohort (1) or not (0)" (p. 1585). "Ninety-nine percent of the players selected for All-Star games during the 10 seasons were coded as top talent in our sample." (p. 1585)
- **MLB:** "We identified top talent using wins above replacement (WAR)" (p. 1588); same top-third coding; "Eighty percent of the players who participated in All-Star games during the 10 seasons were coded as top talent." (p. 1588)
- **Why a share, not a mean:** "Our theory suggests that it is the concentration of top talent that matters for coordination and performance. Thus, we did not use a mean EWA measure for each team because it does not accurately reflect the concentration of top talent" (p. 1585).

### Design
- Five studies: two surveys + three archival (abstract, p. 1581).
- **Study 2 estimation:** "We used generalized estimating equations with country as the subject variable and qualification period as the time variable in a mixed regression method (Tweedie with a log link)" (p. 1584).
- **Studies 3–4 estimation:** "We used fixed-effects linear regressions of panel data, with team as the panel variable and season as the time variable" (p. 1586). Controls: roster size, games played, lagged performance, free-throw percentage (NBA).
- **Curvilinear test:** quadratic term ("Talent-squared") added in Model 2 of Tables 2, 4, 5, 7 (pp. 1585–1589). Outlier robustness by Cook's distance, "values larger than 4/n" (p. 1584).
- **Mediator (NBA):** "We used a three-item measure of on-court performance to quantify intrateam coordination" — assists per game, field-goal percentage, defensive rebounds per game; "We standardized and averaged these three measures (α = .63)." (pp. 1585–1586)

### Main findings (verbatim)
- **Core claim:** "we argue that more talent often facilitates team performance—but only up to a point. Beyond this point, the marginal benefits of more talent will decrease and eventually turn into detriments." (p. 1581)
- **Abstract summary:** "Three archival studies revealed that the too-much-talent effect emerged when team members were interdependent (football and basketball) but not independent (baseball). Our basketball analysis also established the mediating role of team coordination. When teams need to come together, more talent can tear them apart." (p. 1581)
- **Lay beliefs:** "Studies 1a and 1b demonstrate that people believe that more top talent increases team performance and reveal this belief in their selection decisions. Moreover, participants expected that the effect of talent would never turn negative." (p. 1583) Study 1b: "On average, 9.74 (SD = 1.85) top-talent players were selected for the 11 positions (88.55%)." (p. 1583)
- **Football:** "Study 2 also revealed a significant quadratic effect of top talent: Top talent benefited performance only up to a point, after which the marginal benefit of talent decreased and turned negative (Table 2, Model 2; Fig. 2)." (p. 1584) Table 2 Model 2: Talent 4.58*** (0.11), Talent-squared –4.26*** (0.49) (p. 1585).
- **NBA:** "the linear relationship between talent and team performance was positive and significant (Table 4, Model 1), but only up to a point, after which the marginal benefit of talent decreased and the slope eventually turned negative (Table 4, Model 2; Fig. 3)." (p. 1586) Table 4 Model 2: Talent 1.61*** (0.42), Talent-squared –1.83** (0.56) (p. 1586).
- **Mediation:** "A Sobel test revealed significant mediation by intrateam coordination (Sobel's Z = 2.93, p < .01; see Fig. 5)." (p. 1587); "These analyses demonstrate that teams with levels of top talent that are too high perform worse than teams with lower levels of top talent because they coordinate less effectively." (p. 1587)
- **MLB (null for downturn):** "we found a significant linear relationship between top talent and team performance (Table 7, Models 1 and 2) but no curvilinear effect (Fig. 6)." (p. 1588); "Although the curvilinear relationship was significant when control variables were omitted (b = −0.49, SE = 0.24, p = .05), the effect of talent on team performance never turned negative." (p. 1588)
- **Selection implication drawn by authors:** "The current data suggest that selecting fewer top-talent players may produce a better team." (p. 1590)
- **Closing:** "organizational architects should be wary that too much top talent can produce diminishing marginal returns and even decrease performance by hindering intrateam coordination." (p. 1590)

### Mechanism or model
- **Verbal mechanism — status conflict → coordination loss:** "teams with too many dominant individuals produce disputes over within-group authority and status that ultimately undermine performance (Bendersky & Hays, 2012)." (p. 1582); "Status competition within teams can lead individuals to focus their attention on jostling for intragroup rank rather than on directing their efforts toward coordination and team performance" (p. 1582); "In the absence of a clearly defined pecking order, energy that would normally be steered toward intrateam coordination and performance is diverted toward jockeying for dominance." (p. 1582)
- **Moderator — task interdependence:** "when task interdependence is low, each individual's talent contributes additively to the team's outcome (Frank, 1985), and thus less coordination among team members is required." (p. 1582)
- **Formal?** No. Verbal theory + regression with a quadratic term + Sobel mediation. No generative model of how talent share maps to coordination. Status conflict itself is not measured: "Future research could also more directly explore whether status conflicts underlie the too-much-talent effect" (p. 1589).
- **Fit to data?** Only the regression specification is fit; no mechanistic model is fit. **(Assistant judgment)**

### Curve-shape test and validity by Simonsohn's standard (Assistant judgment)
- Test = significance of a negative quadratic term ("Talent-squared"), plus visual plots (Figs. 2, 3, 6). No two-lines / interrupted regression, no test that the slope is significantly negative on the high side of the peak.
- By Simonsohn (2018), a significant quadratic term does not establish a downturn; it is consistent with a monotone concave relation. The "turned negative" claim therefore rests on extrapolation of the quadratic. **Both archival downturns have been reanalyzed with the two-lines test and neither survives:** Simonsohn (2018, p. 553) reanalyzed the **soccer** result (Study 2) — second slope positive, not significant (see §6); Gula et al. (2021) reanalyzed the **NBA** result (Study 3) — second slope flat on the original 10 seasons, positive and significant on 64 seasons (see §2).
- The authors' own MLB result illustrates the ambiguity: a significant quadratic (no controls, p = .05) that they interpret as "never turned negative" (p. 1588) — i.e., they themselves treat a significant quadratic as compatible with no downturn.

### Limitations (tagged)
- **[imperfect measure]** Talent = binary top-third cutoff (NBA/MLB) or elite-club contract share (football), aggregated to a team percentage; coordination index has α = .63 (p. 1586), defended in note 2 (p. 1590). Football "talent" partly reflects club revenue (p. 1583).
- **[imperfect measure]** The proposed mechanism (status conflict) is not measured; the mediator is box-score statistics that are themselves components of winning (Assistant judgment: assists, FG%, rebounds are mechanically linked to win percentage).
- **[no model of mechanism]** Verbal status-conflict account only; authors concede it is not directly tested (p. 1589).
- **[model not fit to data]** No mechanistic model fit; quadratic regression only.
- **[single system]** Interdependence is inferred by comparing sports, not manipulated: "Although we inferred effects of task interdependence by comparing results for football and basketball with those for baseball, future research could manipulate task interdependence directly" (p. 1589). Generalization beyond sport is predicted, not shown (p. 1589).
- **[small data]** Team-level n = 297 (NBA), 300 (MLB) observations from ~30 teams; few teams sit at the extreme high-talent end where the downturn is claimed (Assistant judgment, consistent with Gula et al. 2021).
- **[outcome is not selection]** Outcome is team performance (rank points, win %), not who gets a roster slot. The selection problem appears only as lay behavior in Study 1b and as the authors' advice (p. 1590).
- Data embargo: "there is a 3-year embargo on this data posting" (p. 1590).

### Channel and source (Assistant judgment)
- **Channel:** primarily a team-output/EVALUATION story (how team composition converts to measured performance); implies a SELECTION lever (whom a coach should select), but selection is not modeled. Some DEVELOPMENT flavor is absent — no individual trajectories.
- **Source:** PEERS (teammate talent composition) moderated by ORGANIZATION-level task structure (interdependence of the sport).
- **Use in intro:** canonical "peer talent can hurt" claim; pair immediately with §2 and §6 to show the downturn is contested and that curve shape is an empirical question.

## 2. Gula, Vaci, Alexandrowicz & Bilalić (2021) — Never Too Much (Commentary on Swaab et al., 2014)

**Citation.** Gula, B., Vaci, N., Alexandrowicz, R. W., & Bilalić, M. (2021). Never too much—The benefit of talent to team performance in the National Basketball Association: Comment on Swaab, Schaerer, Anicich, Ronay, and Galinsky (2014). *Psychological Science, 32*(2), 301–304. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620960396 (Commentary; received 7/25/16, revision accepted 8/13/20.)

**Page mapping.** Printed p. = PDF p. + 300 (PDF p. 1 = p. 301).

### Data / setting / sample
- **(a) Replication data:** "we replicated the 10 seasons of the NBA data from Swaab et al. (2014, Study 3) using their described procedure." (p. 301) Fig. 1a: "10 regular NBA seasons (2002–2012; N = 297; from ESPN)" (p. 302).
- **(b) Extended data:** "a larger data set spanning 64 NBA seasons from 1955–1956 to 2018–2019 (www.basketball-reference.com) and including 1,417 data points." (p. 302) Fig. 1b caption: "64 seasons (1956–2019; N = 1,417 [...])" (p. 302).
- Talent = ratio of top-talented players by estimated wins added (EWA), as in Swaab et al. (Fig. 1 caption, p. 302). Outcome = winning proportion.
- Scope: basketball only, described as "the only domain in which the TMT effect has been shown" (p. 301), with note 1 pointing to the Nelson & Simonsohn (2014) Data Colada discussion, Gelman (2016), and the Swaab et al. response (p. 303).

### Design
- Three approaches: (i) replicate the quadratic; (ii) interrupted regression / two-lines test per Simonsohn (2018); (iii) compare alternative monotone functional forms (power, log, logistic) against quadratic.
- Robustness (Supplemental Material): alternative talent cutoffs, Swaab's control variables, separate rule-change periods, other NBA skill metrics (p. 303).
- Open data and R code: "https://osf.io/hfb6r" (p. 303). Not preregistered (p. 303).

### Main findings (verbatim)
- **Headline:** "We demonstrate that Swaab et al.'s evidence of TMT is based on an inappropriate approach to testing the inverse-U-shaped relation. The results demonstrate that the common belief among laypeople (Swaab et al., 2014 Study 1) is actually correct—teams generally benefit from more talented members although the benefits decrease marginally. We did not observe any case in which increased talent was detrimental to team success." (p. 301)
- **Why the quadratic misleads:** "If the quadratic function has a positive linear coefficient (which describes the initial improvement) and a negative quadratic coefficient, the latter will continuously force the fitting curve to bend and eventually continue downward. That is why it is important not only that the quadratic term is significant but also that the inflection point at which benefits turn into detriments is well within the observed talent range [...]. When the data beyond the estimated inflection point (maximum of the curve) are sparse, or the true relationship toward the end of the scale is nearly flat, the approach often indicates inverse-U-shaped relations when they do not really exist (Simonsohn, 2018)." (p. 301)
- **10-season replication:** "The quadratic coefficient is indeed significantly negative (b2 = −1.49, SE = 0.61, p = .014), and the estimated turning point (at x̂ = .52) is within the observed talent range (.13–.64). However, there are only 27 data points (9% of all data) beyond the inflection point." (p. 301)
- **Two-lines on original data — downturn disappears:** "Figure 1a shows that even with this direct test, there is no negative effect of talent on performance in the original NBA data. As a matter of fact, the second slope, which is supposed to capture the negative relation, is flat (and not significant) rather than trending downward." (p. 302) Fig. 1a labels: "Average Slope 1: b = 0.61, p < .001"; "Average Slope 2: b = 0.09, p = .743" (p. 302).
- **64-season data — second line positive:** "The standard quadratic model has a significant negative quadratic term (b2 = −1.13, SE = 0.26, p < 0.001), indicating the inverse-U-shaped relation. The inflection point (at x̂ = .62) is, however, even closer to the edge of the observed talent range (.07–.67), and there are merely 13 data points beyond (less than 1% of the data). The interrupted-regression approach shows that the second line is positive and significant (b2 = 0.31, SE = 0.12, p = .013). In other words, the more talent a team has, the more successful it is, even if the positive effect diminishes over the course of the talent scale." (p. 302)
- **Mediator also fails:** "when we use the measure of intrateam coordination instead of team performance (Swaab et al., 2014, Study 3), either coefficients for the quadratic term are not significant (for the 10-season data) or the estimated inflection point is beyond the observed talent range (64-season data [...])." (pp. 302–303)
- **Robustness:** "Using different talent cutoffs does not change the results [...], nor does the inclusion of free-throw percentage, teams' performance in the previous season, roster size, and games played as control variables [...]. Separate analyses of individual periods to account for rule changes also do not support the TMT effect [...]. Other established measures of player skill in the NBA (see Terner & Franks, 2020) do not show the inverse-U-shaped relation but instead depict an increase in success in relation to talent ratio" (p. 303)
- **Better functional form:** "one of these functions and most often the log function (black line in Fig. 1) describes the relations between talent and performance better than the quadratic function in virtually all scenarios in our analyses" (p. 303)
- **Generalization to TMGT literature:** "As with TMT, most of the evidence for the broad TMGT phenomena may be based on inappropriate inferences from quadratic functions and may constitute a mere method artifact." (p. 303)
- **Closing:** "until appropriate formal tests have been used, whether interrupted regression or comparison of different functions, we should consider a never-too-much (NTM) effect." (p. 303)

### Does the downturn disappear?
- **Yes, in the NBA.** On Swaab's own 10 seasons the second slope is flat and non-significant (b = 0.09, p = .743); on 64 seasons it is positive and significant (b = 0.31, p = .013). Concave-increasing (diminishing returns), not inverted-U (pp. 301–302).
- Soccer (Swaab Study 2) is **not** reanalyzed here; it was reanalyzed earlier by Simonsohn (2018, p. 553; see §6), and in the Nelson & Simonsohn (2014) Data Colada post cited in Gula's note 1 (p. 303).

### Mechanism or model
- None proposed for talent → performance beyond functional-form choice. Log/power/logistic forms are justified by analogy to psychophysics, learning, skill acquisition (p. 303), not by a model of team production. **(Assistant judgment)**
- **Fit to data?** Curves (quadratic, log, two-lines) are fit; no mechanistic model.

### Curve-shape test and validity by Simonsohn's standard (Assistant judgment)
- Uses Simonsohn's two-lines procedure directly (p. 302) plus model comparison — valid by that standard. The contribution is precisely to apply the valid test to Swaab's data.

### Limitations (tagged)
- **[single system]** NBA only; football (soccer) result of Swaab et al. untouched.
- **[imperfect measure]** Retains Swaab's top-third EWA ratio as the talent index (robustness to other skill metrics in supplement, p. 303).
- **[small data]** Even the 64-season set has "merely 13 data points beyond" the quadratic peak (p. 302) — the high-talent tail is thin in any league, so absence of a downturn is also weakly identified at the extreme (Assistant judgment).
- **[no model of mechanism]** No account of why returns diminish.
- **[outcome is not selection]** Outcome is team win proportion, not who is selected.
- Short commentary format; most analyses in Supplemental Material, not read here.

### Channel and source (Assistant judgment)
- **Channel:** EVALUATION of team composition (team output as function of peer talent). Methodological, not substantive.
- **Source:** PEERS (teammate talent share).
- **Use in intro:** the cleanest single citation that a published peer-talent inverted-U in psychology dissolved under a valid test; supports "curve shape is an empirical question."

## 3. Musch & Grondin (2001) — Relative Age Effect in Sport (review)

**Citation.** Musch, J., & Grondin, S. (2001). Unequal competition as an impediment to personal development: A review of the relative age effect in sport. *Developmental Review, 21*(2), 147–167. https://doi.org/10.1006/drev.2000.0516 (Issue number 2 not printed on the PDF; volume 21 and pages 147–167 are. Received June 29, 1999; revised November 23, 1999; published online March 19, 2001 — p. 167.)

**Page mapping.** Printed p. = PDF p. + 146 (PDF p. 1 = p. 147).

### Data / setting / sample
- Narrative review; Table 1 (pp. 150–151) summarizes studies by sport, level, and the "Player born in the first versus second half of the competition year (in %)" contrast (p. 150). Sports: soccer, ice hockey, baseball, basketball, cricket, American football, gymnastics, handball, swimming, tennis, volleyball. (Table 1 text layer is scrambled; individual cell values are not transcribed here — check the PDF page directly before quoting any number from it.)
- "The vast majority of RAE research has been concerned with male athletes." (p. 149)
- Typical method: "looking for a divergence between the expected and observed number of players born per month or per quarter, the expected value being based on the one observed in the general population from which the players' sample was drawn." (p. 149)

### Definition
- "Children born shortly before the cutoff date for age grouping in youth sport programs suffer from being promoted to higher age groups earlier than their later-born peers. Skewed birthdate distributions among participants in youth sport and professional sport leagues have been interpreted as the result of this disadvantage." (Abstract, p. 147)
- "This difference of age between individuals in the same age group is referred to as relative age, and its consequence is known as the Relative Age Effect (RAE)." (p. 147)
- "Children are grouped by chronological age for the purpose of providing developmentally appropriate instruction, fair competition, and equal opportunity." (p. 148)

### Main findings (verbatim)
- **Scope:** "A growing body of research shows that this Relative Age Effect in sport is a worldwide phenomenon and that it exists in many, but not all, competitive sports." (p. 147)
- **Soccer:** "In soccer, the phenomenon is worldwide." (p. 149)
- **Hockey origin:** "Grondin et al. (1984) demonstrated a highly skewed distribution of birthdates among ice hockey players in competitive youth hockey leagues and in the main professional ice hockey league in North America, the National Hockey League (NHL): those born in the first months of the year were overrepresented, whereas there was a lack of players born in the last months of the year." (p. 148)
- **Age gradient in pros:** "Whereas the proportion of players born in the first half of the competition year was as high as 68% among young professionals ages 18–22 years, it monotonically decreased thereafter and finally reached an even proportion (49%) among the 33- to 35-year-olds." (p. 152) Bäumler's interpretation: "a gradual wearing-off of the physical advantage [...] and a corresponding shift toward players with better technical attributes who only initially suffered from a relative age disadvantage." (p. 152)
- **Exceptions:** "For basketball (Daniel & Janssen, 1987), gymnastics (Baxter-Jones, 1995), and American football (Daniel & Janssen, 1987; Stanaway & Hines, 1995), no significant RAE is reported." (p. 152)
- **Causal evidence that the cutoff is the cause (natural experiments):** Germany and Brazil share an August 1 cutoff but opposite seasons — "the distribution of professional players' birthdates was the same in both countries." (p. 153) Australia changed its cutoff from January 1 to August 1 in 1988: "A corresponding shift in the birthdate distribution of professional players 10 years after the change provided strong evidence that the cutoff date is indeed the causal factor underlying the RAE." (p. 154)
- **Hockey Dec/Jan argument:** "December and January—being at opposite ends of the competition year in hockey—are juxtaposed and very similar climatically. Nevertheless, significant RAEs were found in hockey [...]. This suggests that the cutoff date rather than some seasonal influence causes the RAE." (p. 153)

### Mechanisms (verbatim) — KEY for selection framing
- **(a) Competition for scarce slots is a necessary condition (pool size):** "Suppose there are 15 places on a sport team, and that in a given school or community, there are 15 young persons of a given age group interested in occupying these places. In such a case, there is no reason to expect a RAE. Everyone will have a place on the team. However, suppose that there are 150 persons from this age group interested in playing with this team. In such a case, there will be strong competition to obtain a place, and RAEs are much more likely to occur. The general principle that can be derived from the situation is that the larger the pool of potential players for a given sport in a given category, the stronger the resulting RAE should be." (p. 154)
  - Evidence: "the RAE was stronger in higher level, i.e., in cities where more ice hockey players were available to form teams." (p. 154); "For volleyball teams in schools, there was no RAE. We can suppose that most players interested in volleyball had a chance to be part of the teams. Nevertheless, [...] when regional elite teams are formed from this pool of players in schools, there was a RAE for male players in the 14- to 15-years-old category." (p. 154)
  - Authors' caveat: "Although none of these findings provide direct evidence that the level of competition directly determines the magnitude of the RAE, they are at least compatible with the notion that competition for obtaining places on a team is fertile ground for RAEs to occur." (p. 154)
- **(b) Physical maturity:** "Physical development (and also motor skill, a major determinant of team placement in junior ice hockey [...]) is strongly correlated with chronological age" (p. 155); "a relative age disadvantage, coupled with a late maturation, can make it virtually impossible for a young player to compete (see Fig. 1)." (p. 155) Goalkeepers: "In the NHL, 55% of forwards were born in the first half of the selection year, but more than two-thirds of goal keepers were born during the same period" (p. 155).
  - **But physical maturity alone fails its own predictions:** "if physical maturity is the only reason for the RAE, a reduction rather than intensification of the skewness of the distribution should be observable in subsequent years [...]. Both of these predictions are at odds with empirical findings. There was no RAE among the youngest players in the studies of Barnsley and Thompson (1988) and Musch (1998), and the magnitude of the RAE increased rather than decreased with increasing age in these studies. This pattern of results suggests that RAEs cannot be explained by physical variables alone." (p. 157)
- **(c) Psychological: perceived competence, dropout, Pygmalion (evaluation):** "relatively older children advantaged by their higher relative age are more likely to continue their participation once all children are able to accurately assess their competence." (pp. 157–158); "Children profiting most from an initial relative age advantage are likely to be erroneously perceived as the most talented in their age group. The Pygmalion effect may amplify and stabilize this relative age advantage if the behaviors of parents, coaches, and peers covary with the inital [sic] perception of children's abilities" (p. 158)
- **(d) Experience + selection compounding:** "this difference represents an extra year of experience in a given sport itself, which means much more training." (p. 158); "The training time difference among children may also be magnified by the fact that relatively older players are more likely to be chosen for select teams and high ability groups. Such selection is not only associated with receiving better coaching and facing better opponents; being involved in higher competition levels is in itself more prestigious and therefore likely to increase motivation." (p. 159)
  - **Coach's-eye selection rule:** "Thus, if, in a coach's mind, two children are considered to have the same age because they are born in the same year, the child born in January has a huge advantage over the other, first, in chances to be part of the team if too many children compete for limited places on a team, and second, in active participation in the games. The relatively younger child, on the other hand, is likely to be frustrated by his or her limited ability to compete and may finally drop out of organized sport." (p. 159)
- **(e) Self-selection / dropout:** "Contrary to older children, the younger ones are likely to drop out rather than to continue voluntary participation (Barnsley & Thompson, 1988; Musch, 1998)." (pp. 148–149)
- **Summary of mechanism:** "a mixture of physical, cognitive, emotional, and motivational causes work together to produce the effect." (p. 159)
- **Talent loss / system-level cost:** "It is very likely that many promising talents have been overlooked in the past because they suffered from a relative age disadvantage in their early childhood. This may well have resulted in a lowering of the overall quality of professional sport teams." (p. 159); "the long-term result of a RAE will be a lowering in the overall quality of the highest competitive teams if players with good technical attributes are being overlooked at an early age due to a lack of physical development that is simply related to the period of the selection year in which they were born." (p. 161)

### Proposed remedies (verbatim)
- **Biological-age grouping** (height, weight, skeletal age) — but impractical and itself unfair: "the adoption of skeletal age would result, in some cases, in classifying a 12-year-old child with 7-year-old children." (p. 160) Weight classes work in boxing/wrestling (p. 160).
- **Varying cutoff per competition** (individual sports, e.g., swimming) (p. 160).
- **Rotating cutoff dates:** "Rotating cutoff dates offer a possible solution to RAEs in team sports. For example, Grondin et al. (1984) proposed a 15-month or 21-month category system to break the structure based on multiples of 12 [...]. This would have the advantage of constantly cycling the cutoff date throughout the year, thereby eliminating a systematic bias against children born late in a fixed competition year." (p. 160)
- **Narrower age bands** (9-month "novem" system) — practical problems "due to organizational problems and the lower number of players available in each age group" (p. 160).
- **Different cutoffs across activities:** Japan's shared April 1 cutoff vs. U.S. (pp. 160–161).
- **Age quotas:** "quotas of children born throughout the activity year should be required on high-caliber competitive teams" (p. 161).
- **Multiple squads / multiple standards** (p. 161).
- **Coaching practice:** "decision-makers to look at factors such as technical skills rather than physical strength, and ensuring that coaching is equivalent for all players regardless of their present, age-dependent level of ability." (p. 162)

### Mechanism or model
- **Formal?** No. Verbal multi-factor account; Fig. 1 (p. 156) is a schematic of two normal maturity distributions offset by 11 months, not a fitted model.
- **Fit to data?** No. Evidence = birth-month distributional skew vs. population baseline, plus two natural experiments (Brazil/Germany, Australia cutoff change).
- **(Assistant judgment)** The 15-vs-150 thought experiment is an informal selection model: a fixed number of slots K, a pool N, and a score correlated with within-cohort age; the RAE should grow as K/N shrinks. It is never formalized or fit.

### Curve-shape test (Assistant judgment)
- Not applicable — no curvilinear claim. Outcomes are birth-quarter proportions (chi-square style comparisons in underlying studies). The "RAE increases with age / competitive level" claims are monotone gradients drawn across studies, not within-study tests.

### Limitations (tagged)
- **[imperfect measure]** Outcome is the birthdate skew of survivors; mechanisms (maturity, perceived competence, coach expectations, training hours) are almost never measured: "surprisingly little work has been conducted to substantiate the relationship between physical development and RAEs by measuring the somatic and strength properties of young players." (p. 156); "Data comparing the physical properties of dropouts and players disadvantaged by their relative age to the body indices of players profiting from a relative age advantage are completely lacking, however." (p. 156)
- **[no model of mechanism]** "the extent of existence of the RAE has not yet been completely examined nor have the physical, social, and emotional mechanisms that bring it about been clearly identified." (p. 162)
- **[model not fit to data]** Pool-size principle (p. 154) is a thought experiment; no fitted selection model; authors concede no "direct evidence that the level of competition directly determines the magnitude of the RAE" (p. 154).
- **[single system]** Overwhelmingly male youth sport (p. 149; p. 162); "little is known about the role of sex in the RAE" (p. 162).
- **[small data]** Several Table 1 entries flagged by the authors "(but small sample)" (pp. 150–151).
- Non-participants unobserved: "Attention should also be given to nonparticipants to determine if their month of birth is related to their decision not to get involved in organized sports in the first place." (p. 163) Calls for "prospective and longitudinal studies relating drop-out decisions to a children's perceived ability" (p. 163).
- **(Assistant judgment)** Selection, development, and dropout are confounded in a single cross-sectional birthdate distribution — the data cannot separate "selected because older" from "developed more because selected" from "stayed because selected."

### Channel and source (Assistant judgment)
- **Channel:** canonical **SELECTION** case — an organizational rule (cutoff date) defines the comparison pool; coaches select top-K from that pool on a score contaminated by within-cohort age; selection then feeds **DEVELOPMENT** (better coaching, opponents, more training) and **EVALUATION** (Pygmalion: older children "erroneously perceived as the most talented," p. 158). Dropout is self-selection out of the pool.
- **Source:** ORGANIZATION (cutoff rule, number of slots, competitive level) defines which PEERS one is compared to. The peer effect is relative position within the pool, not peer quality.
- **Use in intro:** strongest psychology source for "who you are compared against determines whether you get the slot," with natural-experiment causal evidence, and an explicit (informal) pool-size/slot-count argument — but no formal model and no fit.

## 4. Grant & Schwartz (2011) — Too Much of a Good Thing: The Inverted U

**Citation.** Grant, A. M., & Schwartz, B. (2011). Too much of a good thing: The challenge and opportunity of the inverted U. *Perspectives on Psychological Science, 6*(1), 61–76. https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691610393523

**Page mapping.** Printed p. = PDF p. + 60 (PDF p. 1 = p. 61).

### Data / setting / sample
- Narrative review / position paper; no new data. Organized around Peterson & Seligman's (2004) virtue categories: "We discovered clear evidence for costs of strengths in the first four virtue categories: wisdom and knowledge, courage, humanity and love, and justice." (p. 62) Extended case study: choice overload (pp. 67–69).

### Design
- Literature review + "admittedly speculative model" (p. 69) + methodological recommendations (p. 71). Note 1: "When possible, we focus on experimental studies that facilitate causal inferences. In the remaining cases, we focus on correlational studies [...] that show nonmonotonic, inverted-U-shaped relationships and are suggestive of, albeit not conclusive about, costs of virtues, strengths, and other positive phenomena." (p. 72)

### Core claim (verbatim)
- "This focus has obscured the prevalence and importance of nonmonotonic inverted-U-shaped effects, whereby positive phenomena reach inflection points at which their effects turn negative." (Abstract, p. 61)
- "Our purpose in this article is to draw attention to what may be a fundamental and ubiquitous psychological principle: There is no such thing as an unmitigated good. All positive traits, states, and experiences have costs that at high levels may begin to outweigh their benefits, creating the nonmonotonicity of an inverted U." (p. 62)
- "Underlying the vast majority of existing theory and research is the assumption that positive traits, experiences, and emotions have monotonic effects on well-being and performance." (p. 62)
- "Suedfeld (1969) referred to this principle as the 'ubiquitous U': Across many domains of psychology, one finds that X increases Y to a point, and then it decreases Y." (p. 62)
- Conclusion: "The inverted U is a widespread phenomenon in psychology, and we believe it deserves more attention in psychology writ large and in positive psychology especially." (p. 71)

### Examples most relevant to peers / teams / careers (verbatim)
- **Team composition — share of extraverts (peer-composition inverted U):** "research shows that work teams can have too many extraverts. An inverted-U-shaped relationship exists between the proportion of extraverts in a team and its performance, which is due to the fact that when a team is dominated by extraverts, task focus suffers (Barry & Stewart, 1997). This evidence reveals how the very traits that facilitate teamwork can, in high proportions, undermine it." (pp. 65–66)
- **NBA shared experience:** "in a study of National Basketball Association teams, Berman, Down, and Hill (2002) found that the longer teams practiced and played together, the more games they won. This relationship was mediated by better coordination between players, in the form of assists. However, this benefit of persistent practice dissipated after approximately 4 years" (p. 63)
- **Experience / tenure:** "meta-analytic evidence suggests that in many jobs, the relationship between experience and performance takes the form of an inverted U (Sturman, 2003)." (p. 63)
- **Team learning orientation:** Bunderson & Sutcliffe (2003) "found a curvilinear, nonmonotonic relationship between team learning orientation and business performance." (p. 63)
- **Task conflict and team innovation:** De Dreu (2006) "found an inverted-U-shaped relationship between these task conflicts and team innovation, mediated by information sharing." (p. 65)
- **Assertiveness and leadership:** Ames & Flynn (2007) "found an inverted-U-shaped relationship between assertiveness and leadership effectiveness." (p. 66)
- **Conscientiousness and job performance:** Le et al. "found that conscientiousness has an inverted-U-shaped relationship with job performance, especially in simple jobs" (p. 64)
- **Life satisfaction and later income:** Oishi et al. (2007): "life satisfaction had an inverted-U-shaped relationship with income 5 to 15 years later." (p. 64) Authors flag selection confound: "it may be the case that people with lower dispositional levels of happiness simply come to prioritize success over happiness and thus self-select into higher levels of the achievement continuum." (p. 64)
- **Choice overload:** "the optimal number of choices appears to fall in the range of two to four (Patall, Cooper, & Robinson, 2008 [...])" (p. 68)
- **A counterexample — self-control:** "research has yet to provide empirical evidence of any costs of high self-control." (p. 70)

### Mechanism or model — why inverted Us arise (verbatim)
- **Three general mechanisms:** "The aforementioned findings highlight three mechanisms worthy of further consideration: value conflicts; differences in shapes, slopes, and thresholds for positive and negative effects; and nonmonotonic single effects." (p. 66)
  1. **Virtue conflicts:** "Virtues are often conceptualized as independent, but in a complex social world, the pursuit of one virtue has consequences for other virtues." (p. 66)
  2. **Two opposing processes with different shapes/thresholds:** "Coombs and Avrunin (1977) suggested that as a general matter, 'good things satiate and bad things escalate' (p. 224). The combination of diminishing marginal utility of positive influences and moderators of those influences whose threshold of activation differs from the positive effect and/or whose strength of activation increases nonlinearly can produce the inverted U." (p. 66)
  3. **Single nonmonotonic effect (Yerkes–Dodson):** "a single set of effects that is in and of itself nonmonotonic." (p. 66)
- **The two-curve schematic (Fig. 1, choice):** "although there is diminishing marginal utility to added options, there is increasing marginal disutility to added options (see Coombs & Avrunin, 1977). The point of drawing two separate curves is that the psychological benefits of choice and the psychological costs are different, and independent. As McGuire (1997) argued, an 'inverted-U relation . . . can be creatively interpreted as the resultant of two opposed mediating processes, both monotonic' (p. 23)." (p. 69); "the black curve [...], the algebraic sum of the two preceding curves, is intended to show the psychological state that results when the processes are combined." (p. 69)
- **Not every good turns bad — Warr's "vitamin" model (key for not presuming an inverted U):** "He argues that some job characteristics resemble vitamins A and D in that they have negative effects above a certain level (the inverted U). He predicts that other characteristics, however, resemble vitamins C and E in that they have no incremental value but also cause no harm above a certain level (a diminishing marginal utility function that eventually flattens). It remains to be seen whether there are any virtues that take the form of the latter function." (p. 71)
- **Context dependence of the peak:** "Not only was the mean not computable by formula, but it changed from one context to another." (p. 67)
- **Formal?** No. Fig. 1 is explicitly "admittedly speculative" (p. 69) and schematic; no equations; not fit to data.

### Methodological recommendations (verbatim)
- "most of the evidence that we discovered was correlational. More experimental research on nonmonotonic effects is needed in which psychologists vary treatments at multiple levels of frequency or intensity." (p. 71)
- "When researchers fail to discover nonmonotonic relationships, the methodological artifact of range restriction may be the culprit. To maximize the probability of identifying inverted-U relationships, researchers need to sample on the full range of the independent and dependent variables." (p. 71)
- "a good rule of thumb for study design may be to include a wide enough range of values of the independent variable to enable a judgment of when nonmonotonicity occurs rather than whether nonmonotonicity occurs." (p. 71)

### Curve-shape test and validity by Simonsohn's standard (Assistant judgment)
- No statistical test is specified. The review accepts reported "inverted-U-shaped" findings at face value; most underlying studies (e.g., Barry & Stewart, Sturman, Le et al.) relied on quadratic regression. Gula et al. (2021, p. 303) explicitly cite Grant & Schwartz among the TMGT reviews whose evidence "may be based on inappropriate inferences from quadratic functions."
- The recommendations push toward *finding* inverted Us ("To maximize the probability of identifying inverted-U relationships," p. 71; judge "when" not "whether," p. 71) — i.e., the prior is tilted toward nonmonotonicity. This is the opposite of the dissertation's stance that curve shape is an empirical question. (Assistant judgment)

### Limitations (tagged)
- **[no model of mechanism]** Three mechanism classes are named, not modeled; Fig. 1 is "admittedly speculative" (p. 69).
- **[model not fit to data]** The two-process sum is never fit to any data set.
- **[imperfect measure]** Relies on underlying studies' curve tests (mostly quadratic); no assessment of test validity. Range restriction raised only as a reason for *missing* inverted Us (p. 71), not for spurious ones.
- **[small data]** / **[single system]** Inherited from the reviewed studies (e.g., single Fortune 100 firm for Bunderson & Sutcliffe, p. 63); not systematically assessed.
- **[outcome is not selection]** Outcomes are well-being and performance; none concern who wins a scarce slot.
- Causal ambiguity acknowledged: "Although these data are longitudinal, they remain correlational, leaving questions about causality unanswered." (p. 64)

### Channel and source (Assistant judgment)
- **Channel:** mostly DEVELOPMENT (traits/experiences → well-being, performance) and team-level EVALUATION (composition → output). No SELECTION.
- **Source:** mostly the individual's own attributes; peer composition appears only in the extravert-share and NBA shared-experience examples; organization appears via job complexity / job characteristics (Warr).
- **Use in intro:** cite as the strongest statement of the TMGT prior in psychology and as the clearest articulation of the "two opposing monotone processes" account — and, via Warr's vitamin model, as an in-house acknowledgement that diminishing-then-flat is an alternative shape that must be distinguished empirically.

## 5. Pierce & Aguinis (2013) — The Too-Much-of-a-Good-Thing Effect in Management

**Citation.** Pierce, J. R., & Aguinis, H. (2013). The too-much-of-a-good-thing effect in management. *Journal of Management, 39*(2), 313–338. https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206311410060 (February 2013 issue; PDF carries "© The Author(s) 2011" — online-first 2011.)

**Page mapping.** Printed p. = PDF p. + 312 (PDF p. 1 = p. 313).

### Data / setting / sample
- Conceptual/meta-theoretical review; no new data. "we review eight seemingly unrelated bodies of scholarly work in key areas in organizational behavior, human resource management, entrepreneurship, and strategic management." (p. 318) Choice of areas "guided by the popularity of these subfields based on membership figures in Academy of Management Divisions" (p. 318).
- Eight worked examples: leadership (structure/consideration; assertiveness), conscientiousness, enriched job design, experience in personnel selection, new venture planning, firm growth rate, diversification, organizational slack (pp. 318–323). Brief further list: organizational identification, citizenship behavior, morale, trust and autonomy, "team and group size," board size beyond five to seven members, etc. (p. 324).

### Definition and meta-theoretical claim (verbatim)
- **Definition:** "The TMGT effect occurs when ordinarily beneficial antecedents (i.e., predictor variables) reach inflection points after which their relations with desired outcomes (i.e., criterion variables) cease to be linear and positive. Exceeding these inflection points is always undesirable because it leads either to waste (no additional benefit) or, worse, to undesirable outcomes (e.g., decreased individual or organizational performance). The philosophical tenet underlying the TMGT effect is that too much of any good thing is ultimately bad." (p. 315)
- **Abstract version:** "The authors posit that, due to the TMGT effect, all seemingly monotonic positive relations reach context-specific inflection points after which the relations turn asymptotic and often negative, resulting in an overall pattern of curvilinearity." (p. 313)
- **Three theses resolved by levels of X:** "Thesis A holds when X is less than I, Thesis B holds when X is approximately equal to I, and Thesis C may hold when X is greater than I. The specific location of I on the X continuum depends on the particular context." (p. 317)
- **Asymptote counts as TMGT:** "the confirmation of Thesis C means that the relation follows an inverted U-shaped pattern, whereas lack of confirmation of Thesis C means that the relation follows an asymptotic pattern [...]. In either case, increases in the focal antecedent lead to undesired outcomes." (p. 317)
- **Paradigm-shift claim:** "our meta-theory suggests that a paradigmatic shift from linear to curvilinear models is needed to improve management theory and practice, regardless of level of analysis and subfield of study." (p. 317)
- **Inflection points in observed range:** "In most cases, the inflection point where the relations cease to be linear falls within observable ranges of predictor scores and, hence, does not affect only extreme, unusual, or just a few cases or observations." (p. 324)
- **Acknowledged counterview:** "Hastie and Dawes (2001) concluded that linear decision-making models efficiently approximate curvilinear phenomena subject to diagnostic or predictive judgment because most such phenomena are monotonic." (p. 317)

### Examples most relevant to selection / teams (verbatim)
- **Experience in personnel selection:** "A central objective of personnel selection research is to identify knowledge, skills, abilities, and other characteristics (KSAOs) that can be used as predictors of future employee performance." (p. 320) Sturman (2003) "showed that the relationships are moderated by job complexity such that they have an inverted U-shaped pattern (i.e., positive at low levels, neutral at moderate levels, and negative at higher levels) for low-complexity jobs but a more asymptotic form for high-complexity jobs." (p. 320) (Assistant judgment: here the outcome is post-hire performance, i.e., predictor validity — not who gets selected.)
- **Leadership / assertiveness:** Ames and Flynn "found statistically significant negative quadratic terms for assertiveness [...], indicating an inverted U-shaped pattern." (p. 318)
- **Conscientiousness:** Whetzel et al. "found evidence (ΔR² = .09) of an inverted U-shaped relation between conscientiousness and objectively measured job performance" (p. 319) [text layer reads "DR2"; Δ rendered as D].
- **Diversification:** Qian et al. (2008) "found significant quadratic relations between regional diversification, and return on assets and return on sales." (p. 323)
- **Team and group size, board size:** listed as TMGT cases (p. 324), without detail.

### Mechanism or model
- **Formal?** Partly — they call it "a formalized meta-theory" (p. 316), but the formalization is the regression specification, not a generative mechanism:
  - "Y = b0 + b1X + b2X² + e, (3) [...] where the inflection point occurs precisely at the following value along the X continuum (Weisberg, 2005): –b1 / 2b2" (p. 326)
  - Moderated curvilinear model: "Y = b0 + b1X + b2Z + b3XZ + b4X² + e, (4)" (p. 326)
  - "variable X serves as a predictor and also as a moderator of the relation between itself and the outcome Y. Hence, the nonlinear relations predicted by the TMGT effect can be conceptualized as a special case of the more general moderated relations." (p. 326)
- **Shape taxonomy from Aristotle:** "we expect to find asymptotic relations for predictors that can be classified as being good in themselves (e.g., general mental ability, wisdom) but inverted U-shaped relations between predictors and outcomes for predictors that can be classified as either actions or passions." (p. 326)
- Mechanisms in examples are case-specific verbal accounts (overconfidence, resource strain, agency costs). No unified mechanism is offered beyond the "meta-theoretical principle." **(Assistant judgment)**
- **Fit to data?** No new fits.

### Methodological recommendations for testing curvilinearity (verbatim)
- **Graphs then formal tests:** "As an initial assessment of the TMGT effect, researchers can create graphs such as scatterplots to understand whether a data set may follow an asymptotic or inverted U-shaped pattern. However, more formal tests will often be required. When such formal tests are conducted, it will be necessary to consider issues such as statistical power and effect size, range restriction, and the possible use of advanced data-analytic techniques including meta-analysis and growth modeling." (p. 327)
- **Specify location and shape:** "future theory development efforts should predict not only whether X will be related to Y but also the precise points on the X continuum where the X-Y relation will turn asymptotic and, if applicable, negative." (p. 325)
- **Sign of b4 as shape test:** "specify the nature of the X-Y relation to the left and the right of the inflection points, including whether the curvilinear relation is asymptotic (as indicated by a positive sign for b4) or negative (as indicated by a negative sign for b4)." (pp. 326–327)
- **Power:** "tests of hypotheses regarding curvilinear effects as predicted by the TMGT effect are also likely to suffer from low statistical power." (p. 327); "measures should be highly reliable and should not be coarse, and after collecting data, researchers should avoid artificially dichotomizing continuous variables." (p. 328)
- **Range restriction via selection (directly relevant to selection framing):** "in many domains in the organizational sciences there are selection effects that result when samples do not include scores representing the entire continuum of predictor scores (Heckman, 1979). [...] these effects can occur at any level of analysis from the individual level [...] to the firm level (e.g., only firms that are successful or are still in business are included in the sample) of analysis." (p. 328)
- **Corrections assume linearity:** "these correction procedures ironically render such tests invalid because they assume a linear X-Y relation (Hunter, Schmidt, & Le, 2006)." (p. 328)
- **Meta-analysis and growth modeling** as remedies (pp. 329–330): "The TMGT effect emerges over time. Hence, these techniques are suitable for testing TMGT effect hypotheses." (p. 330); "detecting a curvilinear pattern requires a minimum of three observations (Chan, 1998)" (p. 330).

### Curve-shape test and validity by Simonsohn's standard (Assistant judgment)
- The recommended test is the quadratic regression (Eq. 3/4) with the turning point at –b1/2b2 — exactly the procedure Simonsohn (2018) argues is invalid for establishing a U. No two-lines, spline, or interrupted-regression alternative is recommended.
- The b4-sign rule (p. 327) cannot separate asymptotic from inverted-U: a quadratic with b4 < 0 always eventually turns down, and a positive b4 implies convexity, not an asymptote. A plateau requires a different functional form (log, power, piecewise). Simonsohn's Fig./discussion of concave-monotone functions makes this point directly (see §6).
- Terminology: "inflection point" is used for the vertex/turning point; mathematically the vertex of a quadratic is not an inflection point (Simonsohn and Gula use "maximum"/"break point"). Flag if quoting.
- Definitional issue: by counting asymptotic relations as TMGT ("In either case," p. 317), almost any concave monotone relation confirms the meta-theory — which makes the claim hard to falsify and is in tension with treating curve shape as an empirical question.

### Limitations (tagged)
- **[no model of mechanism]** "Meta-theoretical principle" with no generative mechanism; Aristotelian taxonomy (actions/passions vs. "good in themselves," p. 326) is philosophical.
- **[model not fit to data]** No data; examples cited at face value, mostly from quadratic-term studies.
- **[imperfect measure]** Recommended test (quadratic; b4 sign) does not validly distinguish asymptote from downturn (Assistant judgment, per Simonsohn 2018).
- **[single system]** / selection of examples: chosen by subfield popularity (p. 318); no systematic search or counting of null curvilinear tests. Authors conjecture file-drawer suppression of *non*-significant curvilinear tests (p. 327) but not of spurious significant ones.
- **[outcome is not selection]** Even the "personnel selection" example (p. 320) uses post-hire performance as the outcome. Selection appears as a *threat to inference* (range restriction, p. 328), not as the outcome of interest.

### Channel and source (Assistant judgment)
- **Channel:** EVALUATION / performance of individuals, teams, firms. Selection enters only as a sampling artifact (truncation via Heckman-type selection, p. 328) — useful for the intro: the very selection the dissertation studies truncates the range on which curve shape can be estimated.
- **Source:** mixed — individual traits (conscientiousness), ORGANIZATION (job design, slack, diversification, board size, group size); peers only implicitly (team/group size).
- **Use in intro:** cite for the management-side TMGT prior and as the source of the quadratic-test convention that Simonsohn critiques; also for the point that selection truncates samples.

## 6. Simonsohn (2018) — Two Lines: testing U shapes without quadratic regression

**Citation.** Simonsohn, U. (2018). Two lines: A valid alternative to the invalid testing of U-shaped relationships with quadratic regressions. *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 1*(4), 538–555. https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245918805755
**Corrigendum (bundled as PDF pp. 1–2):** Simonsohn, U. (2019). Corrigendum: Two lines [...]. *Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science, 2*(4), 410–411. https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245919894972 — replaces Fig. 1 and the "Level 1" section because the original Simonton (1976) example "did not take into account that Simonton mean-centered the predictors" (corrigendum, p. 410). (Assistant judgment: the article body in this PDF already carries the corrected Level-1 text and the hypothetical soccer/education Fig. 1, so quotes below from p. 540 match the corrigendum wording.)

**Page mapping.** Article: printed p. = PDF p. + 535 (PDF p. 3 = p. 538). Corrigendum = PDF pp. 1–2.

### Data / setting / sample
- Methods paper. Simulations: false-positive scenarios — "The full combination of parameters led to 180 scenarios" (two-segment, positive-then-flat) and "135 scenarios" (logistic) (Fig. 7 caption, p. 549); power scenarios — "3,420 scenarios" (Fig. 8 caption, p. 550). Sample sizes 100, 200, 500 (p. 549).
- Reanalyses of two published claims: Sterling, Jost & Pennycook (2016) and **Swaab et al. (2014) Study 2 (FIFA)** (pp. 551–553).
- Open data/code: https://osf.io/psfwz/ (p. 539); app at http://webstimate.org/twolines (p. 538).

### Core argument (verbatim)
- **Abstract:** "Despite implying merely a change of sign, hypotheses about U-shaped functions are tested almost exclusively via quadratic regressions, an approach that imposes an arbitrary functional-form assumption that in some scenarios can lead to a 100% rate of false positives (e.g., the incorrect conclusion that y = log(x) is U shaped). Estimating two regression lines, one for low and one for high values of x, allows testing for a sign change without a functional-form assumption." (p. 538)
- **What a U hypothesis claims:** "when social scientists hypothesize that a relationship, y = f(x), is U shaped, they are merely hypothesizing that f(x) contains a sign change" (p. 538)
- **U ≠ quadratic:** "Researchers are so used to testing for U shapes with quadratic regressions that they often use the terms U shape and quadratic as synonyms, though these terms in fact describe distinct features of mathematical functions." (p. 539)
- **Why plateaus produce spurious Us (most relevant to talent/peer curves):** "Elevation of the false-positive rate is especially likely when the true function, f(x), flattens out (e.g., a ceiling effect), because the quadratic formula is unable to generate a long plateau and so, when its functional form is forced on the data, it generates a spurious sign change." (p. 539)
- **False negatives too:** "Under other circumstances, it can also plausibly yield a 100% false-negative rate, indicating with near certainty every time that a relationship that is blatantly U shaped is not U shaped" (p. 539)
- **Formal definition:** "let us formally define a function, y = f(x), as U shaped if there exists an x value, xc, within the set of possible x values, such that the average effect of x on y is of opposite sign for x ≤ xc and x ≥ xc." (pp. 539–540)
- **Three levels of quadratic misuse, all invalid:**
  - Level 1 (significant quadratic term): "The significant coefficient need not, in fact, imply a U-shaped relationship." (p. 540; corrigendum p. 410)
  - Level 2 (turning point in range): "Even with this step, however, it is problematic to conclude that the relationship is U shaped, because we need to take into account sampling error." (p. 540)
  - Level 3 (Johnson–Neyman / Lind & Mehlum): "Even this more sophisticated use of quadratic regressions to test for a U shape is invalid, however. The reason is that the regression results, and therefore the Johnson-Neyman calculations, hinge on the assumption that the true relationship between x and y is exactly quadratic." (p. 541)
- **Log example:** "Figure 2a shows a scenario in which the true relationship is y = log(x) and a quadratic regression would result in ŷ = 13.94x – 10.45x². [...] Of course, that result is wrong, as the effect of x is never negative when y = log(x), but it is estimated as negative because we are incorrectly assuming that the relationship is quadratic. Specification error is behind the erroneous conclusion." (p. 541)
- **Diagnostics don't save it:** "regression diagnostics qualitatively assess the general adequacy of the model, but we want to quantitatively assess the adequacy of the conclusion that the relationship is U shaped." (p. 543)
- **Flexible alternatives invite p-hacking:** "the abundance of alternatives to the quadratic opens the door to overfitting in general and p-hacking in particular." (p. 543)

### The two-lines test (verbatim)
- **Decision rule:** "We can then reject the null hypothesis of absence of a U shape if the slopes are of opposite sign and are both statistically significant." (p. 540)
- **No linearity assumption:** "It is very important to understand that the regression estimate is the average slope for any functional form, and thus we are not assuming that the true function is linear when we compute the average this way." (p. 540)
- **Interrupted regression (Eq. 1):** "y = a + bxlow + cxhigh + d * high + ZBZ, (1) where xlow = x – xc if x < xc and 0 otherwise, xhigh = x – xc if x ≥ xc and 0 otherwise, and high = 1 if x ≥ xc and 0 otherwise." (p. 545) Note 9: "For purposes of U-shape testing, one must rely on interrupted rather than segmented regressions" (p. 554).
- **Break point set for power, not fit:** "when testing for U shapes we are not trying to fit the data as well as possible." (p. 545)
- **Robin Hood algorithm (five steps):** "1. Estimate a cubic spline for the relationship between x and y 2. Identify ŷmax, the most extreme internal fitted value 3. Identify ŷflat, the set of ŷ values within 1 SE of ŷmax 4. Estimate an interrupted regression using as the break point the median x value within ŷflat [...] 5. Set the break point at the z2/(z1 + z2)th percentile of the x values associated with ŷflat" (p. 548)
- **Break point is not an estimate of the peak:** "because the break point is set algorithmically within a set of candidate break points, it conveys no interpretable meaning on its own. We should not conclude that the breakpoint is the point where the sign of the effect switches." (p. 548)
- **Performance:** "the quadratic-regression approach to testing for U-shaped relationships had an unacceptably high false-positive rate—often a 100% rate—for a very broad range of scenarios. In contrast, the two-lines approach in general, and the Robin Hood procedure for setting the break point in particular, showed acceptable performance." (p. 548) "The Robin Hood algorithm generally outperformed all other alternatives." (p. 548)

### Mechanism or model
- None — statistical test only. Explicitly agnostic about functional form: "It makes no assumptions about the functional form of f(x)." (p. 553)

### Curve-shape test validity (Assistant judgment)
- This *is* the validity standard used in this note. Key operational criteria for judging other sources: (1) a significant quadratic is not evidence of a downturn; (2) the downturn requires a significantly negative average slope on the high-x side; (3) a concave-then-flat truth (ceiling, diminishing returns) is the scenario most likely to produce a spurious inverted U — exactly the shape Gula et al. find for NBA talent.

### Limitations (tagged / verbatim)
- "I have proposed an algorithm and evaluated its performance via simulation in small samples, without deriving its theoretical asymptotic properties." (p. 553)
- "It will not perform well, at least in terms of interpretability, if the true relationship has more than one change in sign, for instance, if it is N shaped, X shaped, or W shaped" (p. 553)
- "The precise false-positive rate of the two-lines test is not known, and it cannot be guaranteed to be 5% for any specific data set" (p. 553); "almost never higher than 6% for a nominal α of 5%" (p. 553)
- Inherits regression assumptions: "lack of independence across observations leads to underestimated standard errors in regression results in general and to higher false-positive rates with the two-lines test in particular." (p. 540)
- Does not locate the peak: "The specific point of the sign switch, to the extent it actually exists, is not estimated precisely with the two-lines test." (p. 548)
- **[no model of mechanism]** By design — tests shape, not why. **(Assistant judgment)** It also cannot distinguish "diminishing returns that plateau" from "strictly increasing concave" — both pass as "no U." Distinguishing those requires model comparison (as Gula et al. do with log vs. quadratic).
- **[outcome is not selection]** Not applicable as a methods paper; but note that none of the reanalyzed outcomes is a selection outcome.

### Channel and source (Assistant judgment)
- **Channel:** methodological — applies to any channel. For the dissertation: any claim that peer or organizational environment has an inverted-U effect on DEVELOPMENT, EVALUATION, or SELECTION must pass a two-lines (or equivalent model-comparison) test.
- **Use in intro:** the citation that licenses "we treat curve shape as an empirical question"; plus the specific fact that the flagship too-much-talent soccer result does not survive (p. 553).

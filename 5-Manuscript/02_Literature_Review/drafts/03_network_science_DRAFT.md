## Network science: position, sorting, and the structure of groups

**The question and its unit.** Network science asks how the pattern of ties among individuals shapes what reaches them, what they become, and how they are judged. Its unit is the node and its position in a network: the number and strength of its ties, whether those ties bridge otherwise separate groups, and whether its neighbors are connected to one another. Two features of the field make it the natural setting for the question of this dissertation. First, it represents organizations directly. Breiger (1974, 183) showed that a single matrix of person-to-group affiliations defines both a network of persons, tied by shared memberships, and a network of groups, tied by shared members. A team, a department, or a unit is therefore not a backdrop to the network but part of its structure. Second, the field treats dependence among observations as its object. Lazer et al. (2020, 1060) contrast traditional quantitative social science, "typically with assumptions of independence among observations," with computational social science, which applies "statistical models that capture multifarious dependencies within data." An individual's chance of selection, studied as a function of that individual's peers, is a question about exactly such a dependence.

**The organization shapes who one's peers are.** Peers and organizations are not independent sources of environment, because organizations assemble the pools from which peers are drawn. McPherson, Smith-Lovin, and Cook (2001) found that similarity among connected people, or homophily, structures ties of every kind, and that much of it is created by the composition of organizational settings, which they call foci, rather than by individual choice. They warn that cross-sectional analyses misread the long-term effects of organizational environments as "individual choice or (more appropriately) as unexplained variance, when in fact they are the systematic residues of past foci" (438). Kossinets and Watts (2009, 436) followed 30,396 members of a university over one academic year and found that "the vast majority of new ties form between individuals who already share a friend or a group, and once these conditions are met, similarity loses much, and in some cases all, of its effect." The same structure also shapes how people judge themselves. Feld (1991) proved that in any network in which people differ in their number of ties, a person's friends have on average more friends than the person does, and he argued that individuals who compare themselves with their contacts "may conclude that they themselves are below average and somehow inadequate" (1476). The local comparison set is biased by the structure of the network, which places network position in the *evaluation* channel as well as in the transmission of information.

**Position confers access to opportunities.** The field's best-known results concern the advantages of particular positions. Granovetter (1973) argued that weak ties bridge otherwise separate groups and therefore carry new information, and in a small sample of professional, technical, and managerial job changers (54 of whom found work through a contact), most of those who found work through a contact saw that contact only occasionally or rarely. Burt (1992, 10) extended the argument to competition: among candidates whose abilities fall within an acceptable range, the allocation of opportunities such as promotions turns on criteria other than financial and human capital, which he called social capital. In data on 673 supply-chain managers in one large electronics company, "Compensation, positive performance evaluations, promotions, and good ideas are disproportionately in the hands of people whose networks span structural holes" (Burt 2004, 349). Randomized tie recommendations on LinkedIn, covering more than 20 million people, confirmed that weak ties increase job mobility, but the relationship was an inverted U: "weaker ties increased job transmission but only to a point" (Rajkumar et al. 2022, 1304). The organization's position matters as well. In nearly 19,000 faculty across computer science, business, and history, "faculty hiring follows a common and steeply hierarchical structure that reflects profound social inequality" (Clauset, Arbesman, and Larremore 2015, 1); only 9 to 14 percent of faculty were placed at institutions more prestigious than their doctoral institution, a gradient the authors judged too steep to attribute to merit alone. Gould (2002) supplied a formal account of how such hierarchies arise: when actors partly base their judgments of others on the judgments of third parties, the judgments "amplify underlying differences" (1146), so that those above the mean in quality are overvalued and those below it are undervalued. In each case the environment changes how a given individual is evaluated or which opportunities that individual can reach.

**More connection is not always better.** Several results show that a structural feature that helps up to a point can harm beyond it. In the network of artists who created Broadway musicals between 1945 and 1989, "performance increased up to a threshold, after which point the positive effects reversed" (Uzzi and Spiro 2005, 447). The authors attribute the downturn partly to homogenized ideas and partly to the fact that "newcomers find it harder to land 'slots' on productions" (464), an explicit appeal to scarce positions. In a model of team assembly fitted to Broadway and to four scientific fields, the probability that a team slot goes to an incumbent rather than to a newcomer determines the structure of the collaboration network and, across journals, is associated with team performance (Guimerà et al. 2005). In an agent-based model of problem solving, Lazer and Friedman (2007) found an inverted U between the connectedness of a network and its performance over intermediate horizons, although a later experiment with human groups found that efficient networks did better (Mason and Watts 2012). That experiment also found that players in central positions "consistently earned more points than their peers in the same network" (769), so that the same network structure affected absolute and relative performance differently. Centola and Macy (2007) showed that long ties, which speed the spread of simple information, can block the spread of behaviors that require reinforcement from several contacts. The field therefore already holds that the effect of the environment can depend on its level and can differ for different individuals within it.

**How the field separates influence from sorting.** The central methodological debate in network science concerns whether similar outcomes among connected people reflect influence or sorting. Christakis and Fowler (2007) reported that a person's chance of becoming obese rose by 57 percent if a friend became obese. Cohen-Cole and Fletcher (2008), applying the same specification to a national sample of adolescents and adding controls for shared school environments and individual fixed effects, found that the estimated network effect became statistically indistinguishable from zero. Shalizi and Thomas (2011) then proved that latent homophily and contagion "are generically confounded with each other," so that contagion "cannot be nonparametrically identified from observational data" without strong assumptions. Large observational studies confirm the size of the problem: in a network of 27.4 million instant-messaging users, matching on observed traits showed that earlier methods overestimated peer influence in product adoption by 300 to 700 percent (Aral, Muchnik, and Sundararajan 2009), and in a Facebook experiment, observational estimates erred in both directions relative to randomized ones (Eckles, Kizilcec, and Bakshy 2016). The credible designs are experimental. Centola (2010, 2011) assigned participants to network positions and found that clustered networks spread a health behavior farther than random ones, and that placing participants among similar others tripled adoption. On LinkedIn, ordinary regression reproduced an apparent advantage of strong ties, and the randomized analysis reversed it (Rajkumar et al. 2022). Network science, like economics, thus distinguishes peer influence from sorting and from shared context, and it adds that the organization's assembly of the network is itself one of the sorting processes.

**What network science leaves open.** The studies reviewed here leave the question of this dissertation open for specific reasons. First, almost every outcome is one of development or diffusion, such as weight, adoption of a behavior, or posting, and not selection. Where an outcome approaches selection, the competitors are not observed. Gee, Jones, and Burke (2017, 486) state the concern directly, that a job may be allocated "toward another who has the right type of connection (weak or strong) but who is not necessarily a better worker," but their Facebook data identify only who later works with a friend, not who else applied. Clauset, Arbesman, and Larremore (2015) observe only candidates who were hired, and Burt (2004, 354) cautions that his single-company, cross-sectional association "cannot be causal." Second, the models that produce non-monotonic effects either are simulations not fitted to data or are regressions without a generative model. Lazer and Friedman (2007, 668) assume a setting in which "the success of any one or subset of agents has no direct effect on other agents," which excludes competition for positions by construction; Uzzi and Spiro (2005, 498) leave open how the networks they study arise. Third, each study covers one system: one town, one platform, one industry, or one company. Fourth, observational estimates of peer effects are confounded by sorting unless the environment is assigned. The opening this leaves is a model in which the composition of an individual's group, and the number of positions that group must share, enter that individual's chance of selection, estimated where membership is assigned or rule-based and tested in more than one system. Network science supplies the representation of persons within groups, the evidence that position changes evaluation and access, and the cautions on identification such a model must respect.

**Paragraph 1 — the problem**

No one performs in a vacuum. A basketball player's points are scored alongside teammates and weighed against those of players across the country. An Army officer is rated by a superior who rates other officers as well. A student's examination score competes with those of the other applicants for the same places, and a candidate for tenure is judged against colleagues and against scholars elsewhere. In each case, a person's success depends not only on that person's performance but on the environment in which the performance is produced, judged, and compared. A growing literature has asked whether a team can have too much talent: whether concentrating high performers helps or hurts the group (Swaab et al. 2014). This dissertation reverses that question, asking how the concentration of talent in a group strengthens or undermines the success of an individual within it. A stronger environment can help: strong peers and a strong organization can lend their members prestige, visibility, and credit. It can also hurt: beside stronger peers the same performance can look weaker, and more of those peers are credible candidates for the same scarce positions. Because forces of both kinds can operate at once, the relationship between the strength of a person's environment and that person's chance of success need not take a single form, and the familiar question of whether it is better to be a big fish in a small pond (Davis 1966) has no general answer. The question taken up here is when a stronger environment begins to cost a given performance. The approach is to hold a person's performance fixed, vary the environment, and ask how that person's chance of success changes across environments when the performance does not.

**Paragraph 2 — why each literature, what it says, what it misses**

Several fields have studied parts of this problem, and each is reviewed below for what it contributes and what it leaves out. The science of success establishes that success is a community's response to performance and that recognition amplifies small differences, so the people who confer success and the people who compete for it belong in the explanation; yet it treats strong peers and prestigious organizations mostly as resources and has rarely asked how the strength of the other candidates changes a person's chance of selection. Economics shows that when rewards depend on rank, a person's prospects depend on that person's rivals, and that coworkers can raise output while competitors can lower effort; but its findings differ from one setting to another with no way to predict which, and its outcomes are mostly output and pay rather than who is chosen. Network science represents groups as part of the structure that links people, and makes the dependence of one person's outcome on others its central object; but it seldom observes selection with the other candidates in view, and its results in which more connection eventually hurts come from simulations not fitted to data or regressions without a model of the outcome. Education has asked the big-fish question longest and most directly, and has built the best designs for separating the effect of an environment from the sorting of people into it; but the effect of an elite school ranges from almost none in one system to large in another, the benefit of stronger peers and the cost of lower rank among them are mostly estimated in separate studies, and admission cutoffs are used to measure what attending a school does, not how the strength of the other applicants changed who was admitted. Psychology supplies the mechanisms, since people judge themselves and are judged by others in comparison with those around them; but its outcomes are mostly beliefs rather than selection. The too-much-talent literature in psychology and management asks whether more talent eventually hurts a team. Tests that can tell a downturn from a plateau find that the returns to talent diminish in some settings and hold constant in others (Simonsohn 2018; Gula et al. 2021). Yet every study measures the outcome of the team, although the mechanism proposed for the downturn is a contest among individuals for standing within it (Swaab et al. 2014), so which of those individuals advance remains unmeasured. Organizational studies treats promotion as the allocation of a fixed number of positions governed by vacancies and rules, and shows that much of what looks like individual performance belongs to the organization; but its models set the selection step aside, its competitors are counted rather than measured, and each study examines one organization. The U.S. Army, finally, writes assignment, evaluation, and selection into regulation, with ratings made locally and promotions decided centrally; but what is known about it comes from separate studies with different samples and outcomes.

**Paragraph 3 — the vacuum**

Taken together, these literatures identify forces on both sides and the settings in which they operate. What they rarely supply is the combination this question requires: selection observed with the strength of the competitors measured, an account of how the opposing forces combine that is fitted to data, and a comparison of systems in the same terms that could explain why the answers differ from one system to another.

**Paragraph 4 — why the curve, at fixed performance**

The object that captures this question is the probability of success as a function of the strength of a person's environment, for people of the same performance. Holding performance fixed removes the person's own contribution, so any change in the chance of success across environments reflects how the environment enters the judgment of that performance and the competition for positions. The shape of the curve is the answer to the question: where it rises, a stronger environment helps a given performance; where it falls, it costs; and where it turns, if it turns, marks the point at which a stronger environment begins to impose a disadvantage. Because the opposing forces need not balance in any particular way, the curve has to be measured rather than assumed. A single coefficient on the strength of peers, as in the linear-in-means framework (Sacerdote 2014), presumes that the environment works in one direction at every level of strength, and a quadratic term forces a single turn whether or not one exists (Simonsohn 2018). A curve at fixed performance presumes neither.

**Paragraph 5 — a family of curves, and why not one aggregated curve**

Performance can be fixed at many levels, so the object is a family of curves, one for each band of own performance. The family shows where the environment matters: a person far above the level at which positions are won, or far below it, may be selected or passed over regardless of the environment, while a person near that level may not. A single curve that pools people of all performance levels is a coarse-grained version of this family, and a misleading one when stronger people tend to be in stronger groups, because its shape then reflects who is in a strong group as well as what the group does to them. Holding performance fixed separates the two. In the first instance, the strength of the environment is measured here by the average performance of the other members of a person's group; measures that count only credible competitors are taken up later.

## Heading 1: Synthesis

**Paragraph 1 (v1, 11-03)**

The literatures reviewed here agree that success is not simply a reflection of individual performance. Success is a community's recognition of performance, and recognition can amplify small differences: eminent scientists receive disproportionate credit for contributions comparable to those of lesser-known colleagues (Merton 1968), and talent itself names the conversion of differences in performance into a hierarchy (Menger 2024). The environment in which a person works enters that conversion at two points. First, it changes how a given performance is evaluated. Evaluators credit basketball players for what their teammates produced (Arcidiacono, Kinsler, and Price 2017; Kuehn 2023), and an Army senior rater may give the top rating to fewer than half of the officers of a rank, so the rating a performance receives depends on who else that superior rates (Department of the Army 2019b, 43). Second, it changes how scarce positions are allocated. In a tournament, rewards depend on rank rather than on output (Lazear and Rosen 1981), and in an organization the number of vacancies relative to the size of a cohort sets the odds of promotion at a given level of merit (Stewman and Konda 1983). The environment also changes what people become, since stronger coworkers can raise output and stronger competitors can lower effort (Mas and Moretti 2009; Brown 2011). The question taken up here, however, is how the same performance can lead to different outcomes depending on the environment in which it was produced, evaluated, and compared.

**Paragraph 2 (v1, 11-03)**

The environment, moreover, is not a single set of people. A person's peers are the people with whom that person works; the comparison group is the set of people against whom that person's performance is judged; and the competitors are the candidates for the same scarce positions. In some settings these coincide, as on a team whose members compete for the same playing time. In others they diverge. An Army officer is rated against the other officers in the same senior rater's population but selected by a board that compares officers from across the Army (Department of the Army 2019b, 42; Department of the Army 2020, 2). A college basketball player's teammates shape the statistics on which draft position partly depends (Kuehn 2023), while the competition for draft positions is national. A candidate for tenure is judged within a department, but partly on letters that compare the candidate with scholars elsewhere. The distinction matters because the same environment can help and hurt a person at once. Hedge fund managers who worked with a star received less credit from prospective employers when the fund succeeded, unless they had other evidence of their ability, and less blame when it failed (Kehoe and Bentley 2021, 597–98). A worker who joins a more demanding organization faces lower odds of promotion at the same ability, but greater rewards and a stronger signal if promoted (DeVaro et al. 2024, 77, 86). Someone may benefit from belonging to a prestigious organization while competing against candidates outside it, or may compete directly against colleagues for a limited number of openings. The literatures also measure the strength of an environment in different ways: the average performance of peers (Sacerdote 2014), a person's rank among them (Murphy and Weinhardt 2020), the presence of exceptional performers (Azoulay, Graff Zivin, and Wang 2010), the number of rivals close in rank (Bothner, Kang, and Stuart 2007), and the prestige of the organization (Clauset, Arbesman, and Larremore 2015). These measures need not move together. Improvement by someone far below the level needed to contend for a position raises the average of the group while leaving the competition for that position nearly unchanged.

**Paragraph 3 (v1, 11-03 12:50) — opposing forces; why the shape is the question; in Word 11-03 ~12:58 (between paragraphs 2 and 4)**

Stronger peers can therefore help and hurt the same person, and the literatures reviewed here identify forces on both sides. A strong group or organization can lend its members prestige, visibility, and credit, so that the same record is read more favorably because of where it was produced. A strong group can also make the same record look weaker, because that record is compared with those of the people around it, and it can leave fewer positions for each member, because more of the people around that member are credible candidates for them. Rules that limit how many members of a rating pool may receive a top rating (Department of the Army 2019b, 43), or that dismiss the lowest-rated share of each group (Scullen, Bergey, and Aiman-Smith 2005), make the second kind of force explicit. When forces of both kinds operate, the relationship between the strength of a person's environment and that person's chance of success need not take any one form. It could rise throughout, rise and then level off, or rise and then fall, and the form could differ from one system to another. Opposing forces make a downturn possible, not necessary. Psychology and management have nonetheless often assumed that such relationships eventually turn negative (Grant and Schwartz 2011, 62; Pierce and Aguinis 2013, 313), and the quadratic tests most often used to locate the turn can produce one where the data show only a plateau (Simonsohn 2018, 539). The interesting question is therefore not whether stronger peers help or hurt, but whether the shape of the relationship can be explained, and in particular whether one can predict the point at which a stronger environment begins to impose a disadvantage.

**Paragraph 5 (v1, 11-03 12:50) — contribution; in Word 11-03 ~12:58 (after paragraph 4; shared-penalty sentence highlighted yellow)**

This dissertation develops a model of how the environment shapes an individual's chance of success through evaluation and selection, and examines its predictions in three selection systems: college basketball players entering the professional draft, Army officers considered for promotion, and faculty considered for tenure. The model separates three steps that the literatures reviewed here usually study apart. People are first assigned to groups, partly by their own choices, as in the model of DeVaro and colleagues (2024), and partly by the organization. Each person's performance is then scored, and the score can be adjusted for the competition within that person's group. Scarce positions are finally allocated from those scores, sometimes within the group and sometimes across a wider field. Because each step is explicit, the model states when a stronger environment should raise a person's chance of selection and when it should lower it, depending on how strongly scores are adjusted for competition, how scarce the positions are, and whether selection is made within groups or across them. One consequence is that a penalty shared by everyone in a group leaves selection within that group unchanged but can change selection across groups. The question with which this chapter began, whether it is better to be a big fish in a small pond, then becomes one that can be asked of each system in the same terms: for a given performance, and a given way of being scored and selected, how strong can the pond become before it begins to cost the fish?

**Paragraph 4 (v1, 11-03 12:20) — consolidated gap from the seven "What … leaves open" closings; in Word 11-03 12:20 (tracked insertion, after paragraph 2; paragraph 3 not yet written)**

These trade-offs have been studied before. The big-fish-little-pond literature weighs the benefit of stronger classmates against the cost of lower rank among them (Denning, Murphy, and Weinhardt 2023); tournament theory shows that when rewards depend on rank, a person's prospects depend on that person's rivals (Lazear and Rosen 1981); organizational demography sets the odds of promotion by the size of a cohort relative to the vacancies ahead of it (Stewman and Konda 1983); and DeVaro and colleagues (2024) model careers in which a more demanding organization lowers the chance of promotion while raising its rewards. What these literatures leave open is narrower, and it recurs across them. First, selection is rarely the outcome that is explained. Admission and grant cutoffs are used to measure what winning does to a person, not how the strength of the other candidates changed who won, and most outcomes are output, pay, grades, or opinions. Second, where selection is observed, the competitors are missing or are counted rather than measured, and the number of positions is fixed by the setting rather than varied. Third, the accounts of how advantage and disadvantage combine are mostly verbal, or they are models whose parameters are assumed rather than fitted to data; DeVaro and colleagues generate no new data and note that pond size "has not been operationalized empirically" (2024, 95). Fourth, the evidence comes one system at a time, and where the same question is asked in different systems the answers differ, from almost no effect of an elite school in Boston (Abdulkadiroğlu, Angrist, and Pathak 2014) to a large effect in Trinidad and Tobago (Jackson 2010), with nothing in the literature that predicts which result a given system will produce. The most cited claims about the shape of these relationships, meanwhile, rest on tests that cannot tell a downturn from a plateau (Gula et al. 2021, 302). What remains open is whether one account of how the environment enters evaluation and selection can explain, across systems, when the same performance gains from stronger peers and when it loses.

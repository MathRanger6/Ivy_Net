## The U.S. Army

The U.S. Army officer corps is a setting in which assignment, evaluation, and selection are written into regulation. It is an internal labor market in the sense of Doeringer and Piore (1971), who named the military services as an example: an organization in which the pricing and allocation of labor are governed by administrative rules rather than by an outside market. Officers enter at the bottom and are not hired into higher ranks from outside; as Wardynski, Lyle, and Colarusso (2010, 3) put it, "in its core warfighting competencies, the Army cannot 'buy' talent from outside." Officers who enter in the same year form a cohort, or year group, and are "considered for promotion by entry-year group and are either promoted or not promoted at specific years of service" (Asch and Warner 2001, 526). The share of each cohort to be promoted is set by policy, not by the number of capable officers. The Defense Officer Personnel Management Act of 1980 set goals of promotion to major for 80 percent of a cohort at about ten years of service, to lieutenant colonel for 70 percent at about sixteen years, and to colonel for 50 percent at about twenty-two years. These were goals, not statutory requirements (Rostker et al. 1993, 13–14). Each of the three channels has a documented form in this system. The organization decides, increasingly with officers' input, where each officer serves and under whom. Evaluation is made locally, by an officer's own superiors, under rules that limit how many officers each superior may rate highly. Selection for promotion is made centrally, by boards that compare officers from across the Army.

**Officers state preferences for their assignments, but the Army decides how much weight those preferences carry.** For the period covered by most existing studies, preferences were weighed against the needs of the Army, and Lyle and Smith (2014, 233–34), describing assignments between 1998 and 2008, write that "other than listing installation preferences and special family considerations, junior officers have little influence over their next assignment." Human Resources Command sends an officer to an installation, and the installation, division, brigade, and battalion each assign the officer one level further down. Beginning with the assignment cycle of winter 2019–20, the Army replaced much of this process with the Army Talent Alignment Process, which it describes as "a decentralized, regulated, market-style hiring system" in which "officers are matched to available assignments based on their preferences as well as those of their organization" (U.S. Army 2019). Assignment therefore fixes both the peers with whom an officer serves and the superiors who will rate that officer. Under the older system, that environment was largely given to the officer. Lyle and Smith argue that the assignment of captains to battalion commanders in their data was "as good as random with regard to observable characteristics" (2014, 239). Under the newer system, units choose officers as officers choose units, so the composition of a unit reflects choices made on both sides.

**An officer's rating depends on the other officers the same superior rates.** Each evaluation report has two evaluators: the rater, who is the officer's immediate supervisor, and the senior rater, who is the rater's supervisor. The senior rater assesses each officer's potential "compared to all officers of the same rank," based on "officers the senior rater has previously senior rated and those in their current senior rater population" (Department of the Army 2019b, 42). The senior rater "must have less than 50 percent of the ratings of a rank in the 'Most Qualified' top box," and a report that would breach that limit is processed with the lower label "Highly Qualified" (43). The rater faces a parallel limit on the top rating of "Excels" (39). Under such limits, the rating a given performance receives depends on who else is being rated. Evans and Robinson (2020, 91) give an example: "if a rater has a pool size of five, but has more than two top one-third performers, at least one rated officer will receive an inaccurate evaluation due to the rater's profile constraint." Evans and Bae (2019, 197) simulated the appraisal system for majors facing promotion boards in 2015 and 2016. In their model, an officer's evaluations depend on "the performance percentiles of other officers in the same rating pool" and "the size of the rating pool," and larger pools and a looser limit reduced the number of strong officers the system misidentified (200–203). Both studies are calculations and simulations, not estimates from officers' records. The organization in which an officer serves may also shape how that officer's performance is read. Lyle and Smith (2014, 252) control for the unit in which a captain commanded because "unit reputation effects may attach to captains who complete company command in prestigious units—such as airborne divisions or a ranger regiment."

**Selection is made centrally, from ratings produced locally.** "The Army will provide a centralized selection process for promotion to all grades except GEN, LTG, 1LT, and CW2" (Department of the Army 2020, 2). Before each board meets, the Secretary of the Army sets "the maximum number of officers to be selected from each competitive category" (5). When there are fewer slots than eligible officers, the board selects the "best qualified" (9), and "officers in the same competitive category will compete among themselves for promotion" (Department of the Army 2019a, 29). A board may also select officers a year early, from "below the zone." Such selections are intended for "officers of exceptional ability," and they may not exceed 10 percent of the number the board is authorized to select (Department of the Army 2020, 9). The board's principal evidence is the evaluation report: "the mechanism to judge the value of an individual's performance and potential is the OER" (Department of the Army 2019a, 10). The regulations provide one adjustment for the setting in which a report was written. Boards are told the size of each senior rater's population so they can "identify senior raters with small rating populations and weigh the report accordingly" (Department of the Army 2019b, 43). Early selection compounds. Among the officers Lyle and Smith (2014, 233n4) studied, early promotion to major "increased the likelihood of subsequent early promotion to lieutenant colonel by a factor of nearly 8 and subsequent selection to battalion command by a factor of 3.5." Among West Point graduates of 1992–2004, cadets' military grade point average strongly predicted early promotion and battalion command. Spain, Lin, and Young (2020, 403) caution that this average is built from cadet performance reports "similar in style, authorship, and frequency" to officer evaluation reports, so it "may be more of an early career outcome than a measure of cadet military performance."

**The superiors to whom an officer is assigned are associated with that officer's career, but they also write that officer's evaluations.** Lyle and Smith (2014) studied 4,142 captains who commanded companies between 1998 and 2008, and defined a high-performing mentor as a battalion commander who had himself been promoted early to major. Captains who served under such a commander were "29% more likely to be selected for early promotion to the rank of major" (232). The effect grew with the months spent under that commander and was significant only for captains in the top half of the SAT distribution (244–46); the authors call their estimate "probably an upper-bound estimate on the impact of mentoring on promotion" (250). The same commander, however, provides "a majority of the input for company commander evaluation reports" (235). The authors suggest that high-performing mentors "may better be able to recognize high potential in their protégés, as well as be more skilled at touting their protégés in written evaluations and recommendations" (248), and they state that they "are unable to identify the exact pathways through which mentor effects work" (232). The effect may therefore run through development, through evaluation, or through both. Spain, Mukunda, and Bates (2021) examined 36,032 lieutenants who served under 1,745 battalion commanders and found that the identity of the commander explained more of whether a lieutenant stayed in the Army past the sixth year than any of their control variables (106–7). Most commanders retained between 46 and 72 percent of their lieutenants (108). The study does not argue that lieutenants were assigned to commanders at random, and the authors note that a commander might receive "a cohort of lieutenants who are of unusually high or low quality or predisposed to want to stay in or leave the Army" (113). Formal mentoring programs, by contrast, have rarely been tested: Johnson and Andersen (2010, 117) found "not a single published evaluation of the efficacy of formal military mentoring." Evidence on peers of the same rank comes mainly from the service academies, reviewed in the education subsection; among serving officers, the documented effects concern superiors.

**The cohort shrinks through departures, and departures are not random.** Because officers are not hired into higher ranks, each year group can only shrink. Spain, Lin, and Farina (2023, 3) note that in a hierarchy like the Army "a certain rate of turnover is required," roughly matching the narrowing number of positions at each level, and that "almost all officer losses are voluntary" (2). A common view holds that the strongest officers are the most likely to leave. Spain, Lin, and Farina tested it on 13,309 West Point graduates of the classes of 1992 through 2007. Graduates in the top third of their class on both military and academic grade point averages were as likely as average graduates to stay past the sixth year and more likely to stay past the tenth, and the lowest military performers left at the highest rates (15–16). The authors conclude that "empirical evidence from this study does not support such claims" (22). Departures nonetheless change who remains to be selected. In a simulation of the year group of 1986, in which boards were assumed to judge talent without error, Dabkowski et al. (2010, 1412) found that once attrition takes effect, the boards are "promoting 'the best of what's left,'" and that under historical attrition rates the colonel's board had "little impact" on the talent of those it promoted (1413). Who stays may also depend on the environment, since the commander effect reported by Spain, Mukunda, and Bates concerns exactly this decision. Studies of later outcomes inherit the problem. Lyle and Smith's sample includes only officers who remained to ten years of service (2014, 237), and Spain, Lin, and Young (2020, 405) acknowledge that "retention in the Army to the point of being considered for promotion is not a random process."

**What the Army record leaves open.** Research on the Army documents each part of the process this review has traced. Officers are assigned to superiors whose identity is associated with whether they stay and whether they are promoted early, although the same superiors write their evaluations. Rating rules make the rating a performance receives depend on the other officers in the same rating pool, a dependence shown so far by calculation and simulation. Promotion boards select from across the Army, from ratings produced in many separate pools, and the regulations reviewed here adjust for the size of a senior rater's population but not for who else was in it. Departures from the officer corps are voluntary, selective, and associated with the commanders under whom officers served. These findings come from separate studies with different samples and outcomes: early promotion to major for captains who stayed ten years, retention past six years for lieutenants, and simulated cohorts. The Army is also an unusual system, closed to lateral entry and uniform in its career timing, so its findings carry to more open fields only with care. What remains open is whether officers of comparable measured performance have different chances of selection by a board that sees the whole Army, depending on the strength of the officers with whom they served and were rated, and how much of any such difference reflects who stayed long enough to be considered.
